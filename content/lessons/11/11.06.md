Put your thumb over the end of a garden hose and the water shoots out faster. No water is created or destroyed, so what goes in must come out: squeeze the area and the speed goes up. That's **continuity**, the first of the conservation laws, written once for a control volume and once for every point of the flow.

## The Reynolds transport theorem

Physical laws are written for a fixed lump of matter (a **system**). Engineers prefer a fixed region of space, a **control volume** (CV), that fluid flows through. The Reynolds transport theorem links them: for any extensive property $B$ with intensity $\beta = B/m$,

$$
\frac{dB_\text{sys}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\int_\text{CV}\rho\beta\,dV + \oint_\text{CS}\rho\beta\,(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n)\,dA .
$$

Rate of change of the system = accumulation inside + net outflow through the surface. With $B$ = mass, $\beta = 1$ and the left side is zero.

## Integral and differential forms

For steady flow, mass flow in equals mass flow out: $\sum\dot m_\text{in} = \sum\dot m_\text{out}$. For an incompressible fluid in a pipe,

$$
Q = A_1V_1 = A_2V_2 ,
$$

with $V$ the mean speed. Halve a pipe's diameter and the speed quadruples.

Shrinking the control volume to a point with Gauss's theorem gives

$$
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = 0
$$

for incompressible flow. Any velocity field you invent must satisfy this to be physically possible, and CFD codes spend much of their effort enforcing it.

:::widget contraction
A water main contracting and expanding. The flow rate is the same at every section; the speed isn't.
:::

:::example Checking fields and pipes
1. On an 81 × 81 grid, the stagnation flow $(x, -y)$ and the vortex $(-y, x)$ have zero divergence (to $10^{-15}$) and zero net outflow through the boundary square.
2. The expanding field $(x, y)$ has divergence 2 everywhere and net outflow 8.0000 through the 2 × 2 square, equal to divergence × area, as Gauss requires. It can't be an incompressible flow.
3. A water main carrying 70 L/s: 0.990 m/s in the 300 mm section, 3.961 m/s in the 150 mm throat, 2.228 m/s in the 200 mm section, 70.0 L/s through each.
:::

```python file=continuity.py
"""Conservation of mass: divergence of discrete velocity fields, and equal flow rate through every section of a contraction."""
import numpy as np


def divergence(u, v, dx, dy):
    """Central-difference divergence on the interior of a grid (arrays indexed [j, i] = [y, x])."""
    return (u[1:-1, 2:] - u[1:-1, :-2]) / (2 * dx) + (v[2:, 1:-1] - v[:-2, 1:-1]) / (2 * dy)


def net_outflow(u, v, x, y):
    """Line integral of u . n around the grid boundary (trapezoidal rule)."""
    tr = lambda f, s: float(np.sum((f[1:] + f[:-1]) / 2 * np.diff(s)))
    return tr(u[:, -1], y) - tr(u[:, 0], y) + tr(v[-1, :], x) - tr(v[0, :], x)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    x = np.linspace(-1, 1, 81); y = np.linspace(-1, 1, 81); X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y); dx = x[1] - x[0]
    fields = {"stagnation u = x, v = -y": (X, -Y),
              "vortex u = -y, v = x": (-Y, X),
              "expanding u = x, v = y": (X, Y)}
    for name, (u, v) in fields.items():
        d = divergence(u, v, dx, dx)
        print(f"{name}: max |div u| = {np.abs(d).max():.2e}, net outflow through the square {net_outflow(u, v, x, y):.4f}")
    assert np.abs(divergence(X, -Y, dx, dx)).max() < 1e-12 and abs(net_outflow(X, -Y, x, y)) < 1e-12
    assert abs(net_outflow(X, Y, x, y) - 2 * 4) < 1e-9            # divergence 2 times area 4 (Gauss)
    # a water main contracting from 300 mm to 150 mm and back to 200 mm, 70 L/s
    Q = 0.070
    for D in (0.300, 0.150, 0.200):
        A = np.pi * D * D / 4; V = Q / A
        print(f"  D = {D*1000:.0f} mm: area {A*1e4:.2f} cm^2, mean speed {V:.3f} m/s, flow rate {A*V*1000:.1f} L/s")
        assert abs(A * V - Q) < 1e-15
    print(f"halving the diameter multiplies the speed by {(0.3/0.15)**2:.0f}")
```

:::exercise The thumb on the hose
A 16 mm garden hose delivers 0.3 L/s. A nozzle narrows the outlet to 5 mm. Find the speed in the hose and at the nozzle.
---solution---
1. Hose area $\pi\cdot0.016^2/4 = 2.011\times10^{-4}$ m², so $V_1 = 0.0003/2.011\times10^{-4} = 1.492$ m/s.
2. Nozzle: $V_2 = V_1(16/5)^2 = 1.492\cdot10.24 = 15.28$ m/s, fast enough to reach several metres. In reality the pump or mains pressure may not keep the flow rate constant when you restrict the outlet; that needs the energy equation (next lesson).
:::
