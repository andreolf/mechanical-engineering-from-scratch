Dive to the bottom of a pool and your ears hurt; drive up to the Jungfraujoch and a sealed crisp packet puffs up. Both come from one equation. In a fluid at rest there's no shear stress, only pressure, and the pressure at a point is the same in every direction. It changes with height because each layer has to hold up the weight of everything above it.

## The hydrostatic equation

Balance the forces on a small fluid element of height $dz$: the pressure difference across it carries its weight, so

$$
\frac{dp}{dz} = -\rho g .
$$

For a liquid, $\rho$ is constant and $p = p_0 + \rho g h$ at depth $h$ below a free surface at $p_0$. Ten metres of water adds $1000\cdot9.81\cdot10 = 98.1$ kPa, about one atmosphere. Engineers often quote **gauge pressure**, measured relative to the local atmosphere, rather than **absolute pressure**.

For a gas, density depends on pressure: $\rho = p/(RT)$. With the standard atmosphere's temperature falling 6.5 K per km, integration gives $p = p_0(T/T_0)^{g/(R\lambda)}$. Pressure falls by about 12 % per kilometre near the ground.

## Manometers

A U-tube holding a dense liquid measures a pressure difference by the height difference $h$ it supports. Walking down one leg and up the other, adding $\rho g\,\Delta z$ going down and subtracting going up,

$$
p_1 - p_2 = (\rho_m - \rho_f)\,g\,h ,
$$

where $\rho_m$ is the manometer fluid and $\rho_f$ the fluid in the pipe above it. Mercury gives compact readings for large differences; water or oil gives sensitivity for small ones.

:::widget manometer
A U-tube manometer. Change the reading and the fluids.
:::

:::example Water, air and mercury
1. Gauge pressure at 10 m depth: 98.10 kPa, from integrating $dp/dz = -\rho g$ numerically.
2. Standard atmosphere, integrated with the 6.5 K/km lapse rate: 965.2 hPa in Zurich (408 m) and 661.4 hPa on the Jungfraujoch (3454 m). The numerical integration matches the closed form to within 1 Pa.
3. At 661.4 hPa water boils at 88.5 °C (Antoine equation), so pasta cooks slowly up there.
4. A mercury manometer under water reading 120 mm: $(13\,546 - 998)\cdot9.81\cdot0.120 = 14.772$ kPa.
:::

```python file=hydrostatics.py
"""Hydrostatics: dp/dz = -rho g integrated for water and for the atmosphere, plus a U-tube manometer."""
import numpy as np

G = 9.81


def integrate(rho_of, p0, z):
    """March dp/dz = -rho(p, z) g upward with a midpoint (RK2) step from p0 at z[0]."""
    p = [p0]
    for i in range(len(z) - 1):
        dz = z[i + 1] - z[i]
        pm = p[-1] - rho_of(p[-1], z[i]) * G * dz / 2
        p.append(p[-1] - rho_of(pm, z[i] + dz / 2) * G * dz)
    return np.array(p)


def manometer(h, rho_m, rho_f):
    """Pressure difference across a U-tube with manometer fluid rho_m reading h, under fluid rho_f."""
    return (rho_m - rho_f) * G * h


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # water: gauge pressure at 10 m depth (z measured upward from the bottom)
    z = np.linspace(0, 10, 101)
    p = integrate(lambda p, z: 1000.0, 1000 * G * 10, z)
    print(f"gauge pressure at 10 m depth: {p[0]/1000:.2f} kPa; at the surface {p[-1]:.2e} Pa")
    assert abs(p[0] - 98.1e3) < 1 and abs(p[-1]) < 1e-6
    # atmosphere with the standard 6.5 K/km lapse rate, from Zurich (408 m) to Jungfraujoch (3454 m)
    R = 287.05
    T = lambda z: 288.15 - 0.0065 * z
    z = np.linspace(0, 3454, 3455)
    p = integrate(lambda p, zz: p / (R * T(zz)), 101325.0, z)
    isa = 101325 * (T(z) / 288.15) ** (G / (R * 0.0065))
    assert np.max(np.abs(p - isa)) < 1.0
    print(f"standard atmosphere: Zurich 408 m {p[408]/100:.1f} hPa, Jungfraujoch 3454 m {p[-1]/100:.1f} hPa")
    t_boil = 1730.63 / (8.07131 - np.log10(p[-1] / 133.322)) - 233.426          # Antoine equation for water
    print(f"water boils at {t_boil:.1f} C on the Jungfraujoch")
    # manometer: mercury reading 120 mm across an orifice in a water pipe
    dp = manometer(0.120, 13546, 998)
    print(f"mercury manometer, 120 mm reading under water: {dp/1000:.3f} kPa")
```

:::exercise The bottom of Lake Zurich
Lake Zurich is 136 m deep at its deepest. Find the gauge and absolute pressure there, taking 96.52 kPa for atmospheric pressure at the lake surface. Then find the pressure difference read by a water manometer showing 50 mm across a fan duct carrying air ($\rho = 1.2$ kg/m³).
---solution---
1. Gauge: $1000\cdot9.81\cdot136 = 1.334$ MPa, about 13 atmospheres.
2. Absolute: $1.334 + 0.0965 = 1.431$ MPa.
3. Manometer: $(998 - 1.2)\cdot9.81\cdot0.050 = 488.9$ Pa. Air's density barely matters here.
:::
