Away from walls and wakes, many flows are almost free of vorticity. For an irrotational, incompressible flow the velocity is the gradient of a potential, and the whole flow field can be built by adding simple pieces together. The method is old, but it still gives the most important result in aerodynamics: lift is proportional to circulation.

## The velocity potential and stream function

If $\nabla\times\mathbf u = 0$, then $\mathbf u = \nabla\phi$. Continuity, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0$, turns this into Laplace's equation,

$$
\nabla^2\phi = 0 .
$$

Laplace's equation is linear, so solutions add. In 2D there is also a stream function $\psi$ with $u = \partial\psi/\partial y$, $v = -\partial\psi/\partial x$; lines of constant $\psi$ are streamlines. Both combine into one **complex potential** $w(z) = \phi + i\psi$ of $z = x + iy$, and the velocity comes from a single derivative:

$$
\frac{dw}{dz} = u - i v .
$$

## Building blocks

| Flow | $w(z)$ |
|---|---|
| Uniform stream | $Uz$ |
| Source of strength $m$ | $\dfrac{m}{2\pi}\ln z$ |
| Doublet | $\dfrac{\mu}{z}$ |
| Vortex, circulation $\Gamma$ (anticlockwise) | $-\dfrac{i\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln z$ |

A uniform stream plus a doublet of strength $Ua^2$ gives the flow past a cylinder of radius $a$; adding a vortex gives a cylinder with circulation:

$$
w = U\left(z + \frac{a^2}{z}\right) - \frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln z .
$$

## Pressure, d'Alembert and Kutta-Joukowski

On the surface the speed is $u_\theta = -2U\sin\theta + \Gamma/(2\pi a)$. Bernoulli gives the pressure coefficient $C_p = 1 - (u_\theta/U)^2$: $+1$ at the stagnation points and $-3$ at the top and bottom without circulation. That distribution is symmetric front to back, so the drag is zero: **d'Alembert's paradox**. Real flows have drag because the boundary layer separates, as Phase 12 shows.

Circulation breaks the top-bottom symmetry. Integrating the pressure gives the **Kutta-Joukowski theorem**, valid for any 2D body:

$$
L' = \rho U \Gamma \quad\text{per unit span.}
$$

A clockwise circulation (negative in the anticlockwise convention) lifts the body upwards. A wing creates its circulation through its sharp trailing edge; a spinning ball or a rotor ship drags it round with its surface (the Magnus effect).

:::example Checking Kutta-Joukowski by integration
Air at ρ = 1.2 kg/m³, U = 10 m/s, a cylinder of radius a = 0.5 m.

1. Without circulation: $C_p = 1.000$ at the stagnation points and $-3.000$ at the top and bottom. Integrating around 20 000 surface points gives drag and lift both below $10^{-13}$ N/m.
2. With Γ = −20 m²/s (clockwise): lift 240.0000 N/m $= \rho U|\Gamma| = 1.2\cdot10\cdot20$. Drag is still zero.
3. The stagnation points sit where $\sin\theta = \Gamma/(4\pi U a)$: at −18.56° and 198.56°.
4. At $|\Gamma| = 4\pi U a = 62.832$ m²/s they merge at the bottom; lift is 753.9822 N/m.
:::

```python file=potential.py
"""Potential flow past a cylinder with circulation: complex potential, surface pressure, and the Kutta-Joukowski lift by integration."""
import numpy as np

RHO = 1.2


def velocity(z, U, a, Gamma):
    """dw/dz = u - i v for w = U (z + a^2/z) - i Gamma/(2 pi) log z (circulation positive anticlockwise)."""
    dw = U * (1 - a * a / z ** 2) - 1j * Gamma / (2 * np.pi * z)
    return dw.real, -dw.imag


def surface_forces(U, a, Gamma, n=20000):
    """Integrate the Bernoulli pressure p - p_inf = rho/2 (U^2 - |u|^2) around the cylinder."""
    th = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, n, endpoint=False); dth = 2 * np.pi / n
    z = a * np.exp(1j * th)
    u, v = velocity(z, U, a, Gamma)
    cp = 1 - (u * u + v * v) / U ** 2
    p = 0.5 * RHO * U ** 2 * cp
    # outward normal (cos, sin); force on body = -integral p n dl
    Fx = -np.sum(p * np.cos(th)) * a * dth
    Fy = -np.sum(p * np.sin(th)) * a * dth
    return Fx, Fy, th, cp


if __name__ == "__main__":
    U, a = 10.0, 0.5
    for Gamma in (0.0, -20.0, -50.0, -4 * np.pi * U * a):           # the last one merges the stagnation points at the bottom
        Fx, Fy, th, cp = surface_forces(U, a, Gamma)
        print(f"Gamma = {Gamma:8.3f} m^2/s: drag {Fx:+.2e} N/m, lift {Fy:9.4f} N/m (rho U |Gamma| = {RHO*U*abs(Gamma):.4f})")
        assert abs(Fx) < 1e-8 and abs(Fy - (-RHO * U * Gamma)) < 1e-6     # d'Alembert and Kutta-Joukowski (clockwise Gamma lifts up)
    Fx, Fy, th, cp = surface_forces(U, a, 0.0)
    print(f"no circulation: Cp = {cp[0]:.3f} at the stagnation points, {cp.min():.3f} at the top and bottom")
    # stagnation points on the surface: sin(theta) = Gamma / (4 pi U a)
    G = -20.0
    print(f"Gamma = {G}: stagnation points at {np.degrees(np.arcsin(G/(4*np.pi*U*a))):.2f} and {180-np.degrees(np.arcsin(G/(4*np.pi*U*a))):.2f} deg")
```

:::exercise A rotor ship
A Flettner rotor 3 m in diameter spins in a 12 m/s wind (ρ = 1.2 kg/m³). Model it as a cylinder with circulation $\Gamma = 2\pi a u_s$, where the surface speed is $u_s = 36$ m/s. Find Γ, the force per metre of rotor height, and the force on a 20 m tall rotor. Where are the stagnation points?
---solution---
1. $a = 1.5$ m, $\Gamma = 2\pi\cdot1.5\cdot36 = 339.29$ m²/s.
2. $L' = \rho U\Gamma = 1.2\cdot12\cdot339.29 = 4885.8$ N/m.
3. Over 20 m: 97.7 kN, ignoring end effects (real rotors give less, which is why they carry end plates).
4. $\Gamma/(4\pi U a) = 339.29/(4\pi\cdot12\cdot1.5) = 1.5 > 1$: the stagnation point has left the surface and a ring of air circulates with the rotor.
:::
