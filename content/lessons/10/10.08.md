The first law says energy is conserved. It doesn't say which way things go. A hot coffee cools in a cold room; it never spontaneously gets hotter. A car's engine turns at most about 40 % of its fuel energy into work, not because engineers are careless but because nature sets a ceiling. The **second law** of thermodynamics is that ceiling, and the Carnot efficiency puts a number on it.

## Two statements

- **Kelvin-Planck:** no cyclic device can take heat from a single reservoir and turn it all into work. Every heat engine must reject some heat to a colder sink.
- **Clausius:** heat can't flow from cold to hot on its own. A refrigerator needs work input.

The two are equivalent: a device violating one can be combined with an ordinary engine or fridge to violate the other.

## The Carnot efficiency

A reversible engine between a hot reservoir at $T_h$ and a cold one at $T_c$ (in kelvin) has efficiency

$$
\eta_\text{Carnot} = 1 - \frac{T_c}{T_h} .
$$

**No engine working between the same two reservoirs can do better**, and all reversible ones do exactly this well. In general, for any cycle the **Clausius inequality** holds:

$$
\oint \frac{\delta Q}{T} \le 0 ,
$$

with equality only for reversible cycles. On a T-s diagram a reversible cycle's heat input is the area under its top, and its net work the enclosed area. The Carnot cycle is a rectangle, the shape that uses the temperature limits best. Any other shape between the same limits takes in heat below $T_h$ or rejects it above $T_c$, and does worse.

The practical lesson is: raise $T_h$ (hotter combustion, superheated steam, better turbine blade alloys) and lower $T_c$ (cold river water, cooling towers). A 150 °C geothermal source can never exceed 29.5 % however clever the machine.

:::example No shape beats the rectangle
1. Between 300 K and 900 K the Carnot limit is $1 - 300/900 = 0.6667$.
2. A rectangle on the T-s diagram reproduces it to machine precision.
3. 20 000 random reversible cycles drawn inside the same band: the best manages 0.5330, and every one stays below the Carnot limit for its own temperature extremes.
4. Real limits: a coal plant with 565 °C steam and a 25 °C river, 64.4 % (real plants reach about 45 %); an engine with 2300 K peak gas and 300 K air, 87.0 %; geothermal at 150 °C, 29.5 %.
:::

```python file=carnot.py
"""The second law: Carnot efficiency, and a numerical check that no cycle working between two temperature limits beats it."""
import math
import numpy as np


def carnot(Tc, Th):
    return 1 - Tc / Th


def cycle_efficiency(T, s):
    """Efficiency of a reversible cycle drawn as a closed polygon in the T-s plane (traversed clockwise).
    Heat in = integral of T ds where ds > 0; net work = enclosed area."""
    T = np.append(T, T[0]); s = np.append(s, s[0])
    dq = (T[1:] + T[:-1]) / 2 * np.diff(s)
    q_in = dq[dq > 0].sum(); q_out = -dq[dq < 0].sum()
    return (q_in - q_out) / q_in


if __name__ == "__main__":
    Tc, Th = 300.0, 900.0
    print(f"Carnot between {Tc:.0f} and {Th:.0f} K: {carnot(Tc, Th):.4f}")
    # the Carnot cycle itself as a rectangle in T-s
    rect = cycle_efficiency(np.array([Th, Th, Tc, Tc]), np.array([0.0, 1.0, 1.0, 0.0]))
    assert abs(rect - carnot(Tc, Th)) < 1e-12
    # random reversible cycles confined between Tc and Th: none exceeds Carnot
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0); best = 0.0
    for _ in range(20000):
        n = rng.integers(3, 12)
        ang = np.sort(rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, n))[::-1]        # clockwise
        rad = rng.uniform(0.3, 1.0, n)
        s = 1 + rad * np.cos(ang)
        T = (Tc + Th) / 2 + (Th - Tc) / 2 * rad * np.sin(ang)
        eta = cycle_efficiency(T, s)
        best = max(best, eta)
        assert eta <= carnot(T.min(), T.max()) + 1e-12
    print(f"20 000 random cycles: best efficiency {best:.4f} (limit {carnot(Tc, Th):.4f})")
    # real examples
    for name, a, b in (("coal plant, 565 C steam, 25 C river", 298.15, 838.15), ("car engine, 2300 K peak, 300 K air", 300.0, 2300.0), ("geothermal, 150 C", 298.15, 423.15)):
        print(f"  {name}: Carnot limit {carnot(a, b)*100:.1f} %")
```

:::exercise Check an inventor's claim
An inventor says her engine takes 1000 kJ from a reservoir at 500 K, delivers 450 kJ of work and rejects 550 kJ to the surroundings at 300 K. Is it possible? Check both the efficiency and the Clausius inequality.
---solution---
1. The first law is satisfied: $1000 = 450 + 550$.
2. Claimed efficiency $450/1000 = 0.45$; Carnot limit $1 - 300/500 = 0.40$. Impossible.
3. Clausius: $\oint\delta Q/T = 1000/500 - 550/300 = 2.000 - 1.833 = +0.167$ kJ/K, which is greater than zero, so the cycle violates the second law.
4. To be possible it would need to reject at least $1000\cdot300/500 = 600$ kJ.
:::
