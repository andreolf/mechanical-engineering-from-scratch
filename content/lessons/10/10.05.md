Most energy machines have fluid flowing through them: air through a jet engine, steam through a turbine, water through a pump, refrigerant through a heat pump. We can't follow a fixed lump of mass through them easily, so we draw a fixed **control volume** around the machine and keep account of energy carried in and out by the flow. In steady operation the result is one of the most useful equations in engineering.

## Flow work and enthalpy

Pushing fluid into a control volume takes work: a slug of volume $V$ pushed in against pressure $p$ needs $pV$, or $pv$ per kilogram. This **flow work** always accompanies the internal energy $u$ carried in, so the natural combination is the enthalpy, $h = u + pv$.

## The steady-flow energy equation

For one inlet (1) and one outlet (2) with mass flow $\dot m$, steady operation:

$$
\dot Q - \dot W = \dot m\left[(h_2 - h_1) + \frac{V_2^2 - V_1^2}{2} + g(z_2 - z_1)\right] .
$$

$\dot W$ is shaft power out; flow work is already inside $h$.

## Typical devices

- **Nozzle**: no work, nearly adiabatic. The enthalpy drop becomes kinetic energy: $V_2 = \sqrt{V_1^2 + 2(h_1 - h_2)}$.
- **Turbine**: adiabatic, kinetic energy changes small. $\dot W = \dot m(h_1 - h_2)$.
- **Compressor or pump**: the same with work in. For a pump with liquid, $h_2 - h_1 \approx v\Delta p$.
- **Throttle valve**: no work, no heat, so $h_2 = h_1$. An ideal gas leaves at the same temperature; a real fluid can cool sharply (10.15).
- **Heat exchanger**: no work; heat lost by one stream equals heat gained by the other.

Orders of magnitude: 1 K of temperature in water is 4186 J/kg; 1 m of height is 9.81 J/kg; a speed of 45 m/s is 1000 J/kg. Kinetic and potential terms matter only in nozzles, diffusers and hydropower (10.07).

For an ideal gas flowing through a reversible adiabatic device, $T_2/T_1 = (p_2/p_1)^{(k-1)/k}$ (10.10 covers real devices).

:::example Nozzle, turbine and compressor
Air with $c_p = 1005$ J/(kg K), $k = 1.4$.

1. Nozzle: 500 K, 300 kPa, 30 m/s in, expanding isentropically to 100 kPa. $T_2 = 500\cdot(1/3)^{0.2857} = 365.36$ K, so $V_2 = \sqrt{30^2 + 2\cdot1005\cdot134.64} = 521.1$ m/s. The energy residual is zero.
2. Turbine: 2 kg/s from 1200 K and 1 MPa to 100 kPa. Exit 621.7 K, power 1162.3 kW.
3. Compressor: 2 kg/s from 300 K and 100 kPa to 1 MPa. Exit 579.0 K, power absorbed 560.8 kW.
4. Together they deliver 601.5 kW: the heart of a gas turbine (10.13).
5. Throttle: $h_2 = h_1$ gives zero power residual, and for an ideal gas no temperature change.
:::

```python file=sfee.py
"""Steady-flow energy equation for nozzles, turbines, compressors and throttles with air as an ideal gas."""
import math

CP, R = 1005.0, 287.0
K = CP / (CP - R)


def sfee_power(mdot, h1, h2, V1=0.0, V2=0.0, z1=0.0, z2=0.0, Q=0.0):
    """Shaft power out (W) from Q - W = mdot [(h2 - h1) + (V2^2 - V1^2)/2 + g (z2 - z1)]."""
    return Q - mdot * ((h2 - h1) + (V2**2 - V1**2) / 2 + 9.81 * (z2 - z1))


def isentropic_T(T1, p1, p2):
    return T1 * (p2 / p1) ** ((K - 1) / K)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # nozzle: air enters at 500 K, 300 kPa, 30 m/s; expands isentropically to 100 kPa
    T1, p1, V1 = 500.0, 300e3, 30.0
    T2 = isentropic_T(T1, p1, 100e3)
    V2 = math.sqrt(V1**2 + 2 * CP * (T1 - T2))
    print(f"nozzle: exit T = {T2:.2f} K, exit velocity {V2:.1f} m/s")
    assert abs(sfee_power(1.0, CP * T1, CP * T2, V1, V2)) < 1e-6       # no work, no heat: residual zero
    # turbine: 2 kg/s from 1200 K, 1 MPa to 100 kPa, isentropic
    T2t = isentropic_T(1200.0, 1e6, 100e3)
    Wt = sfee_power(2.0, CP * 1200, CP * T2t)
    print(f"turbine: exit {T2t:.1f} K, power {Wt/1e3:.1f} kW")
    # compressor: 2 kg/s from 300 K, 100 kPa to 1 MPa, isentropic (power is negative: absorbed)
    T2c = isentropic_T(300.0, 100e3, 1e6)
    Wc = sfee_power(2.0, CP * 300, CP * T2c)
    print(f"compressor: exit {T2c:.1f} K, power {Wc/1e3:.1f} kW")
    print(f"net from this ideal pair: {(Wt + Wc)/1e3:.1f} kW")
    # throttle valve: h2 = h1, so an ideal gas leaves at the same temperature
    assert sfee_power(1.0, CP * 400, CP * 400) == 0.0
    # 1 m of height vs 1 K of temperature for water flowing through a pump
    print(f"water: g x 1 m = {9.81:.2f} J/kg, c x 1 K = {4186:.0f} J/kg")
```

:::exercise A water pump
A pump lifts 20 kg/s of water from 100 kPa to 1.5 MPa ($v = 0.001$ m³/kg). Neglecting heat loss and velocity changes, what power does it need if it's ideal? What temperature rise would friction cause if the pump were only 70 % efficient?
---solution---
1. Ideal: $\dot W = \dot m v\Delta p = 20\cdot0.001\cdot1.4\times10^6 = 28.0$ kW.
2. Actual: $28.0/0.7 = 40.0$ kW. The extra 12.0 kW is dissipated into the water.
3. $\Delta T = 12000/(20\cdot4186) = 0.14$ K. Pump losses barely warm the water, so measuring that rise needs very precise thermometers.
:::
