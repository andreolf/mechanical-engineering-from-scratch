Squeeze the air in a bicycle pump and you push the handle through a distance against the pressure. That's work, the same force times distance as in mechanics. In thermodynamics we write it in terms of the gas's pressure and volume, and it turns out that the amount of work depends on **how** you squeeze, not just where you start and end.

## Boundary work

A gas at pressure $p$ pushes on a piston of area $A$ with force $pA$. Moving the piston by $dx$ changes the volume by $dV = A\,dx$ and does work $pA\,dx = p\,dV$. For a slow (quasi-equilibrium) process:

$$
W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} p\,dV .
$$

With the sign convention used here, $W > 0$ when the gas expands and does work on the surroundings, and $W < 0$ for compression. On a $p$-$V$ diagram, $W$ is the area under the path.

## Common processes

- **Isobaric** ($p$ constant): $W = p(V_2 - V_1)$.
- **Isothermal** ideal gas ($pV = $ const): $W = p_1V_1\ln(V_2/V_1)$.
- **Polytropic**, $pV^n = $ const with $n \ne 1$:

$$
W = \frac{p_2V_2 - p_1V_1}{1 - n} .
$$

$n = 0$ is isobaric, $n = 1$ isothermal, $n = k = c_p/c_v$ (1.4 for air) is reversible adiabatic, and $n \to \infty$ is constant volume (no work).

## Heat

**Heat** $Q$ is energy crossing a boundary because of a temperature difference. Like work, it's a transfer, not something a system contains. Both depend on the path, which is why we write $\delta Q$ and $\delta W$ for small amounts rather than $dQ$: they aren't changes of a property.

:::example Compressing 10 L of air to 2 L
Air at 100 kPa and 300 K (11.614 g) compressed from 10 L to 2 L:

1. Isobaric at 100 kPa: $W = 100\,000\cdot(-0.008) = -800.0$ J.
2. Isothermal: $W = 1000\ln0.2 = -1609.438$ J. Numerical integration of $p\,dV$ gives the same to $10^{-3}$ J.
3. Polytropic $n = 1.3$: $p_2 = 810.3$ kPa, $T_2 = 486.2$ K, $W = -2068.855$ J.
4. Adiabatic $n = 1.4$: $p_2 = 951.8$ kPa, $T_2 = 571.1$ K, $W = -2259.135$ J. The steeper the path, the more work it takes, because the gas heats up and pushes back harder.
5. Two rectangular paths between the same end states take $-800$ J and $-7615$ J. Work depends on the path.
:::

```python file=pvwork.py
"""Boundary work W = integral of p dV for isobaric, isothermal and polytropic processes, by numerical integration against the closed forms."""
import math
import numpy as np


def work_numeric(p_of_V, V1, V2, n=200001):
    V = np.linspace(V1, V2, n); p = p_of_V(V)
    return float(np.sum((p[1:] + p[:-1]) / 2 * np.diff(V)))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # 0.01 m^3 of air at 100 kPa and 300 K, compressed to 2 L
    p1, V1, T1, R = 100e3, 0.01, 300.0, 287.0
    m = p1 * V1 / (R * T1)
    print(f"mass of air {m*1000:.3f} g")
    V2 = 0.002
    W_iso_b = work_numeric(lambda V: np.full_like(V, p1), V1, V2)
    print(f"isobaric 10 -> 2 L at 100 kPa: W = {W_iso_b:.1f} J (p dV = {p1*(V2-V1):.1f})")
    W_iso_T = work_numeric(lambda V: p1 * V1 / V, V1, V2)
    exact = p1 * V1 * math.log(V2 / V1)
    print(f"isothermal: W = {W_iso_T:.3f} J, nRT ln(V2/V1) = {exact:.3f} J")
    assert abs(W_iso_T - exact) < 1e-3
    for n in (1.3, 1.4):
        W = work_numeric(lambda V: p1 * (V1 / V) ** n, V1, V2)
        p2 = p1 * (V1 / V2) ** n
        closed = (p2 * V2 - p1 * V1) / (1 - n)
        print(f"polytropic n = {n}: W = {W:.3f} J (closed form {closed:.3f}); p2 = {p2/1e3:.1f} kPa, T2 = {p2*V2/(m*R):.1f} K")
        assert abs(W - closed) < 1e-3 * abs(closed)
    # work is path dependent: same end states, different paths
    p2 = p1 * (V1 / V2) ** 1.4
    W_a = p1 * (V2 - V1)                  # compress at p1, then heat at constant volume to p2
    W_b = p2 * (V2 - V1)                  # heat at V1 up to p2, then compress at p2
    print(f"same end states, two rectangular paths: {W_a:.0f} J and {W_b:.0f} J")
```

:::exercise A pump stroke
A bicycle pump compresses 0.5 L of air at 100 kPa to 0.1 L so quickly that it's adiabatic ($n = 1.4$). Find the final pressure and the work input.
---solution---
1. $p_2 = 100\cdot5^{1.4} = 951.8$ kPa.
2. $W = (p_2V_2 - p_1V_1)/(1 - n) = (951.8\cdot0.1 - 100\cdot0.5)/(-0.4) = (95.18 - 50)/(-0.4) = -113.0$ J.
3. You do 113 J of work on the air, and it ends up much hotter. That's why the pump barrel warms up.
:::
