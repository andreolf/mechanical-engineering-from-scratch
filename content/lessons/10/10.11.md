A litre of water at 90 °C and a litre of seawater at 15 °C hold huge amounts of internal energy, but only the first is useful: you can run an engine between it and the surroundings. **Exergy** is the maximum work obtainable from a system as it comes into equilibrium with the environment, the **dead state** at $T_0$, $p_0$. Unlike energy, exergy is destroyed in every real process, and that destruction is what engineers should be minimising.

## Exergy of heat and of a flowing stream

Heat $Q$ delivered at temperature $T$ could at best drive a Carnot engine rejecting to $T_0$:

$$
X_Q = Q\left(1 - \frac{T_0}{T}\right) .
$$

1000 kJ at 90 °C is worth only 206.5 kJ of work; at 1500 °C, 837.5 kJ. Same energy, very different quality.

For a stream (ignoring kinetic and potential energy), the specific **flow exergy** is

$$
\psi = (h - h_0) - T_0(s - s_0) .
$$

## The exergy balance and Gouy-Stodola

In any device, exergy in = exergy out + exergy destroyed, and the destruction is

$$
\dot X_\text{dest} = T_0\,\dot S_\text{gen} \ge 0 ,
$$

the **Gouy-Stodola theorem**. A heat exchanger with no heat leaks has a first-law efficiency of 100 %, yet can destroy most of the exergy given up by the hot stream if the two streams are far apart in temperature. The **second-law efficiency**, exergy recovered over exergy supplied, shows that.

:::example A water heat exchanger
Dead state 15 °C. Hot water at 1 kg/s cools from 90 to 50 °C; cold water at 1.5 kg/s enters at 10 °C; $c = 4.186$ kJ/(kg K).

1. Duty $Q = 4.186\cdot40 = 167.44$ kW; the cold stream leaves at $10 + 40/1.5 = 36.67$ °C.
2. Exergy given by the hot stream: 26.678 kW. Gained by the cold stream: 4.596 kW.
3. Destroyed: 22.081 kW, equal to $T_0\dot S_\text{gen}$ to machine precision.
4. Second-law efficiency $4.596/26.678 = 0.172$. The cold stream, warmed to only 37 °C, can't store the quality of 90 °C heat.
:::

```python file=exergy.py
"""Exergy: flow exergy of water streams, a heat exchanger's exergy balance, and the Gouy-Stodola theorem X_dest = T0 S_gen."""
import math

C = 4.186         # kJ/(kg K)
T0 = 288.15       # dead state: 15 C


def flow_exergy(T):
    """Specific flow exergy (kJ/kg) of liquid water at T (K), ignoring pressure: (h - h0) - T0 (s - s0)."""
    return C * (T - T0) - T0 * C * math.log(T / T0)


def heat_exergy(Q, T):
    """Exergy (work potential) of heat Q delivered at constant temperature T."""
    return Q * (1 - T0 / T)


def exchanger(mh, Th_in, Th_out, mc, Tc_in):
    Q = mh * C * (Th_in - Th_out)
    Tc_out = Tc_in + Q / (mc * C)
    dXh = mh * (flow_exergy(Th_in) - flow_exergy(Th_out))
    dXc = mc * (flow_exergy(Tc_out) - flow_exergy(Tc_in))
    Sgen = mh * C * math.log(Th_out / Th_in) + mc * C * math.log(Tc_out / Tc_in)
    return Q, Tc_out, dXh, dXc, Sgen


if __name__ == "__main__":
    Q, Tc, dXh, dXc, S = exchanger(1.0, 363.15, 323.15, 1.5, 283.15)
    print(f"heat duty {Q:.2f} kW; cold stream leaves at {Tc-273.15:.2f} C")
    print(f"exergy given by hot stream {dXh:.3f} kW, gained by cold {dXc:.3f} kW")
    print(f"destroyed {dXh-dXc:.3f} kW; T0 x Sgen = {T0*S:.3f} kW; second-law efficiency {dXc/dXh:.3f}")
    assert abs((dXh - dXc) - T0 * S) < 1e-9           # Gouy-Stodola
    assert S > 0
    print(f"exergy of 1000 kJ of heat at 90 C: {heat_exergy(1000, 363.15):.1f} kJ; at 1500 C: {heat_exergy(1000, 1773.15):.1f} kJ")
    print(f"first-law efficiency of the exchanger (no heat loss): 1.000")
```

:::exercise Electric heating
An electric heater supplies 10 kW of heat to a room at 20 °C on a 0 °C day. What's the exergy of that heat, and the second-law efficiency of the heater? What does that say about heat pumps?
---solution---
1. Electricity is pure exergy: 10 kW in.
2. Exergy of the heat with $T_0 = 273.15$ K: $10(1 - 273.15/293.15) = 0.682$ kW.
3. Second-law efficiency $0.682/10 = 6.8$ %, even though the first-law efficiency is 100 %.
4. A device that used 0.682 kW of work to pump heat in would be ideal: that's a Carnot heat pump with COP 14.7. Real heat pumps with a COP of 3–4 are far from perfect but still 3–4 times better than resistance heating.
:::
