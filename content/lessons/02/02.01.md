A force has a size and a direction, and so does a velocity, a displacement or a moment. Vectors are the bookkeeping that keeps both together. Two products do most of the work in mechanics: the dot product measures how much one vector points along another, and the cross product builds the moment of a force about a point.

## Components and length

In a Cartesian frame a vector is a list of components, $\mathbf a = (a_1, a_2, a_3)$. Its length follows from Pythagoras:

$$
|\mathbf a| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2} .
$$

Adding vectors adds components, and scaling multiplies each one. That is the whole algebra; the geometry comes from the two products.

## The dot product

$$
\mathbf a \cdot \mathbf b = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3 = |\mathbf a|\,|\mathbf b| \cos\theta .
$$

The second form gives the angle between two vectors, and it says $\mathbf a \cdot \mathbf b = 0$ exactly when they're perpendicular. Dividing by $|\mathbf a|$ gives the **scalar projection** of $\mathbf b$ on $\mathbf a$, the length of the shadow $\mathbf b$ casts on the line of $\mathbf a$. The vector projection is

$$
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf a}\mathbf b = \frac{\mathbf a \cdot \mathbf b}{\mathbf a \cdot \mathbf a}\,\mathbf a .
$$

Mechanical work is a dot product: $W = \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf s$. Only the force component along the motion does work.

## The cross product

$$
\mathbf a \times \mathbf b = \begin{pmatrix} a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2 \\ a_3 b_1 - a_1 b_3 \\ a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\mathbf a \times \mathbf b| = |\mathbf a|\,|\mathbf b|\sin\theta .
$$

The result is perpendicular to both inputs, its direction follows the right-hand rule, and its length is the area of the parallelogram the two vectors span. Swapping the order flips the sign: $\mathbf b \times \mathbf a = -\,\mathbf a \times \mathbf b$.

The moment of a force $\mathbf F$ applied at position $\mathbf r$ about the origin is $\mathbf M = \mathbf r \times \mathbf F$. Its component along an axis $\mathbf u$ (a unit vector) is $\mathbf M \cdot \mathbf u$, the twisting effect about that axis.

:::example Moment on a bracket
A bracket bolted at the origin carries a cable force $\mathbf F = (30, -40, 120)$ N attached at $\mathbf r = (0.2, 0.5, 0)$ m.

1. Force magnitude: $|\mathbf F| = \sqrt{30^2 + 40^2 + 120^2} = 130$ N.
2. Moment: $\mathbf M = \mathbf r \times \mathbf F = (0.5\cdot120 - 0\cdot(-40),\; 0\cdot30 - 0.2\cdot120,\; 0.2\cdot(-40) - 0.5\cdot30) = (60, -24, -23)$ N m.
3. Size: $|\mathbf M| = \sqrt{60^2 + 24^2 + 23^2} = 68.6$ N m.
4. Twist about the bolt axis $\mathbf u = (0, 0, 1)$: $M_z = -23$ N m, clockwise seen from above. That's the torque the bolt must resist; the other two components bend it.
5. Check: $\mathbf M \cdot \mathbf r = 12 - 12 + 0 = 0$ and $\mathbf M \cdot \mathbf F = 1800 + 960 - 2760 = 0$. The moment is perpendicular to both, as it must be.
:::

```python file=vectors.py
"""A small vector toolkit: length, angle, projection, cross product and moments."""
import math
import random


def dot(a, b): return sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))
def norm(a): return math.sqrt(dot(a, a))
def scale(s, a): return [s * x for x in a]
def sub(a, b): return [x - y for x, y in zip(a, b)]


def cross(a, b):
    return [a[1] * b[2] - a[2] * b[1], a[2] * b[0] - a[0] * b[2], a[0] * b[1] - a[1] * b[0]]


def angle_deg(a, b):
    c = dot(a, b) / (norm(a) * norm(b))
    return math.degrees(math.acos(max(-1.0, min(1.0, c))))   # clamp: rounding can push c past 1


def project(b, a):
    """Vector projection of b on the line of a."""
    return scale(dot(a, b) / dot(a, a), a)


def triangle_area(p, q, r):
    return 0.5 * norm(cross(sub(q, p), sub(r, p)))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    r, F = [0.2, 0.5, 0.0], [30.0, -40.0, 120.0]
    M = cross(r, F)
    print("M =", M, "N m, |M| =", round(norm(M), 2))
    assert M == [60.0, -24.0, -23.0] and abs(norm(F) - 130) < 1e-12
    # the cross product is orthogonal to both inputs, for many random vectors
    rng = random.Random(1)
    for _ in range(10000):
        a = [rng.uniform(-10, 10) for _ in range(3)]
        b = [rng.uniform(-10, 10) for _ in range(3)]
        c = cross(a, b)
        assert abs(dot(c, a)) < 1e-12 * norm(a) * norm(b) * norm(a) + 1e-12
        assert abs(dot(c, b)) < 1e-12 * norm(a) * norm(b) * norm(b) + 1e-12
        # |a x b|^2 + (a.b)^2 = |a|^2 |b|^2 (Lagrange's identity)
        assert abs(dot(c, c) + dot(a, b) ** 2 - dot(a, a) * dot(b, b)) < 1e-9 * dot(a, a) * dot(b, b)
    # the residual of a projection is perpendicular to the line
    a, b = [3.0, 4.0, 0.0], [4.0, -1.0, 2.0]
    assert abs(dot(sub(b, project(b, a)), a)) < 1e-12
    assert abs(angle_deg(a, b) - 69.5647) < 1e-4
    assert abs(triangle_area([0, 0, 0], [1, 0, 0], [0, 1, 0]) - 0.5) < 1e-15
    print("all vector checks passed")
```

:::exercise Work along a ramp
A crate slides 4 m along a ramp in the direction $\mathbf s = 4\,(0.8, 0, -0.6)$ m while gravity $\mathbf F = (0, 0, -490)$ N acts on it. Find the work done by gravity, and the component of gravity along the ramp.
---solution---
1. Work: $W = \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf s = 4\,(0 + 0 + 490\cdot0.6) = 1176$ J.
2. The ramp direction $(0.8, 0, -0.6)$ is already a unit vector, so the component along it is $\mathbf F \cdot \mathbf u = 294$ N.
3. Check: $294 \text{ N} \times 4 \text{ m} = 1176$ J. The crate drops $4 \times 0.6 = 2.4$ m and $490 \times 2.4 = 1176$ J, the change in potential energy.
:::
