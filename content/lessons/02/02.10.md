Stiffness matrices, mass matrices, stress and strain tensors, inertia tensors: the matrices of mechanics are almost always symmetric. That's no accident (it comes from energy and from the balance of angular momentum), and it buys a guarantee: real eigenvalues and a full set of perpendicular eigenvectors.

## The spectral theorem

If $A = A^{\mathsf T}$ is real, then

$$
A = Q\Lambda Q^{\mathsf T}, \qquad Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I,
$$

with $\Lambda$ diagonal and real. In words: there is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, and in that basis the matrix is just a list of stretches along perpendicular axes.

Two parts of the proof are short enough to see:

1. **Real eigenvalues.** If $A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v$ with complex $\mathbf v$, then $\bar{\mathbf v}^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf v = \lambda\,\bar{\mathbf v}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf v$. The left side equals its own conjugate when $A$ is real and symmetric, and $\bar{\mathbf v}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf v > 0$, so $\lambda$ is real.
2. **Perpendicular eigenvectors.** For $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2$: $\lambda_1\,\mathbf v_2\cdot\mathbf v_1 = \mathbf v_2^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf v_1 = (A\mathbf v_2)^{\mathsf T}\mathbf v_1 = \lambda_2\,\mathbf v_2\cdot\mathbf v_1$, which forces $\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_2 = 0$.

## Principal stresses

The plane stress state at a point is the symmetric tensor $\boldsymbol\sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_x & \tau \\ \tau & \sigma_y \end{pmatrix}$. Its eigenvalues are the **principal stresses**, and its eigenvectors are the directions with no shear. For $2\times2$:

$$
\sigma_{1,2} = \frac{\sigma_x + \sigma_y}{2} \pm \sqrt{\Big(\frac{\sigma_x - \sigma_y}{2}\Big)^2 + \tau^2}, \qquad \tan 2\theta_p = \frac{2\tau}{\sigma_x - \sigma_y} .
$$

Mohr's circle is this formula drawn: centre at the mean stress, radius equal to the square root.

## The Jacobi eigenvalue method

For symmetric matrices there's a beautifully simple algorithm. Pick the largest off-diagonal entry $a_{pq}$ and rotate the $(p, q)$ plane by the angle that zeros it, exactly the principal-stress angle above. Repeat. Each rotation reduces the sum of squared off-diagonal entries, so the matrix converges to diagonal; the product of the rotations is $Q$.

:::example Principal stresses at a weld
A strain-gauge rosette gives $\sigma_x = 80$ MPa, $\sigma_y = 20$ MPa, $\tau_{xy} = 30$ MPa.

1. Mean stress: $(80 + 20)/2 = 50$ MPa.
2. Radius: $\sqrt{30^2 + 30^2} = 42.43$ MPa.
3. Principal stresses: $\sigma_1 = 92.43$ MPa, $\sigma_2 = 7.57$ MPa. Check: their sum is 100 (the trace) and their product $700$ equals $\det\boldsymbol\sigma = 1600 - 900$.
4. Direction: $\tan 2\theta_p = 60/60 = 1$, so $\theta_p = 22.5^\circ$ from the $x$ axis.
5. Maximum in-plane shear: the radius, $42.43$ MPa, acting on planes at $45^\circ$ to the principal ones.

For a steel with yield strength 235 MPa, the Tresca criterion compares $\sigma_1 - \min(\sigma_2, 0) = 92.4$ MPa with the yield stress. A safety factor of about 2.5.
:::

```python file=jacobi_eig.py
"""Jacobi eigenvalue method for symmetric matrices, with principal stresses as the test case."""
import math
import numpy as np


def jacobi_eig(A, tol=1e-14, max_rot=10000):
    A = np.array(A, dtype=float); n = len(A); Q = np.eye(n)
    for _ in range(max_rot):
        off = np.abs(A - np.diag(np.diag(A)))
        p, q = np.unravel_index(np.argmax(off), off.shape)
        if off[p, q] < tol * max(1.0, np.abs(np.diag(A)).max()):
            break
        theta = 0.5 * math.atan2(2 * A[p, q], A[p, p] - A[q, q])   # the principal-axis angle
        c, s = math.cos(theta), math.sin(theta)
        J = np.eye(n); J[p, p] = J[q, q] = c; J[p, q] = -s; J[q, p] = s
        A = J.T @ A @ J
        Q = Q @ J
    return np.diag(A), Q


def principal_2d(sx, sy, txy):
    c, r = (sx + sy) / 2, math.hypot((sx - sy) / 2, txy)
    return c + r, c - r, 0.5 * math.degrees(math.atan2(2 * txy, sx - sy))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    s1, s2, th = principal_2d(80, 20, 30)
    print(f"sigma1 = {s1:.2f} MPa, sigma2 = {s2:.2f} MPa, theta = {th:.1f} deg")
    assert abs(s1 - (50 + 30 * 2 ** 0.5)) < 1e-12 and abs(th - 22.5) < 1e-12
    w, Q = jacobi_eig([[80, 30], [30, 20]])
    assert np.allclose(sorted(w), [s2, s1]) and np.allclose(Q.T @ Q, np.eye(2))
    # the eigenvector of sigma1 points at 22.5 degrees
    v = Q[:, np.argmax(w)]
    assert abs(abs(math.degrees(math.atan2(v[1], v[0]))) % 180 - 22.5) < 1e-9
    # a 3D stress tensor (MPa)
    S = np.array([[50, 20, -10], [20, -30, 15], [-10, 15, 40.0]])
    w, Q = jacobi_eig(S)
    assert np.allclose(sorted(w), np.linalg.eigvalsh(S)) and np.allclose(Q @ np.diag(w) @ Q.T, S)
    assert np.allclose(Q.T @ Q, np.eye(3), atol=1e-13)
    print("3D principal stresses:", np.round(sorted(w, reverse=True), 3))
    # random symmetric matrices
    rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
    for n in (4, 8, 12):
        B = rng.standard_normal((n, n)); B = B + B.T
        w, Q = jacobi_eig(B)
        assert np.allclose(sorted(w), np.linalg.eigvalsh(B)) and np.allclose(Q.T @ Q, np.eye(n))
```

:::exercise Pure shear
A shaft surface in torsion has $\sigma_x = \sigma_y = 0$ and $\tau = 60$ MPa. Find the principal stresses and their directions. Why do brittle chalk sticks break along a helix at $45^\circ$ when twisted?
---solution---
1. Mean stress 0, radius $\sqrt{0 + 60^2} = 60$, so $\sigma_1 = 60$ MPa and $\sigma_2 = -60$ MPa.
2. $\tan 2\theta_p = 120/0$ is infinite, so $2\theta_p = 90^\circ$ and $\theta_p = 45^\circ$.
3. Brittle materials fail in tension. The largest tension acts at $45^\circ$ to the shaft axis, so the crack runs perpendicular to it, tracing a $45^\circ$ helix.
:::
