Equilibrium of a pin-jointed structure, currents in a circuit, a finite-element model with a million nodes: all of them end as $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$. Before solving big systems you need to see small ones two ways, as intersecting lines and as combinations of columns, and to know when a solution exists, is unique, and can be trusted.

## Two pictures of the same equations

Take

$$
\begin{aligned} 2x - y &= 0 \\ -x + 2y &= 3 \end{aligned}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

- **Row picture**: each equation is a line in the plane; the solution $(1, 2)$ is where they cross.
- **Column picture**: find weights $x, y$ so that $x\,(2, -1) + y\,(-1, 2) = (0, 3)$. The solution says how much of each column builds the right-hand side.

The column picture scales to any size and is the one linear algebra is built on: $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ has a solution exactly when $\mathbf b$ is a combination of the columns of $A$.

## Exist, unique, or neither

For a square $n \times n$ system there are three cases:

1. One solution: the columns are independent ($\det A \ne 0$).
2. No solution: the columns are dependent and $\mathbf b$ isn't in their span (parallel lines).
3. Infinitely many: dependent columns and $\mathbf b$ is in the span (the same line twice).

In a structure, case 2 or 3 means a **mechanism**: the bars can't carry the load, or they can carry it in endless ways because something can move freely.

## Nearly singular is the practical danger

Real data carries errors. If the two lines cross at a shallow angle, a tiny shift in one of them moves the crossing a long way. The **condition number** $\kappa(A) = \|A\|\,\|A^{-1}\|$ measures this amplification: relative errors in $\mathbf b$ can grow by a factor up to $\kappa$ in $\mathbf x$.

:::example Forces in a two-bar joint
A joint carries a 10 kN load downward. Bar 1 leaves the joint at $150^\circ$ from the $x$ axis, bar 2 at $45^\circ$. Call the bar forces $T_1$, $T_2$ (positive in tension, pulling the joint along the bar).

1. Equilibrium in $x$: $T_1\cos150^\circ + T_2\cos45^\circ = 0$.
2. Equilibrium in $y$: $T_1\sin150^\circ + T_2\sin45^\circ - 10 = 0$.
3. Matrix form: $\begin{pmatrix} -0.8660 & 0.7071 \\ 0.5 & 0.7071 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} T_1 \\ T_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 10 \end{pmatrix}$, with $\det = -0.966$.
4. Subtract the rows: $-1.366\,T_1 = -10$, so $T_1 = 7.32$ kN, then $T_2 = 0.866 \cdot 7.32 / 0.7071 = 8.97$ kN.

Both bars are in tension, and both carry less than 10 kN each only because they share the load at a good angle. Swing bar 2 towards $150^\circ$ and the determinant goes to zero while the forces blow up.
:::

```python file=linear_2x2.py
"""Solve 2x2 systems by Cramer's rule, check residuals, and measure conditioning."""
import math


def solve2(A, b):
    (a, c), (d, e) = A
    det = a * e - c * d
    if abs(det) < 1e-14 * max(abs(a * e), abs(c * d), 1e-300):
        raise ValueError("singular or nearly singular matrix")
    return [(b[0] * e - c * b[1]) / det, (a * b[1] - d * b[0]) / det]


def residual(A, x, b):
    return max(abs(sum(A[i][j] * x[j] for j in range(2)) - b[i]) for i in range(2))


def cond_inf(A):
    """Infinity-norm condition number of a 2x2 matrix."""
    (a, c), (d, e) = A
    det = a * e - c * d
    inv = [[e / det, -c / det], [-d / det, a / det]]
    n = lambda M: max(sum(abs(v) for v in row) for row in M)
    return n(A) * n(inv)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    t1, t2 = math.radians(150), math.radians(45)
    A = [[math.cos(t1), math.cos(t2)], [math.sin(t1), math.sin(t2)]]
    T = solve2(A, [0.0, 10.0])
    print(f"T1 = {T[0]:.3f} kN, T2 = {T[1]:.3f} kN, cond = {cond_inf(A):.2f}")
    assert abs(T[0] - 7.3205) < 1e-4 and abs(T[1] - 8.9658) < 1e-4
    assert residual(A, T, [0.0, 10.0]) < 1e-12
    assert solve2([[2, -1], [-1, 2]], [0, 3]) == [1.0, 2.0]
    # a nearly parallel pair: a 0.01 change in b moves x by about 1
    B = [[1.0, -1.0], [1.0, -1.01]]
    x0, x1 = solve2(B, [0.0, -0.01]), solve2(B, [0.0, 0.0])
    print("near-parallel shift:", [round(u - v, 3) for u, v in zip(x0, x1)], "cond", round(cond_inf(B)))
    assert abs(x0[0] - x1[0] - 1.0) < 1e-9 and cond_inf(B) > 400
    try:
        solve2([[1, 2], [2, 4]], [1, 2]); raise AssertionError("should be singular")
    except ValueError:
        pass
    print("all checks passed")
```

:::exercise A three-way check
For $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\mathbf x = \mathbf b$, say whether there are zero, one or infinitely many solutions when $\mathbf b = (3, 6)$ and when $\mathbf b = (3, 5)$, and give the solutions where they exist.
---solution---
1. The second column is twice the first, so $\det A = 4 - 4 = 0$ and the columns span only the line through $(1, 2)$.
2. $\mathbf b = (3, 6) = 3\,(1, 2)$ lies on that line: infinitely many solutions, $x_1 + 2x_2 = 3$, for example $(3, 0)$ or $(1, 1)$.
3. $\mathbf b = (3, 5)$ isn't a multiple of $(1, 2)$: no solution. In the row picture the lines $x_1 + 2x_2 = 3$ and $x_1 + 2x_2 = 2.5$ are parallel.
:::
