Lesson 05.13 gave the bending moment along a beam. This lesson turns a moment into stress, the step that decides whether a bamboo beam in a Bali pavilion, a floor joist or a bike handlebar holds. It's the most used formula in structural and mechanical design, and it rests on one geometric assumption.

## Plane sections remain plane

Euler–Bernoulli beam theory assumes that cross-sections stay flat and perpendicular to the bent axis. A bent beam then curves with radius $\rho$, and a fibre at distance $y$ above the **neutral axis** shortens in proportion to $y$:

$$
\varepsilon_x = -\frac{y}{\rho} = -\kappa\,y .
$$

That's the bending field you saw in 06.02. With Hooke's law, $\sigma_x = -E\kappa y$: stress varies linearly across the depth, zero on the neutral axis.

## Equilibrium of the section

Two conditions fix everything:

- No axial force: $\int\sigma\,dA = 0$, so the neutral axis passes through the **centroid** (05.17).
- The stresses add up to the moment: $M = -\int\sigma\,y\,dA = E\kappa\int y^2\,dA = E\kappa I$.

With $I = \int y^2\,dA$, the **second moment of area**:

$$
\kappa = \frac{M}{EI}, \qquad \sigma = -\frac{M\,y}{I}, \qquad \sigma_{max} = \frac{M\,c}{I} = \frac{M}{W} ,
$$

where $c$ is the distance to the outermost fibre and $W = I/c$ is the **section modulus**. For a rectangle $b\times h$: $I = bh^3/12$, $W = bh^2/6$. Depth counts with its square, so a plank is far stronger on edge than flat.

:::example A joist and a bamboo culm
1. Timber joist 50 × 200 mm with $M = 6$ kN m: $I = 50\cdot200^3/12 = 3.333\times10^7$ mm⁴, $\sigma_{max} = 6\times10^6\cdot100/3.333\times10^7 = 18$ MPa. The code integrates the stress field and gets zero axial force and exactly 6 kN m back.
2. Bamboo culm, outer diameter 100 mm, wall 8 mm, 3 m span, 2 kN at mid-span: $M = PL/4 = 1.5$ kN m.
3. $I = \pi(100^4 - 84^4)/64 = 2.465\times10^6$ mm⁴, so $\sigma_{max} = 1.5\times10^6\cdot50/2.465\times10^6 = 30.4$ MPa, comfortably below bamboo's bending strength (typically above 100 MPa).
4. The same amount of bamboo as a solid rod (54.3 mm diameter) would reach 95.7 MPa: 3.1 times higher. Nature's hollow tube is no accident.
:::

```python file=bending.py
"""Bending stress sigma = M y / I over a cross-section, checked by integrating the stress field."""
import math
import numpy as np


def stress_field(M, I, ys):
    """Positive M (sagging) compresses the top: sigma = -M y / I with y measured up from the neutral axis."""
    return -M * ys / I


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # a timber joist 50 x 200 mm, M = 6 kN m
    b, h, M = 50.0, 200.0, 6e6                    # mm, N mm
    I = b * h**3 / 12
    n = 2001
    y = np.linspace(-h / 2, h / 2, n); dy = h / (n - 1)
    s = stress_field(M, I, y)
    w = np.ones(n); w[1:-1:2] = 4; w[2:-1:2] = 2; w *= dy / 3 * b      # Simpson weights times width
    N, Mr = w @ s, -(w @ (s * y))
    print(f"joist: I = {I:.4e} mm^4, max stress {abs(s).max():.4f} MPa (M c / I = {M * (h / 2) / I:.4f}, 6M/(b h^2) = {6 * M / (b * h * h):.4f})")
    print(f"  resultant axial force {N:.2e} N (zero), resultant moment {Mr / 1e6:.6f} kN m (= M)")
    assert abs(abs(s).max() - M * (h / 2) / I) < 1e-9 and abs(N) < 1e-6 and abs(Mr - M) < 1e-3
    # a bamboo culm beam in Bali: outer diameter 100 mm, wall 8 mm, 3 m span, 2 kN at mid-span
    D, t, L, P = 100.0, 8.0, 3000.0, 2000.0
    d = D - 2 * t
    Ib = math.pi * (D**4 - d**4) / 64
    Mb = P * L / 4
    sb = Mb * (D / 2) / Ib
    print(f"bamboo culm: I = {Ib:.4e} mm^4, M = {Mb / 1e6:.2f} kN m, max bending stress {sb:.3f} MPa")
    # the same area as a solid rod would be much weaker
    A = math.pi * (D**2 - d**2) / 4; Ds = math.sqrt(4 * A / math.pi)
    ss = Mb * (Ds / 2) / (math.pi * Ds**4 / 64)
    print(f"  solid rod of the same area (D = {Ds:.2f} mm): {ss:.2f} MPa, {ss / sb:.2f} times higher")
    assert ss > 3 * sb
```

:::exercise A handlebar
An aluminium handlebar is a tube, 22.2 mm outside diameter, 2 mm wall. A rider pulls up with 400 N at 0.25 m from the stem clamp (a cantilever). Find the bending stress at the clamp.
---solution---
1. $M = 400\cdot250 = 1\times10^5$ N mm.
2. $d = 18.2$ mm, $I = \pi(22.2^4 - 18.2^4)/64 = 6.54\times10^3$ mm⁴.
3. $\sigma = 1\times10^5\cdot11.1/6537 = 170$ MPa. That's a large fraction of a typical 6061-T6 yield stress (about 275 MPa), and under repeated loading fatigue governs, which is why real bars are thicker or bulged at the clamp.
:::
