Statics told you the forces in a bike frame tube or a rail. It didn't tell you whether the tube survives. A 2 kN force is nothing for a thick steel bar and fatal for a thin wire. What matters is how the force is spread over the material, and that is **stress**. This lesson defines it properly, as the thing that gives the force per area on *any* cut through a body.

## Force per area on a cut

Cut a loaded body with a plane through a point, with unit normal $\mathbf n$. The material on one side pushes and pulls on the other. Over a tiny area $\Delta A$ it exerts a force $\Delta\mathbf F$. The **traction vector** is

$$
\mathbf t(\mathbf n) = \lim_{\Delta A\to0}\frac{\Delta\mathbf F}{\Delta A} \qquad [\text{N/m}^2 = \text{Pa};\ \text{engineers use MPa} = \text{N/mm}^2].
$$

Its component along $\mathbf n$ is the **normal stress** $\sigma_n = \mathbf t\cdot\mathbf n$ (tension positive). The part in the plane is the **shear stress** $\tau$. The traction depends on the plane you choose: the same point can carry pure tension on one plane and mostly shear on another.

## The stress tensor

Cauchy's result: you don't need to know $\mathbf t$ on every plane. Nine numbers, the tractions on the three coordinate planes, give all the others:

$$
\mathbf t = \boldsymbol\sigma\,\mathbf n, \qquad \boldsymbol\sigma = \begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{xx} & \tau_{xy} & \tau_{xz}\\ \tau_{xy} & \sigma_{yy} & \tau_{yz}\\ \tau_{xz} & \tau_{yz} & \sigma_{zz}\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The proof is equilibrium of a tiny tetrahedron with three faces on the coordinate planes and one with normal $\mathbf n$. Moment equilibrium of a tiny cube makes $\boldsymbol\sigma$ symmetric, so only **six** numbers are independent. Column $j$ of $\boldsymbol\sigma$ is the traction on the plane with normal $\mathbf e_j$.

:::example A 3D stress state in a frame lug
$\boldsymbol\sigma = \begin{pmatrix}80 & 30 & 0\\30 & -20 & 0\\0 & 0 & 10\end{pmatrix}$ MPa, plane normal $\mathbf n = (1,1,1)/\sqrt3$.

1. $\mathbf t = \boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n = (110, 10, 10)/\sqrt3 = (63.51, 5.77, 5.77)$ MPa.
2. Normal stress $\sigma_n = \mathbf t\cdot\mathbf n = 130/3 = 43.33$ MPa.
3. Shear $\tau = \sqrt{|\mathbf t|^2 - \sigma_n^2} = 47.14$ MPa. On this plane the shear is larger than the tension.
4. In 2D, a tube with 60 MPa axial and 25 MPa shear: the plane at 30° carries 66.65 MPa normal stress. Scanning every angle, the largest normal stress is 69.05 MPa, exactly the largest eigenvalue of $\boldsymbol\sigma$ (more in 06.07).
:::

```python file=stress.py
"""Cauchy stress: the traction on any plane is t = sigma n; split it into normal and shear parts."""
import numpy as np


def traction(sigma, n):
    n = np.asarray(n, float); n = n / np.linalg.norm(n)
    t = sigma @ n
    sn = t @ n                                   # normal stress (tension positive)
    tau = np.linalg.norm(t - sn * n)             # shear stress magnitude in the plane
    return t, sn, tau


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # a stress state in a bike frame lug (MPa); symmetric, as moment balance on a small cube demands
    S = np.array([[80.0, 30.0, 0.0], [30.0, -20.0, 0.0], [0.0, 0.0, 10.0]])
    assert np.allclose(S, S.T)
    n = np.array([1.0, 1.0, 1.0]) / np.sqrt(3)
    t, sn, tau = traction(S, n)
    print(f"plane n = (1,1,1)/sqrt3: traction {np.round(t, 4)} MPa, normal {sn:.4f} MPa, shear {tau:.4f} MPa")
    assert abs(np.linalg.norm(t) ** 2 - sn**2 - tau**2) < 1e-9
    # Cauchy's tetrahedron: the slanted face (area A, normal n) and the three coordinate faces (areas A n_i, outward -e_i)
    A = 1.0
    F = t * A + sum(S @ (-np.eye(3)[i]) * A * n[i] for i in range(3))
    print(f"tetrahedron force balance: {np.round(F, 12)}")
    assert np.allclose(F, 0)
    # on the coordinate planes the traction components are just the columns of sigma
    for i in range(3):
        assert np.allclose(traction(S, np.eye(3)[i])[0], S[:, i])
    # 2D: a tube under 60 MPa axial stress and 25 MPa torsional shear, planes rotated by phi
    S2 = np.array([[60.0, 25.0], [25.0, 0.0]])
    for deg in (0, 30, 45, 90):
        ph = np.radians(deg); n2 = np.array([np.cos(ph), np.sin(ph)])
        t2 = S2 @ n2; print(f"  plane at {deg:2d} deg: normal {t2 @ n2:8.4f} MPa, shear {t2 @ np.array([-n2[1], n2[0]]):8.4f} MPa")
    # the largest normal stress over all planes is the largest eigenvalue
    phis = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 100001)
    sn_all = 60 * np.cos(phis) ** 2 + 2 * 25 * np.sin(phis) * np.cos(phis)
    print(f"max normal stress over all planes {sn_all.max():.4f} MPa, largest eigenvalue {np.linalg.eigvalsh(S2).max():.4f}")
    assert abs(sn_all.max() - np.linalg.eigvalsh(S2).max()) < 1e-6
```

:::exercise A tie rod
A steel tie rod of 16 mm diameter carries 30 kN. Find the axial stress, then the normal and shear stress on a plane cut at 45° to the axis.
---solution---
1. $A = \pi\cdot8^2 = 201.1$ mm², so $\sigma = 30000/201.1 = 149.2$ MPa.
2. In the rod, $\boldsymbol\sigma = \operatorname{diag}(149.2, 0)$. With $\mathbf n = (\cos45°, \sin45°)$: $\mathbf t = (105.5, 0)$ MPa.
3. $\sigma_n = 105.5\cdot0.7071 = 74.6$ MPa and $\tau = 74.6$ MPa: half the axial stress each. Ductile metals in tension fail by slip on such planes, which is why their fractures show 45° shear lips.
:::
