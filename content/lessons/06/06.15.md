Bike frames, aircraft wings, car chassis rails and box girders all carry torsion in thin walls. For these, whether the section is closed or open matters more than anything else. Take a cardboard tube and twist it: stiff. Slit it along its length and twist again: it gives way like paper. This lesson puts numbers on that.

## Closed sections: Bredt's formula

In a thin closed wall the shear stress is nearly uniform through the thickness. The **shear flow** $q = \tau t$ (force per unit length along the wall) turns out to be constant all the way round, even if $t$ varies, by equilibrium of a little piece of wall. Its moment about any point must equal the torque. Since the moment of a constant shear flow round a closed loop is $q\cdot2A_m$, where $A_m$ is the area enclosed by the mid-line:

$$
q = \frac{T}{2A_m}, \qquad \tau = \frac{T}{2A_m t}, \qquad J = \frac{4A_m^2}{\oint ds/t} .
$$

These are the **Bredt–Batho** formulas. For a circular tube they agree with the exact $J$ within a fraction of a percent.

## Open sections

An open thin section (a slit tube, a channel, an I-beam) can't sustain a circulating shear flow. Each wall twists on its own, with the stress reversing through the thickness, like a thin flat strip:

$$
J \approx \sum \frac{b\,t^3}{3}, \qquad \tau_{max} = \frac{T\,t}{J} .
$$

Thickness enters cubed and the enclosed area doesn't appear at all. For a circular tube of mean radius $r_m$, the ratio closed/open is $3(r_m/t)^2$: hundreds of times stiffer for typical tubes.

## Design consequences

- Members that carry torsion should be closed: tubes, boxes, torsion boxes in wings.
- Open sections (I-beams, channels) are excellent in bending and poor in torsion. Loads that twist them should be avoided or they need bracing.
- A car's floor pan becomes stiff in torsion when the sills, tunnels and roof form closed loops.

:::widget slittube
An aluminium bike tube carries 60 N m of torque. Change the diameter and wall thickness, and slit it to see how much stiffness and strength the closed section provides.
:::

:::example A bike down tube, closed and slit
Aluminium ($G = 26$ GPa) tube, 35 mm outside, 1.2 mm wall: $r_m = 16.9$ mm, $A_m = 897.3$ mm².

1. Bredt: $J = 4A_m^2/(2\pi r_m/t) = 3.639\times10^4$ mm⁴, against an exact thick-wall $J$ of $3.644\times10^4$ (0.13 % low).
2. Slit: $J = 2\pi r_m t^3/3 = 61.16$ mm⁴. Closed/open = 595, which equals $3(r_m/t)^2$.
3. Under 60 N m: closed $\tau = T/(2A_mt) = 27.86$ MPa; slit $\tau = Tt/J = 1177$ MPa, 42 times higher. The slit tube would fail long before the closed one notices the load.
4. A box 120 × 60 × 3 mm: $J = 1.534\times10^6$ mm⁴ and $\tau = 25.0$ MPa at 1 kN m.
:::

```python file=thin_torsion.py
"""Thin-walled torsion: Bredt-Batho for closed sections against the open (slit) section."""
import math


def closed_J(Am, perimeter_over_t):
    """J = 4 Am^2 / (closed integral ds / t); shear flow q = T / (2 Am)."""
    return 4 * Am**2 / perimeter_over_t


def open_J(segments):
    """Sum of b t^3 / 3 over the thin walls (b = length, t = thickness)."""
    return sum(b * t**3 / 3 for b, t in segments)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    G = 26e3                                   # MPa, aluminium
    # a bike down tube: 35 mm outside diameter, 1.2 mm wall
    D, t = 35.0, 1.2
    rm = (D - t) / 2; Am = math.pi * rm**2; s = 2 * math.pi * rm
    Jc = closed_J(Am, s / t)
    Jexact = math.pi * (D**4 - (D - 2 * t) ** 4) / 32
    Jo = open_J([(s, t)])
    print(f"bike tube: Bredt J = {Jc:.1f} mm^4, exact thick-wall J = {Jexact:.1f} mm^4 ({(Jc / Jexact - 1) * 100:+.2f} %)")
    print(f"  slit along its length: J = {Jo:.2f} mm^4, closed / open = {Jc / Jo:.1f} (3 (rm/t)^2 = {3 * (rm / t) ** 2:.1f})")
    assert abs(Jc / Jexact - 1) < 0.01 and abs(Jc / Jo - 3 * (rm / t) ** 2) < 1e-9
    # stresses under 60 N m of twist
    T = 60e3
    tau_c = T / (2 * Am * t)                   # Bredt: q = T / (2 Am), tau = q / t
    tau_o = T * t / Jo                         # open section: tau_max = T t / J
    print(f"  60 N m: closed tube tau = {tau_c:.2f} MPa; slit tube tau = {tau_o:.1f} MPa ({tau_o / tau_c:.0f} times higher)")
    # a rectangular box beam 120 x 60 mm with 3 mm walls
    b, h, tw = 120.0, 60.0, 3.0
    Am = (b - tw) * (h - tw); per = 2 * ((b - tw) + (h - tw)) / tw
    Jb = closed_J(Am, per)
    print(f"box 120 x 60 x 3: J = {Jb:.4e} mm^4, tau at 1 kN m = {1e6 / (2 * Am * tw):.3f} MPa")
```

:::exercise A square tube against a channel
A square steel tube is 50 × 50 mm with 2 mm walls, under 200 N m. Find its shear stress and $J$. Then imagine cutting one wall along its length to make an open section with the same walls. What are $J$ and the stress now?
---solution---
1. Mid-line square 48 × 48 mm: $A_m = 2304$ mm², $\oint ds/t = 4\cdot48/2 = 96$.
2. Closed: $J = 4\cdot2304^2/96 = 2.212\times10^5$ mm⁴; $\tau = 200000/(2\cdot2304\cdot2) = 21.7$ MPa.
3. Open: $J = 4\cdot48\cdot2^3/3 = 512$ mm⁴, 432 times less; $\tau = 200000\cdot2/512 = 781$ MPa, beyond the yield strength of most steels.
:::
