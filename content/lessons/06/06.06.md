Swiss railway tracks are welded into continuous rails hundreds of metres long. On a hot summer afternoon the steel wants to grow by several centimetres, and it can't. The result is a large compressive force in the rail, with no load on the track at all. Thermal stress like this cracks engine blocks, buckles rails and breaks glass, and it follows from one extra term in Hooke's law.

## Thermal strain

A free bar heated by $\Delta T$ expands by a strain $\varepsilon_{th} = \alpha\,\Delta T$, without any stress. $\alpha$ is the coefficient of thermal expansion: about $12\times10^{-6}$/K for steel, $23\times10^{-6}$/K for aluminium, $9\times10^{-6}$/K for glass. The total strain is the sum of a mechanical and a thermal part:

$$
\varepsilon = \frac{\sigma}{E} + \alpha\,\Delta T .
$$

## Constrained expansion

If the bar can't change length at all, $\varepsilon = 0$ and

$$
\sigma = -E\,\alpha\,\Delta T .
$$

That doesn't depend on the bar's length or area. For steel, $E\alpha \approx 2.4$ MPa per kelvin: 30 K of heating gives about 72 MPa of compression. A strain gauge on that rail reads **zero**, since the total strain is zero. The stress is real but the strain is invisible.

If the bar can move a little first (an expansion gap $g$ in a bar of length $L$), it expands freely until the gap closes, at $\Delta T = g/(\alpha L)$. Beyond that, only the extra expansion is resisted: $\sigma = -E(\alpha\Delta TL - g)/L$.

## Rails and buckling

Continuous welded rail is laid and clamped at a **neutral temperature** well above the yearly average, often around 25 °C in central Europe. Cold days then put the rail in tension, which steel handles well. Very hot days put it in compression, and the track can buckle sideways ("sun kink") if the sleepers and ballast don't hold it. That's why track crews check ballast shoulders before summer.

:::example A welded rail through the seasons
UIC 60 rail, $A = 7686$ mm², $E = 210$ GPa, $\alpha = 11.5\times10^{-6}$/K, neutral temperature 25 °C.

1. Rail at 55 °C in the sun: $\sigma = -210000\cdot11.5\times10^{-6}\cdot30 = -72.45$ MPa. Force $72.45\cdot7686 = 557$ kN of compression.
2. Rail at $-20$ °C: $\Delta T = -45$ K, $\sigma = +108.7$ MPa, 835 kN of tension.
3. Old jointed track: 25 m rails with 5 mm gaps laid at 10 °C. The gaps close at $5/(11.5\times10^{-6}\cdot25000) = 17.4$ K above laying temperature. At 30 °C the rail is under only 6.3 MPa; at 45 °C, 42.5 MPa.
:::

```python file=thermal.py
"""Thermal stress: a fully constrained bar, a bar with an expansion gap, and a continuous welded rail."""
import math


def constrained_stress(E, alpha, dT):
    """Free thermal strain alpha dT is prevented completely: mechanical strain -alpha dT, stress -E alpha dT."""
    return -E * alpha * dT


def gap_stress(E, alpha, dT, L, gap):
    """The bar expands freely until it closes the gap, then is constrained. Compression only if alpha dT L > gap."""
    free = alpha * dT * L
    return 0.0 if free <= gap else -E * (free - gap) / L


if __name__ == "__main__":
    E, alpha = 210e3, 11.5e-6                       # MPa, 1/K for rail steel
    A = 7686.0                                      # mm^2, a UIC 60 rail
    T_neutral = 25.0                                # rail laid stress-free at 25 C
    for T in (55.0, -20.0):
        s = constrained_stress(E, alpha, T - T_neutral)
        print(f"rail at {T:5.1f} C: stress {s:8.2f} MPa, axial force {s * A / 1e3:8.1f} kN ({'compression' if s < 0 else 'tension'})")
    assert abs(constrained_stress(E, alpha, 30) + E * alpha * 30) < 1e-12
    # the strain gauge on a constrained rail reads zero: total strain = thermal + mechanical = alpha dT + s / E = 0
    s = constrained_stress(E, alpha, 30)
    assert abs(alpha * 30 + s / E) < 1e-15
    # old jointed track: 25 m rails with 5 mm gaps, laid at 10 C
    for T in (30.0, 45.0):
        s = gap_stress(E, alpha, T - 10.0, 25000.0, 5.0)
        print(f"jointed 25 m rail with a 5 mm gap at {T:.0f} C: free expansion {alpha * (T - 10) * 25000:.3f} mm, stress {s:.2f} MPa")
    # gap closes at dT = gap / (alpha L)
    dT_close = 5.0 / (alpha * 25000.0)
    print(f"the gap closes {dT_close:.2f} K above the laying temperature")
    assert gap_stress(E, alpha, dT_close - 1e-9, 25000, 5) == 0 and gap_stress(E, alpha, dT_close + 1, 25000, 5) < 0
    # aluminium expands twice as much as steel but is three times softer: the product E alpha sets the stress
    print(f"E alpha: steel {E * alpha:.4f} MPa/K, aluminium {70e3 * 23e-6:.4f} MPa/K")
```

:::exercise A brass bushing
A brass bushing ($E = 100$ GPa, $\alpha = 19\times10^{-6}$/K) is pressed between two rigid steel plates at 20 °C with no initial stress. The machine runs at 80 °C. Ignoring the plates' own expansion, what stress develops in the bushing? Brass yields at about 200 MPa; is it safe?
---solution---
1. $\sigma = -E\alpha\Delta T = -100000\cdot19\times10^{-6}\cdot60 = -114$ MPa.
2. Below 200 MPa, so it doesn't yield, but with a safety factor of only 1.75. Steel plates would expand too ($\alpha \approx 12\times10^{-6}$/K), so the real stress is set by the difference in $\alpha$: about $100000\cdot7\times10^{-6}\cdot60 = 42$ MPa.
:::
