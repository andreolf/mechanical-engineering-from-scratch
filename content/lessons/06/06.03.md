Stress and strain are linked by the material. For most engineering metals at working loads, and for bamboo along its fibres, the link is linear and reversible: double the load, double the stretch, unload and it springs back. That is Hooke's law, and two numbers capture it for an isotropic material. This lesson builds the full 3D version as a 6 × 6 matrix and runs a virtual tensile test on it.

## One dimension

Pull a bar: the axial strain is proportional to the stress, $\sigma = E\varepsilon$, with **Young's modulus** $E$. The bar also gets thinner. The ratio of lateral contraction to axial stretch is **Poisson's ratio** $\nu$:

$$
\varepsilon_{lat} = -\nu\,\varepsilon_{ax}.
$$

Typical values: steel $E = 210$ GPa, $\nu = 0.30$; aluminium 70 GPa, 0.33; bamboo along the culm around 18 GPa (but far softer across it). For isotropic materials, $\nu$ lies between $-1$ and $0.5$; rubber is close to 0.5 (nearly incompressible).

## Three dimensions

Superpose the three directions and add shear, $\tau = G\gamma$:

$$
\varepsilon_{xx} = \frac{1}{E}\big[\sigma_{xx} - \nu(\sigma_{yy} + \sigma_{zz})\big], \qquad \gamma_{xy} = \frac{\tau_{xy}}{G}, \qquad G = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)} .
$$

Inverted, with the **Lamé constants** $\lambda = \dfrac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}$ and $\mu = G$:

$$
\boldsymbol\sigma = \lambda\,\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon)\,\mathbf I + 2\mu\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon .
$$

In **Voigt notation** the six stresses and six strains (with engineering shear strains) become vectors and the law becomes $\boldsymbol\sigma = \mathbf C\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ with a 6 × 6 matrix. Finite element programs store exactly that. The **bulk modulus** $K = E/(3(1-2\nu))$ links pressure to volume change: $\Delta V/V = -p/K$.

:::example A virtual tensile test
The code builds $\mathbf C$ from $E$ and $\nu$, applies $\sigma_{xx} = 100$ MPa alone, and solves for the strains.

1. Steel: $\lambda = 121.15$ GPa, $G = 80.77$ GPa, $K = 175$ GPa. The test returns $E = 210$ GPa and $\nu = 0.3$ exactly, with $\varepsilon_{yy} = \varepsilon_{zz}$ and no shear strain.
2. Aluminium: $G = 26.32$ GPa, $K = 68.63$ GPa.
3. 100 MPa hydrostatic pressure on steel: volume strain $-100/175000 = -5.71\times10^{-4}$.
4. A 300 mm steel bar of 20 mm diameter at 100 MPa gets 0.143 mm longer and 2.86 µm thinner.
:::

```python file=hooke.py
"""Isotropic Hooke's law in Voigt form: 6x6 stiffness from E and nu, Lame constants, and a virtual tensile test."""
import numpy as np


def stiffness(E, nu):
    """Voigt order (xx, yy, zz, yz, xz, xy) with engineering shear strains gamma = 2 eps."""
    lam = E * nu / ((1 + nu) * (1 - 2 * nu)); mu = E / (2 * (1 + nu))
    C = np.zeros((6, 6))
    C[:3, :3] = lam
    C[:3, :3] += 2 * mu * np.eye(3)
    C[3:, 3:] = mu * np.eye(3)
    return C, lam, mu


MATERIALS = {"steel": (210e3, 0.30), "aluminium": (70e3, 0.33), "bamboo (along the culm, idealised isotropic)": (18e3, 0.30)}

if __name__ == "__main__":
    for name, (E, nu) in MATERIALS.items():
        C, lam, mu = stiffness(E, nu)
        K = E / (3 * (1 - 2 * nu))
        # virtual tensile test: apply sigma_xx = 100 MPa only, solve for the strains with the compliance C^-1
        sig = np.array([100.0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0])
        eps = np.linalg.solve(C, sig)
        E_meas, nu_meas = sig[0] / eps[0], -eps[1] / eps[0]
        print(f"{name:45s} lambda {lam / 1e3:7.3f} GPa, G {mu / 1e3:7.3f} GPa, K {K / 1e3:7.3f} GPa | test: E {E_meas / 1e3:.3f} GPa, nu {nu_meas:.4f}")
        assert abs(E_meas - E) < 1e-6 * E and abs(nu_meas - nu) < 1e-12
        assert abs(eps[1] - eps[2]) < 1e-15 and np.allclose(eps[3:], 0)
    # pure shear: tau = G gamma; hydrostatic pressure: volume change = -p / K
    C, lam, mu = stiffness(210e3, 0.3)
    eps = np.linalg.solve(C, np.array([0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 50.0]))
    assert abs(eps[5] - 50 / mu) < 1e-15
    eps = np.linalg.solve(C, -np.array([100.0, 100, 100, 0, 0, 0]))
    print(f"steel under 100 MPa hydrostatic pressure: volume strain {eps[:3].sum():.4e} (= -p/K = {-100 / (210e3 / (3 * 0.4)):.4e})")
    assert abs(eps[:3].sum() + 100 / (210e3 / (3 * (1 - 0.6)))) < 1e-15
    # a 300 mm steel bar under 100 MPa: stretches 0.143 mm and its 20 mm diameter shrinks by 2.9 micrometres
    e = 100 / 210e3
    print(f"300 mm bar, 20 mm diameter at 100 MPa: +{300 * e:.4f} mm long, {-20 * 0.3 * e * 1000:.3f} micrometres in diameter")
```

:::exercise A rubber pad and a steel block
A cube is squeezed with 10 MPa on the top face while its sides are free. Compare the lateral expansion of steel ($E = 210$ GPa, $\nu = 0.3$) and of rubber ($E = 5$ MPa, $\nu = 0.49$), as strains.
---solution---
1. Steel: $\varepsilon_{ax} = -10/210000 = -4.76\times10^{-5}$, lateral $+0.3\cdot4.76\times10^{-5} = 1.43\times10^{-5}$.
2. Rubber: $\varepsilon_{ax} = -10/5 = -2$. That's far beyond small strain, so the linear law no longer applies, which is the real lesson. At 0.1 MPa instead, $\varepsilon_{ax} = -0.02$ and lateral $+0.0098$: the rubber bulges almost as much as it shortens, keeping its volume.
:::
