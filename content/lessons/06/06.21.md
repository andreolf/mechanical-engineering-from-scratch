Gas bottles, boilers, scuba tanks, water mains, aircraft fuselages and the steel pipes that carry water down a mountain to a hydro turbine all hold pressure in a thin wall. Two lines of equilibrium give the stresses, and they explain why sausages split lengthwise.

## Hoop stress

Take a cylinder of radius $r$ and wall thickness $t \ll r$ under internal pressure $p$. Cut a ring of length $\ell$ in half along a diameter. The pressure pushes the half outwards with a resultant of $p\cdot2r\ell$ (the projected area). The two cut walls hold it back with $2\sigma_h t\ell$:

$$
\sigma_h = \frac{pr}{t} .
$$

## Axial stress

Cut across the cylinder instead. The pressure on the end cap, $p\pi r^2$, is carried by the wall's ring of area $2\pi rt$:

$$
\sigma_a = \frac{pr}{2t} = \frac{\sigma_h}{2} .
$$

The hoop stress is twice the axial stress, so a cylinder splits along its length. A welded seam running along the pipe is therefore more critical than a seam round it. A sphere has $\sigma = pr/(2t)$ in every direction: for the same material it needs half the wall thickness, which is why big gas tanks are spheres.

## Thin enough?

These formulas assume the stress is uniform through the wall. That is good to a few percent for $r/t > 10$. Thick vessels (gun barrels, hydraulic cylinders) need the Lamé solution, where the stress peaks at the inner wall.

## Penstocks

A penstock carries water from a reservoir down to the turbines. The static pressure grows with the drop: $p = \rho g H$. When valves close quickly, water hammer adds a surge (Phase 12), here allowed for with a factor of 1.25. Plates thicken down the mountain.

:::example A 650 m penstock
Pipe diameter 1.2 m. Allowable stress 160 MPa, weld efficiency 0.9, surge factor 1.25, 2 mm corrosion allowance.

1. The code first checks the formulas by equilibrium. It integrates the pressure on a half ring and on an end cap for $p = 2$ MPa, $r = 500$ mm, $t = 10$ mm, getting 100.00 and 50.00 MPa, a ratio of exactly 2.
2. At the turbines: $p = 1000\cdot9.81\cdot650\cdot1.25 = 7.97$ MPa.
3. $t = pr/(\sigma_{allow}\eta) + 2 = 7.97\cdot600/144 + 2 = 35.2$ mm. Use 36 mm plate.
4. With the corrosion allowance gone (34 mm left): hoop 140.7 MPa, axial 70.3 MPa.
5. Higher up, at 100 m of head, 7.1 mm is enough. Real penstocks step the plate thickness every few hundred metres.
:::

```python file=vessel.py
"""Thin-walled pressure vessels and a penstock wall-thickness calculator for an alpine hydro plant."""
import math
import numpy as np


def hoop_axial(p, r, t):
    return p * r / t, p * r / (2 * t)


def hoop_by_integration(p, r, t, n=200001):
    """Cut a 1 mm long ring in half: the vertical pressure resultant on the half must equal 2 * hoop force."""
    th = np.linspace(0, np.pi, n)
    Fy = np.trapezoid(p * r * np.sin(th), th)        # = 2 p r per unit length
    return Fy / (2 * t)


def axial_by_integration(p, r, t, n=200001):
    """End cap: pressure on the disc area pi r^2 is carried by the wall area 2 pi r t."""
    rr = np.linspace(0, r, n)
    F = np.trapezoid(p * 2 * np.pi * rr, rr)
    return F / (2 * np.pi * r * t)


def penstock_thickness(head_m, D_mm, allow=160.0, joint=0.9, surge=1.25, corrosion=2.0, rho=1000.0, g=9.81):
    """Wall thickness (mm) along a penstock: static head plus a water-hammer surge allowance."""
    p = rho * g * head_m * surge / 1e6               # MPa
    return p * (D_mm / 2) / (allow * joint) + corrosion, p


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # check the formulas by equilibrium
    p, r, t = 2.0, 500.0, 10.0
    h, a = hoop_axial(p, r, t)
    hi, ai = hoop_by_integration(p, r, t), axial_by_integration(p, r, t)
    print(f"p = 2 MPa, r = 500, t = 10: hoop {h:.2f} MPa (integrated {hi:.4f}), axial {a:.2f} MPa (integrated {ai:.4f}), ratio {h / a:.1f}")
    assert abs(hi - h) < 1e-6 * h and abs(ai - a) < 1e-6 * a and abs(h / a - 2) < 1e-12
    # alpine penstock, 1.2 m diameter, dropping 650 m from the intake to the turbines
    D = 1200.0
    for head in (0, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 650):
        tt, pp = penstock_thickness(head, D)
        print(f"  head {head:3d} m: design pressure {pp:5.2f} MPa, wall {tt:5.1f} mm")
    t_bot, p_bot = penstock_thickness(650, D)
    t_use = math.ceil(t_bot)
    hs, ax = hoop_axial(p_bot, D / 2, t_use - 2.0)
    print(f"bottom section: use {t_use} mm plate; with 2 mm corroded away, hoop {hs:.1f} MPa, axial {ax:.1f} MPa")
    assert hs <= 160 * 0.9 + 1e-9
    # a sphere of the same radius and wall carries half the hoop stress
    print(f"sphere, same p, r, t: {p * r / (2 * t):.1f} MPa in every direction")
```

:::exercise A scuba tank
An aluminium scuba tank has an outside diameter of 184 mm and is filled to 23 MPa (230 bar). The allowable stress is 200 MPa. Estimate the wall thickness with the thin-wall formula, and check whether thin-wall theory is reasonable.
---solution---
1. Use the mean radius, roughly 92 mm to start: $t = pr/\sigma = 23\cdot92/200 = 10.6$ mm.
2. With $t = 10.6$ mm the mean radius is $92 - 5.3 = 86.7$ mm, giving $t = 23\cdot86.7/200 = 9.97$ mm. Iterating once more gives about 10.0 mm.
3. $r/t \approx 8.7$: below 10, so thin-wall theory is only a rough guide. The Lamé thick-wall solution would give a slightly higher inner-wall stress. Real tanks are around 11 to 12 mm thick, with a margin for fatigue from repeated filling.
:::
