A good engineer can say "about ten thousand cubic metres a day" before opening a spreadsheet, and know how wrong that might be. In this lesson you estimate a quantity as a product of rough factors, put honest error bars on it, and check the error bars with a Monte Carlo simulation.

## Break the unknown into factors you can bound

Take a Subak irrigation canal in Bali, the kind that feeds terraced rice fields. How much water passes a point in one day? You don't know, but you can bound the pieces:

- Width $w$: somewhere between $0.4$ and $1.6\ \mathrm{m}$, best guess $0.8\ \mathrm{m}$
- Depth $d$: between $0.15$ and $0.6\ \mathrm{m}$, best guess $0.3\ \mathrm{m}$
- Mean flow speed $v$: between $0.25$ and $1\ \mathrm{m/s}$, best guess $0.5\ \mathrm{m/s}$

Volume per day is

$$
V = w\,d\,v\,t = 0.8 \times 0.3 \times 0.5 \times 86\,400 \approx 1.04\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{m^3}.
$$

## When a guess is "within a factor of 2", use logs

Each bound above is "best guess, times or divided by 2". Errors like that are multiplicative, so they live naturally on a log scale. If each factor is $x_i = \hat{x}_i\,k^{u_i}$ with $u_i$ uniform on $[-1, 1]$, then

$$
\ln V = \sum_i \ln \hat{x}_i + \ln k \sum_i u_i .
$$

A uniform variable on $[-1,1]$ has standard deviation $1/\sqrt{3}$. Independent errors add in quadrature, so with $m$ uncertain factors

$$
\sigma_{\ln V} = \ln k \,\sqrt{\frac{m}{3}} .
$$

With $m = 3$ uncertain factors and $k = 2$: $\sigma_{\ln V} = \ln 2 = 0.693$. A central 90% interval spans about $\pm 1.645\sigma$ in log space, a factor of $e^{1.645 \times 0.693} = 3.1$ either side. Two lessons follow:

1. The uncertainty of a product grows like $\sqrt{m}$, not $m$. Errors partly cancel.
2. When the low and high guesses differ by a lot, the best single number is their **geometric mean** $\sqrt{x_\text{low}\,x_\text{high}}$, not the arithmetic mean.

## Monte Carlo as a check on the algebra

The quadrature formula assumes independent errors and leans on the central limit theorem with only three terms. Sampling is the honest check: draw thousands of random input sets, compute $V$ for each and read off the percentiles.

```python file=fermi.py
"""Monte Carlo a Fermi estimate with log-uniform inputs, using only the standard library."""
import math
import random


def log_uniform(best, k, rng):
    return best * k ** rng.uniform(-1.0, 1.0)


def canal_volume(rng, k=2.0):
    w = log_uniform(0.8, k, rng)    # width, m
    d = log_uniform(0.3, k, rng)    # depth, m
    v = log_uniform(0.5, k, rng)    # mean speed, m/s
    return w * d * v * 86400.0      # m^3 per day


def percentile(sorted_xs, p):
    i = p * (len(sorted_xs) - 1)
    lo, hi = math.floor(i), math.ceil(i)
    return sorted_xs[lo] + (sorted_xs[hi] - sorted_xs[lo]) * (i - lo)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = random.Random(42)
    xs = sorted(canal_volume(rng) for _ in range(200_000))
    med, p05, p95 = (percentile(xs, p) for p in (0.5, 0.05, 0.95))
    hand = 0.8 * 0.3 * 0.5 * 86400
    sigma = math.log(2) * math.sqrt(3 / 3)
    print(f"hand {hand:.0f}, median {med:.0f}, 90% [{p05:.0f}, {p95:.0f}]")
    # median within one decade of the hand calculation (the lesson's test)
    assert abs(math.log10(med / hand)) < 1
    # in fact the median sits on the hand value, because log-errors are symmetric
    assert abs(med / hand - 1) < 0.02
    # the quadrature formula predicts the 90% factor within a few percent
    assert abs((p95 / med) / math.exp(1.645 * sigma) - 1) < 0.06
```

The simulated 90% factor comes out within a few percent of the hand value of 3.1, even with only three factors. The algebra was good enough.

:::exercise Hot water for a Zurich flat
Estimate the energy a household of 4 uses per year to heat shower and tap water. Assume 40 litres of hot water per person per day, heated by $40\ \mathrm{K}$, with $c = 4186\ \mathrm{J/(kg\,K)}$. Give the answer in kWh.
---solution---
1. Mass per day: $4 \times 40\ \mathrm{L} = 160\ \mathrm{kg}$.
2. Energy per day: $Q = m c \Delta T = 160 \times 4186 \times 40 = 2.68\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{J}$.
3. In kWh: $2.68\times10^{7} / 3.6\times10^{6} = 7.44\ \mathrm{kWh}$ per day.
4. Per year: $7.44 \times 365 \approx 2\,720\ \mathrm{kWh}$, roughly $3\,000\ \mathrm{kWh}$.
5. Sanity check: Swiss statistics put hot water at a few thousand kWh per household per year, so the estimate is the right order of magnitude.
:::
