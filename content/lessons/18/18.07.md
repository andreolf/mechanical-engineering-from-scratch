A kitchen scale, a crane's overload sensor and a bicycle power meter all use the same trick: let the force bend a piece of metal a little, measure the strain with gauges in a bridge, and calibrate the result against known weights. This lesson designs a 5 kg bending-beam load cell and calibrates it.

## The bending-beam load cell

A cantilever of width $b$ and thickness $h$ carries the force $F$ at a distance $a$ from the gauges. The bending moment there is $M = Fa$, and the surface strain is

$$
\varepsilon = \frac{M\,(h/2)}{E\,I} = \frac{6\,F\,a}{E\,b\,h^2} .
$$

Put two gauges on top ($+\varepsilon$) and two underneath ($-\varepsilon$) in a full bridge (lesson 18.02). The output is $V_\text{out}/V = GF\,\varepsilon$, usually quoted in **mV/V**: a cell rated at 2 mV/V gives 10 mV at full load on 5 V excitation. Design for about 1000 µε at rated load: enough signal, and a stress far below yield so the beam returns to zero and survives overloads.

## Calibration by least squares

Beam theory predicts the sensitivity within a few per cent, but gauge position, glue and the beam's real thickness change it, so every cell is **calibrated**. Apply known masses $m_i$, read the outputs $r_i$, and fit $r = r_0 + S m$ by least squares:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} r_0 \\ S \end{bmatrix} = (A^\mathsf{T}A)^{-1}A^\mathsf{T} r, \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & m_1 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & m_n \end{bmatrix} .
$$

The residual standard deviation $s$ gives the parameter covariance $s^2(A^\mathsf{T}A)^{-1}$, which feeds the uncertainty of every later reading.

## The numbers on a datasheet

- **Linearity error**: the largest deviation of the calibration points from the best-fit straight line, as a percentage of full scale (% FS).
- **Hysteresis**: the largest difference between the loading and unloading readings at the same load, as % FS.
- **Zero** and **sensitivity**, with their uncertainties.

Commercial cells of OIML class C3 are within about 0.02 % FS on each.

:::widget loadcal
Residuals of a 5 kg calibration from its best-fit line, loading (lime) and unloading (amber). A bow means non-linearity; a gap between the curves means hysteresis.
:::

:::example Design and calibrate a 5 kg cell
**Design.** Aluminium 6061-T6 ($E = 69$ GPa, yield 276 MPa), $b = 12.7$ mm, $a = 60$ mm, $F = 5 \times 9.81 = 49.05$ N.

1. For 1000 µε, $h = \sqrt{6Fa/(E b \varepsilon)} = 4.489$ mm. Choose 4.5 mm: $\varepsilon = 995.1$ µε, stress 68.7 MPa, a factor of 4.02 on yield.
2. Full bridge with $GF = 2.0$: $1.9902$ mV/V. On 5 V excitation that predicts 1.99019 mV/kg.

**Calibration.** Masses 0 to 5 kg in 1 kg steps up and back down, read with an amplifier noise of 0.4 µV.

3. Least squares: sensitivity 1.98988 mV/kg ± 0.00049, within 0.02 % of the beam-theory prediction, and zero 9.75 µV.
4. Residual standard deviation 2.57 µV. Linearity error 0.039 % FS, hysteresis 0.047 % FS: about class C3.
5. **Use.** A reading of 4.21 mV gives $(4.21 - r_0)/S = 2.1108$ kg. Propagating the fit covariance and the residual scatter gives ±2.7 g ($k = 2$).
:::

```python file=loadcell.py
"""A bending-beam load cell: size the beam for 1000 microstrain at rated load, predict the
full-bridge output, then calibrate it with known masses by least squares and report sensitivity,
zero, linearity error, hysteresis and the uncertainty of a reading. numpy only."""
import numpy as np

g = 9.81
E, Sy, GF = 69e9, 276e6, 2.0            # aluminium 6061-T6: modulus, yield; foil gauges
b, a, m_rated = 12.7e-3, 60e-3, 5.0     # beam width, load-to-gauge distance, rated mass
F = m_rated * g
h_req = np.sqrt(6 * F * a / (E * b * 1000e-6))          # eps = M c / (E I) = 6 F a / (E b h^2)
h = 4.5e-3
eps = 6 * F * a / (E * b * h**2)
sig = E * eps
print(f"required h = {h_req*1e3:.3f} mm, chosen {h*1e3:.1f} mm: strain {eps*1e6:.1f} microstrain, stress {sig/1e6:.1f} MPa, yield factor {Sy/sig:.2f}")
print(f"full bridge (2 gauges on top, 2 below): output GF eps = {GF*eps*1e3:.4f} mV/V at rated load")
assert 900e-6 < eps < 1100e-6 and Sy / sig > 3

# calibration: 0, 1, ..., 5 kg up then down; the 'true' cell has 0.05 % FS non-linearity and hysteresis
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
Vex = 5.0
masses = np.r_[np.arange(6), np.arange(4, -1, -1)].astype(float)
up = np.r_[np.ones(6), np.zeros(5)].astype(bool)
FS = GF * eps * Vex * 1e3                                       # full-scale output, mV
x = masses / m_rated
true = 0.012 + FS * (x - 0.0005 * 4 * x * (1 - x)) + np.where(up, -1, 1) * 0.0002 * FS * np.sin(np.pi * x)
reading = true + 0.0004 * rng.standard_normal(len(masses))     # 0.4 microvolt amplifier noise, mV units
A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(masses), masses])
coef, res, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, reading, rcond=None)
zero, sens = coef
fit = A @ coef
resid = reading - fit
s = np.sqrt(np.sum(resid**2) / (len(masses) - 2))
cov = s**2 * np.linalg.inv(A.T @ A)
lin = np.max(np.abs(resid)) / (sens * m_rated) * 100
hyst = max(abs(reading[(masses == m) & ~up][0] - reading[(masses == m) & up][0]) for m in range(1, 5)) / (sens * m_rated) * 100
print(f"fit: zero {zero*1e3:.2f} microvolt, sensitivity {sens:.5f} mV/kg (+- {np.sqrt(cov[1,1]):.5f}); predicted {FS/m_rated:.5f} mV/kg")
print(f"best-fit-line linearity error {lin:.3f} % FS, hysteresis {hyst:.3f} % FS, residual s = {s*1e3:.2f} microvolt")
for m, r, e in zip(masses, reading, resid): print(f"  {m:3.0f} kg {r:9.5f} mV   residual {e*1e3:+7.2f} microvolt")
assert abs(sens / (FS / m_rated) - 1) < 0.01                  # the beam theory predicts the sensitivity to 1 %
assert lin < 0.1 and hyst < 0.1
# use: a reading of 4.21 mV; uncertainty of the inverse prediction m = (r - zero) / sens
r = 4.21
m_est = (r - zero) / sens
J = np.array([-1 / sens, -(r - zero) / sens**2])
u_m = np.sqrt(J @ cov @ J + (s / sens) ** 2)
print(f"reading {r} mV -> {m_est:.4f} kg +- {2*u_m*1e3:.1f} g (k = 2)")
assert 2 * u_m < 0.005
```

:::exercise Make it a 50 kg cell
Keep $b$ and $a$ and the 1000 µε target. How thick must the beam be for 50 kg, and what happens to the safety factor on yield?
---solution---
1. $h \propto \sqrt F$, so $h = 4.489 \times \sqrt{10} = 14.20$ mm.
2. The design strain is the same 1000 µε, so the stress is again $E\varepsilon = 69$ MPa and the factor on yield stays about 4.
3. A thicker beam is stiffer: its deflection scales as $F a^3/(E b h^3)$, so the 50 kg cell deflects $10/10^{1.5} = 0.32$ times as much as the 5 kg cell at full load.
:::
