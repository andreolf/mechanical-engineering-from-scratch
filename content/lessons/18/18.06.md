How much water does a canal carry? In Bali the answer decides how a subak shares water between terraces. In Zurich the Federal Office for the Environment publishes the Limmat's discharge every ten minutes. Both rest on the same idea: discharge is velocity times area, added up over the cross-section.

## The velocity-area method

Discharge is the flux of velocity through the cross-section $A$:

$$
Q = \int_A u\,dA \approx \sum_i \bar v_i\, A_i .
$$

Divide the section into panels with **verticals** at positions $y_i$. At each one, measure the depth $d_i$ with a wading rod and the depth-mean velocity $\bar v_i$ with a current meter. The **mid-section method** (ISO 748) gives each vertical a panel reaching halfway to its neighbours:

$$
Q = \sum_i \bar v_i\, d_i\, w_i, \qquad w_i = \frac{y_{i+1} - y_{i-1}}{2} ,
$$

with the banks as the end points. ISO 748 asks for at least 20 verticals, with no panel carrying more than 10 % of the flow.

## Where to hold the meter: the 0.6-depth rule

In a rough channel the velocity grows from the bed roughly as a power law, $u = u_s (z/d)^{1/m}$ with $m \approx 7$. Its depth-mean is

$$
\bar v = \frac{1}{d}\int_0^d u_s\left(\frac zd\right)^{1/7} dz = \frac78\,u_s ,
$$

and the profile takes that value at $z/d = (7/8)^7 = 0.39$. So a single reading at 0.6 of the depth **below the surface** (0.4 above the bed) gives the mean velocity. The 0.2/0.8 two-point method is more robust when the profile is not a clean power law.

## The float method

With no current meter, time an orange or a stick over a known length $L$. That gives the **surface** velocity $L/t$, so multiply by a coefficient $k = \bar v/u_s$. For the 1/7 profile $k = 0.875$; field guides quote 0.8 to 0.9 depending on depth and roughness. The uncertainty in $k$ usually dominates the error, so the float method is a 5 to 10 % method at best.

:::example Gauge an irrigation canal
The canal is 3 m wide with a parabolic bed, 0.8 m deep at the centre, and a surface velocity of 0.9 m/s at the centre that falls towards the banks as $(d/D)^{2/3}$.

1. **Exact.** The area is $2BD/3 = 1.6$ m², and integrating $\tfrac78 u_s d$ across the section gives $Q = 1.07465$ m³/s.
2. **0.6-depth rule.** $0.4^{1/7} = 0.87731$, within 0.3 % of 0.875.
3. **Current meter**, depths read to 5 mm and velocities with 2 % scatter:

| Verticals | Mid-section $Q$, m³/s | Error | Floats, $k = 0.85$, m³/s | Error |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 1.0501 | −2.28 % | 1.0258 | −4.55 % |
| 10 | 1.0676 | −0.66 % | 1.0407 | −3.16 % |
| 20 | 1.0797 | +0.47 % | 1.0591 | −1.45 % |

4. With perfect readings at 20 verticals the method gives 1.07664 m³/s (+0.19 %), so the remaining error is the instruments, not the method.
5. The floats carry a systematic −2.9 % error because the textbook $k = 0.85$ is not this canal's 0.875. Random timing errors average out over many verticals; that bias does not.
:::

```python file=riverflow.py
"""Discharge of a canal by the velocity-area method: mid-section sums from current-meter readings
at 0.6 depth, and the float method with a surface-to-mean coefficient. A synthetic cross-section
with a 1/7-power velocity profile gives the exact answer to compare against. numpy only."""
import numpy as np

B, D, Us = 3.0, 0.8, 0.9          # canal width m, max depth m, max surface velocity m/s
depth = lambda y: D * np.clip(1 - (2 * y / B - 1) ** 2, 0, None)           # parabolic bed
usurf = lambda y: Us * (depth(y) / D) ** (2 / 3)                             # faster where deeper (Manning-like)
u = lambda y, z: usurf(y) * np.clip(z / np.maximum(depth(y), 1e-12), 0, 1) ** (1 / 7)   # z above the bed

# exact discharge: the depth-mean of a 1/7 profile is 7/8 of the surface value
yf = np.linspace(0, B, 200_001)
Q_exact = np.trapezoid(7 / 8 * usurf(yf) * depth(yf), yf)
zf = np.linspace(0, 1, 100_001)
assert abs(np.trapezoid(zf ** (1 / 7), zf) - 7 / 8) < 1e-6
print(f"exact Q = {Q_exact:.5f} m^3/s, area {np.trapezoid(depth(yf), yf):.5f} m^2 (2BD/3 = {2*B*D/3:.5f})")
print(f"0.6 depth below the surface is 0.4 d above the bed: u/u_s = 0.4^(1/7) = {0.4**(1/7):.5f} vs mean 0.875")

def mid_section(yv, dv, vv):
    """ISO 748 mid-section method: Q = sum v_i d_i w_i, panel width w_i = (y_{i+1} - y_{i-1}) / 2."""
    yy = np.concatenate([[0.0], yv, [B]])                      # the banks have zero depth
    w = (yy[2:] - yy[:-2]) / 2
    A = dv * w
    return np.sum(vv * A), A

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
print(" verticals   Q, 0.6-depth meter   error    Q, floats (k=0.85)   error")
for n in (5, 10, 20):
    yv = (np.arange(n) + 0.5) * B / n
    dv = depth(yv) + 0.005 * rng.standard_normal(n)            # wading rod read to about 5 mm
    v06 = u(yv, 0.4 * depth(yv)) * (1 + 0.02 * rng.standard_normal(n))   # current meter, 2 % repeatability
    Q06, A = mid_section(yv, dv, v06)
    assert abs(Q06 - np.sum(v06 * A)) < 1e-15                  # the discharge is the sum of v_i A_i
    L = 10.0                                                   # floats timed over 10 m
    tf = L / usurf(yv) * (1 + 0.04 * rng.standard_normal(n))   # stopwatch reaction, wind gusts
    Qf, _ = mid_section(yv, dv, 0.85 * L / tf)
    print(f"{n:6d} {Q06:16.4f} {(Q06/Q_exact-1)*100:+9.2f} % {Qf:16.4f} {(Qf/Q_exact-1)*100:+9.2f} %")
    if n >= 10: assert abs(Q06 / Q_exact - 1) < 0.05
yv = (np.arange(20) + 0.5) * B / 20
Qn, _ = mid_section(yv, depth(yv), u(yv, 0.4 * depth(yv)))
print(f"20 verticals, no reading errors: {Qn:.5f} ({(Qn/Q_exact-1)*100:+.2f} %): the rest of the error above is the instruments")
assert abs(Qn / Q_exact - 1) < 0.01
```

:::exercise Gauge a ditch with oranges
A rectangular ditch is 1.2 m wide and 0.35 m deep. Three oranges take 12.4, 11.8 and 12.9 s to float 8 m down the centre. Estimate the discharge with $k = 0.85$, and say what limits the result.
---solution---
1. Mean time 12.37 s, surface velocity $8/12.37 = 0.647$ m/s.
2. Mean velocity $0.85 \times 0.647 = 0.550$ m/s. Area $1.2 \times 0.35 = 0.42$ m².
3. $Q \approx 0.231$ m³/s, about 230 L/s.
4. The oranges ran down the centre, the fastest part of the ditch, so the sidewalls make the real mean lower. With $k$ only known within about ±10 %, quote $Q = 0.23 \pm 0.03$ m³/s.
:::
