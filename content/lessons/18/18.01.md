A measurement without an uncertainty is only half a result. "The flow is 7.3 L/s" says little. "7.3 L/s, 95 % interval 3.5 to 12.1 L/s" tells the reader whether the number can support a decision. The international rulebook is the **GUM**, the *Guide to the expression of uncertainty in measurement* (JCGM 100:2008).

## Standard uncertainty: type A and type B

Every input $x_i$ gets a **standard uncertainty** $u(x_i)$, the standard deviation of what you believe about it. The GUM names two ways to get it:

- **Type A**, from statistics of repeated readings. For $n$ readings with sample standard deviation $s$, the uncertainty of the mean is $u = s/\sqrt n$.
- **Type B**, from anything else: a resolution, a calibration certificate, a tolerance. If all you know is that the value lies within $\pm a$, a rectangular distribution gives $u = a/\sqrt 3$.

Both kinds are then treated the same way. The labels say how you got the number, not how much you trust it.

## Propagation by linearisation

For a result $y = f(x_1, \dots, x_N)$ with independent inputs, a first-order Taylor expansion gives the **combined standard uncertainty**

$$
u_c^2(y) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} c_i^2\, u^2(x_i), \qquad c_i = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} .
$$

The $c_i$ are **sensitivity coefficients**, and each term $c_i^2 u^2(x_i)/u_c^2$ is that input's share of the **uncertainty budget**. For a power law $y = x_1^{a} x_2^{b}$, this reduces to relative uncertainties: $(u_c/y)^2 = a^2 (u_1/x_1)^2 + b^2 (u_2/x_2)^2$. The **expanded uncertainty** $U = k u_c$ with $k = 2$ gives roughly a 95 % interval when the result is close to normal.

## Propagation by Monte Carlo

GUM Supplement 1 skips the Taylor series. Draw $M$ samples of every input from its distribution, push each set through $f$, and read the mean, the standard deviation and the 2.5 % and 97.5 % percentiles of the results. It costs a few hundred thousand function calls and needs no derivatives. When $f$ is close to linear over $\pm 2u$ of its inputs, both methods agree. When it is not, Monte Carlo is the one to believe.

:::example g from a pendulum, then a weir that breaks linearisation
**Pendulum.** Ten stopwatch readings of 20 swings: 40.13, 40.21, 40.08, 40.17, 40.25, 40.11, 40.19, 40.06, 40.22, 40.15 s. The length to the bob centre is $L = 1.000$ m $\pm 2$ mm (tape measure).

1. Period $T = 2.00785$ s. Type A: $u = 0.00099$ s. Add the 0.01 s stopwatch resolution as type B, $0.01/\sqrt3/20$, to get $u(T) = 0.00103$ s.
2. $u(L) = 0.002/\sqrt 3 = 0.00115$ m.
3. $g = 4\pi^2 L/T^2 = 9.7926$ m/s². Sensitivities: $\partial g/\partial L = 9.7926$ s⁻², $\partial g/\partial T = -2g/T = -9.7543$ m/s³.
4. $u_c = 0.0151$ m/s², so $g = 9.793 \pm 0.030$ m/s² ($k = 2$). The budget is 56 % length and 44 % period: a better tape measure helps as much as more timing.
5. Monte Carlo with 400 000 samples: mean 9.7926, $u = 0.0151$, 95 % interval [9.7639, 9.8214]. The methods agree to better than 1 %. The local value in Zurich, 9.807 m/s², is inside the interval.

**Weir.** $Q = \tfrac23 C_d b\sqrt{2g}\,h^{1.5}$ with $C_d = 0.62 \pm 0.02$, $b = 0.50$ m $\pm 5$ mm, and a small head $h = 40 \pm 8$ mm (ripples on the staff gauge).

6. Linearised: $Q = 7.323$ L/s, $U = 4.420$ L/s, interval [2.903, 11.744] L/s.
7. Monte Carlo: mean 7.434 L/s and 95 % interval [3.465, 12.088] L/s. Because $h^{1.5}$ is convex, the mean is biased 1.5 % upwards and the interval reaches 4.77 L/s above the estimate but only 3.86 L/s below it. Linearisation cannot see either effect. Report the Monte Carlo interval, and if the decision matters, measure the head in a stilling well.
:::

```python file=uncertainty.py
"""GUM uncertainty propagation two ways: linearisation (first-order Taylor, numerical partial
derivatives) and Monte Carlo (GUM Supplement 1). Case 1, a pendulum measurement of g, is close
to linear and the two agree. Case 2, a weir discharge from a small measured head, is not. numpy only."""
import numpy as np

def type_a(x):
    """Type A: standard uncertainty of the mean of n repeated readings, s / sqrt(n)."""
    x = np.asarray(x, float)
    return x.mean(), x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(x))

def type_b_rect(half_width):
    """Type B, rectangular distribution of half-width a (a resolution or a tolerance): a / sqrt(3)."""
    return half_width / np.sqrt(3)

def linearised(f, x, u):
    """u_c^2 = sum (df/dx_i)^2 u_i^2 for independent inputs; derivatives by central differences."""
    x, u = np.asarray(x, float), np.asarray(u, float)
    c = np.empty_like(x)
    for i in range(len(x)):
        h = 1e-6 * max(abs(x[i]), 1e-12)
        xp, xm = x.copy(), x.copy(); xp[i] += h; xm[i] -= h
        c[i] = (f(*xp) - f(*xm)) / (2 * h)
    return f(*x), np.sqrt(np.sum((c * u) ** 2)), c

def monte_carlo(f, samplers, M=400_000, seed=1):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    y = f(*[s(rng, M) for s in samplers])
    lo, hi = np.percentile(y, [2.5, 97.5])
    return y.mean(), y.std(ddof=1), lo, hi

# ---------- case 1: g from a pendulum, g = 4 pi^2 L / T^2 ----------
# ten stopwatch readings of 20 swings each, seconds
t20 = np.array([40.13, 40.21, 40.08, 40.17, 40.25, 40.11, 40.19, 40.06, 40.22, 40.15])
T, uT_a = type_a(t20 / 20)                       # period and its type A uncertainty
uT = np.hypot(uT_a, type_b_rect(0.01) / 20)       # add the 0.01 s stopwatch resolution, spread over 20 swings
L, uL = 1.0000, type_b_rect(0.002)               # tape measure to the bob centre, +-2 mm
g = lambda L, T: 4 * np.pi**2 * L / T**2
gl, ugl, c = linearised(g, [L, T], [uL, uT])
gm, ugm, lo, hi = monte_carlo(g, [lambda r, M: L + uL * np.sqrt(3) * r.uniform(-1, 1, M),
                                  lambda r, M: T + uT * r.standard_normal(M)])
print(f"T = {T:.5f} s, u(T) = {uT:.5f} s (type A {uT_a:.5f}); L = {L:.4f} m, u(L) = {uL:.5f} m")
print(f"sensitivity coefficients dg/dL = {c[0]:.4f} 1/s^2, dg/dT = {c[1]:.4f} m/s^3")
print(f"linearised: g = {gl:.4f} m/s^2, u_c = {ugl:.4f}, expanded (k=2) U = {2*ugl:.4f}")
print(f"Monte Carlo: mean {gm:.4f}, u = {ugm:.4f}, 95 % interval [{lo:.4f}, {hi:.4f}]")
budget = (c * [uL, uT]) ** 2 / ugl**2
print(f"budget: length {budget[0]*100:.1f} %, period {budget[1]*100:.1f} %")
assert abs(ugm / ugl - 1) < 0.02 and abs(gm - gl) < 0.1 * ugl     # near-linear: methods agree

# ---------- case 2: sharp-crested weir, Q = (2/3) Cd b sqrt(2 g) h^1.5, small head ----------
Cd, uCd = 0.62, 0.02
b, ub = 0.50, type_b_rect(0.005)
h, uh = 0.040, 0.008                               # head of 40 mm read off a staff gauge, +-8 mm (1 sigma): ripples
Q = lambda Cd, b, h: 2 / 3 * Cd * b * np.sqrt(2 * 9.81) * np.clip(h, 0, None) ** 1.5
Ql, uQl, _ = linearised(Q, [Cd, b, h], [uCd, ub, uh])
Qm, uQm, qlo, qhi = monte_carlo(Q, [lambda r, M: Cd + uCd * r.standard_normal(M),
                                    lambda r, M: b + ub * np.sqrt(3) * r.uniform(-1, 1, M),
                                    lambda r, M: h + uh * r.standard_normal(M)])
print(f"weir: linearised Q = {Ql*1e3:.3f} L/s +- {2*uQl*1e3:.3f} (k=2) -> [{(Ql-2*uQl)*1e3:.3f}, {(Ql+2*uQl)*1e3:.3f}]")
print(f"weir: Monte Carlo mean {Qm*1e3:.3f} L/s, u = {uQm*1e3:.3f}, 95 % interval [{qlo*1e3:.3f}, {qhi*1e3:.3f}]")
assert Qm > Ql * 1.01                    # h^1.5 is convex: the mean is biased up
assert (qhi - Ql) > 1.15 * (Ql - qlo)     # and the interval is lopsided: linearisation misses this
```

:::exercise The budget of a density measurement
A steel cylinder has mass $m = 245.3 \pm 0.1$ g, diameter $d = 25.00 \pm 0.02$ mm and length $L = 64.00 \pm 0.05$ mm (all standard uncertainties). Find $\rho = 4m/(\pi d^2 L)$, its relative uncertainty and the biggest term in the budget.
---solution---
1. $\rho = 4 \times 0.2453/(\pi \times 0.025^2 \times 0.064) = 7808$ kg/m³.
2. Power law with exponents 1, −2, −1: $(u/\rho)^2 = (0.1/245.3)^2 + (2 \times 0.02/25)^2 + (0.05/64)^2$.
3. Terms: $(4.08\times10^{-4})^2$, $(1.60\times10^{-3})^2$, $(7.81\times10^{-4})^2$. Sum $3.34\times10^{-6}$, so $u/\rho = 0.183$ %, $u = 14$ kg/m³.
4. The diameter dominates (77 % of the budget) because it enters squared. Measure it at several positions and use a better micrometer.
:::
