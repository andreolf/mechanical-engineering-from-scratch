Almost every flow an engineer meets is turbulent, and no practical computer can resolve all its eddies. Engineering CFD solves for the **mean** flow and models what the fluctuations do to it.

## Reynolds averaging

Split each quantity into mean and fluctuation, $u = \bar u + u'$, and average the Navier-Stokes equations. One new term appears, the **Reynolds stress** $-\rho\overline{u'v'}$: momentum carried by the eddies. For a flow along a wall, the total shear stress is

$$
\tau = \rho\nu\frac{d\bar u}{dy} - \rho\overline{u'v'} .
$$

The **eddy-viscosity** idea (Boussinesq) writes $-\overline{u'v'} = \nu_t\,d\bar u/dy$, and **Prandtl's mixing length** (1925) sets $\nu_t = \ell^2|d\bar u/dy|$: eddies of size $\ell$ carry momentum across the gradient. Near a wall $\ell = \kappa y$, with $\kappa \approx 0.41$ the von Kármán constant. Van Driest's factor $1 - e^{-y^+/A^+}$, $A^+ = 26$, damps it in the viscous layer.

## Wall units and the law of the wall

With the friction velocity $u_\tau = \sqrt{\tau_w/\rho}$, define $y^+ = y u_\tau/\nu$ and $u^+ = \bar u/u_\tau$. Where the stress is still close to the wall value:

- **Viscous sublayer**, $y^+ < 5$: $u^+ = y^+$.
- **Log layer**, $30 < y^+$, $y \ll R$: $(\kappa y^+)^2 (du^+/dy^+)^2 = 1$, so $u^+ = \frac{1}{\kappa}\ln y^+ + B$, with $B \approx 5.0$ to 5.5.

In a pipe of radius $R$ the stress falls linearly to zero on the axis. Solving $(1 + \ell^{+2}\,du^+/dy^+)\,du^+/dy^+ = 1 - y^+/Re_\tau$ for $du^+/dy^+$ (a quadratic) and integrating gives the whole profile, and the bulk velocity gives the friction factor $f = 8(u_\tau/U_b)^2$. Away from the wall the code uses Nikuradse's mixing-length distribution $\ell/R = 0.14 - 0.08(1 - y/R)^2 - 0.06(1 - y/R)^4$, which behaves like $0.40\,y$ at the wall.

:::example The log law and the friction factor
1. **Sublayer.** For $y^+ < 3$, $u^+$ matches $y^+$ within 0.4 %.
2. **Law of the wall** (constant stress), fitted over $100 < y^+ < 10^4$: $u^+ = 2.4347\ln y^+ + 5.313$. The slope is $1/\kappa = 2.4390$ to within 0.2 %, and $B = 5.31$ comes out of the van Driest constant $A^+ = 26$.
3. In the pipe at $Re_\tau = 5000$ the stress drops by up to 10 % across $100 < y^+ < 500$, so a log fit there gives slope 2.56 and intercept 4.98: the log law is a property of the constant-stress layer, not of the whole pipe.
4. **Friction factor** against Prandtl's smooth-pipe law $1/\sqrt f = 2.0\log_{10}(Re\sqrt f) - 0.8$:

| $Re_\tau$ | $Re$ | $f$, mixing length | Blasius | Prandtl | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 200 | 5747 | 0.03875 | 0.03629 | 0.03594 | +7.8 % |
| 500 | 17125 | 0.02728 | 0.02762 | 0.02690 | +1.4 % |
| 1000 | 38032 | 0.02212 | 0.02263 | 0.02223 | −0.5 % |
| 2000 | 83312 | 0.01844 | 0.01860 | 0.01870 | −1.4 % |
| 5000 | 231664 | 0.01491 | 0.01440 | 0.01520 | −1.9 % |

A one-line algebraic model predicts pipe friction within 2 % from $Re \approx 17000$ up. It fails near transition ($Re_\tau = 200$), where turbulence is not in equilibrium, and it cannot handle separation or recirculation. That is the job of transport-equation models such as $k$-$\varepsilon$ and $k$-$\omega$ SST.
:::

```python file=mixlength.py
"""Turbulent pipe flow from Prandtl's mixing length with van Driest damping: the mean
velocity profile in wall units, the viscous sublayer, the log law, and the friction factor
against Blasius and Prandtl's smooth-pipe law. numpy only."""
import numpy as np

kappa, Aplus = 0.41, 26.0

def profile(Re_tau, n=4000, const_stress=False):
    """u+(y+) across a pipe of radius Re_tau in wall units. The total shear stress falls
    linearly from the wall to zero on the axis: (1 + nu_t+) du+/dy+ = 1 - y+/Re_tau.
    const_stress=True gives the inner layer alone (stress = wall stress), the law of the wall."""
    yp = np.concatenate([[0], np.geomspace(1e-3, Re_tau, n)])
    lp = kappa * yp * (1 - np.exp(-yp / Aplus))                 # mixing length, wall units
    if const_stress:
        tau = np.ones_like(yp)
    else:
        eta = 1 - yp / Re_tau                                   # r / R
        lp = Re_tau * (0.14 - 0.08 * eta**2 - 0.06 * eta**4) * (1 - np.exp(-yp / Aplus))   # Nikuradse's l/R, van Driest damped
        tau = 1 - yp / Re_tau
    dudy = 2 * tau / (1 + np.sqrt(1 + 4 * lp**2 * tau))          # root of l^2 (du/dy)^2 + du/dy - tau = 0
    up = np.concatenate([[0], np.cumsum(0.5 * (dudy[1:] + dudy[:-1]) * np.diff(yp))])
    return yp, up

def bulk(Re_tau):
    yp, up = profile(Re_tau)
    r = Re_tau - yp                                              # radius in wall units
    Ub = 2 / Re_tau**2 * np.sum(0.5 * (up[1:] * r[1:] + up[:-1] * r[:-1]) * -np.diff(r))   # 2/R^2 int u r dr
    Re = 2 * Re_tau * Ub                                         # Re = Ub D / nu
    f = 8 / Ub**2                                                # Darcy f = 8 (u_tau / Ub)^2
    return Re, f, Ub, yp, up

Re_tau = 5000
Re, f, Ub, yp, up = bulk(Re_tau)
sub = yp < 3
print(f"Re_tau = {Re_tau}: bulk U+ = {Ub:.3f}, Re = {Re:.0f}; viscous sublayer: max |u+ - y+| / y+ for y+ < 3 is {np.max(abs(up[sub][1:] - yp[sub][1:]) / yp[sub][1:]):.4f}")
yw, uw = profile(1e4, const_stress=True)
m = (yw > 100) & (yw < 1e4)
slope, B = np.polyfit(np.log(yw[m]), uw[m], 1)
print(f"law of the wall (constant stress), fit for 100 < y+ < 10000: u+ = {slope:.4f} ln y+ + {B:.3f}  (1/kappa = {1/kappa:.4f}; the usual B is 5.0 to 5.5)")
m = (yp > 100) & (yp < 0.1 * Re_tau)
s2, B2 = np.polyfit(np.log(yp[m]), up[m], 1)
print(f"in the pipe itself the stress falls by up to 10 % over 100 < y+ < {0.1*Re_tau:.0f}: slope {s2:.4f}, intercept {B2:.3f}")
assert np.max(abs(up[sub][1:] - yp[sub][1:]) / yp[sub][1:]) < 0.01
assert abs(slope - 1 / kappa) < 0.01 and 4.8 < B < 5.8

print(" Re_tau      Re     f (mixing length)  Blasius   Prandtl smooth law")
for Rt in (200, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000):
    Re, f, *_ = bulk(Rt)
    fb = 0.316 * Re ** -0.25
    fp = 0.02
    for _ in range(50):
        fp = (2.0 * np.log10(Re * np.sqrt(fp)) - 0.8) ** -2
    print(f"{Rt:6d} {Re:8.0f}     {f:.5f}         {fb:.5f}   {fp:.5f}   ({(f/fp-1)*100:+.1f} % vs Prandtl)")
    assert abs(f / fp - 1) < (0.03 if Rt >= 500 else 0.1)
```

:::exercise Wall units in a river
A river flows with $u_\tau = 0.05$ m/s ($\nu = 10^{-6}$ m²/s). How thick is the viscous sublayer, and where does the log layer start?
---solution---
1. $y = y^+\nu/u_\tau$, and $\nu/u_\tau = 2\times10^{-5}$ m.
2. Viscous sublayer: $y^+ < 5$, so $y < 0.1$ mm.
3. Log layer from $y^+ = 30$, $y = 0.6$ mm. Practically the whole depth of the river is log layer and outer flow.
:::
