Stand on the Mittlere Brücke in Basel and watch the Rhine: boils rise and spread, swirls of every size appear and vanish. That's **turbulence**, the normal state of almost every flow around us. It is chaotic, three-dimensional and rotational, and it mixes far faster than molecular diffusion. Nobody can predict each eddy, but its statistics follow simple laws.

## The energy cascade

Turbulence takes energy from the mean flow at the scale of the largest eddies, size $L$ and velocity $u'$, comparable to the river depth and a tenth of the mean speed. Large eddies are unstable; they break into smaller ones, which break again, passing energy down a **cascade** with little loss. Only at the smallest scales does viscosity turn the motion into heat. In a steady state the energy flux through the cascade equals the **dissipation rate** $\varepsilon$ (W/kg), set by the large eddies:

$$
\varepsilon \sim \frac{u'^3}{L} .
$$

Viscosity plays no part in that estimate. That's why drag on bluff bodies hardly depends on Re.

## Kolmogorov scales

In 1941 Kolmogorov argued that the smallest eddies depend only on $\nu$ and $\varepsilon$. Dimensional analysis then fixes their size, time and speed:

$$
\eta = \left(\frac{\nu^3}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/4}, \qquad \tau_\eta = \left(\frac\nu\varepsilon\right)^{1/2}, \qquad u_\eta = (\nu\varepsilon)^{1/4} .
$$

Their Reynolds number $\eta u_\eta/\nu$ is exactly 1: viscous and inertial effects balance. The range of scales is $L/\eta \sim \mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4}$, which is why simulating every eddy (DNS) costs about $\mathrm{Re}^3$.

Between $L$ and $\eta$ lies the **inertial subrange**, where only $\varepsilon$ matters. There the energy spectrum must be

$$
E(k) = C\,\varepsilon^{2/3}k^{-5/3}, \qquad C \approx 1.5 ,
$$

the famous **−5/3 law**, measured in rivers, wind tunnels, the atmosphere and the ocean.

:::example Eddies in the Rhine
Take the Rhine near Basel: depth 6 m sets $L = 6$ m, velocity fluctuations $u' = 0.2$ m/s, $\nu = 10^{-6}$ m²/s.

1. $\varepsilon \sim 0.2^3/6 = 0.001333$ W/kg.
2. Kolmogorov length $\eta = 0.1655$ mm, time 27.39 ms, velocity 6.043 mm/s. Their Reynolds number is exactly 1.
3. $L/\eta = 36\,257 = \mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4}$ with $\mathrm{Re}_L = 1.2\times10^6$.
4. A synthetic 65 536-sample velocity record at 1 kHz, built from random Fourier modes with a −5/3 target, gives a fitted spectral slope of −1.6447 between 1 and 100 Hz.
:::

```python file=kolmogorov.py
"""Turbulence phenomenology: Kolmogorov scales, and a synthetic signal with a -5/3 spectrum analysed by FFT."""
import math
import numpy as np


def kolmogorov(nu, eps):
    eta = (nu ** 3 / eps) ** 0.25
    tau = (nu / eps) ** 0.5
    u = (nu * eps) ** 0.25
    return eta, tau, u


def synthetic_signal(n, dt, slope=-5 / 3, f0=0.05, seed=1):
    """Random Gaussian Fourier coefficients whose expected power falls as f^slope above f0."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    f = np.fft.rfftfreq(n, dt)
    S = np.zeros_like(f); S[1:] = (1 + (f[1:] / f0) ** 2) ** (slope / 2)
    coef = np.sqrt(S / 2) * (rng.standard_normal(len(f)) + 1j * rng.standard_normal(len(f)))
    u = np.fft.irfft(coef, n)
    return u / u.std()


def spectrum_slope(u, dt, fmin, fmax):
    f = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(u), dt)
    P = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(u)) ** 2
    sel = (f >= fmin) & (f <= fmax)
    return np.polyfit(np.log(f[sel]), np.log(P[sel]), 1)[0]


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # the Rhine near Basel: 2 m/s, 6 m deep; large eddies ~ depth, velocity fluctuation ~ 10 % of the mean
    nu = 1.0e-6; u_rms = 0.2; L = 6.0
    eps = u_rms ** 3 / L
    eta, tau, ueta = kolmogorov(nu, eps)
    print(f"dissipation eps ~ u'^3/L = {eps:.6f} W/kg")
    print(f"Kolmogorov length {eta*1000:.4f} mm, time {tau*1000:.2f} ms, velocity {ueta*1000:.3f} mm/s")
    assert abs(eta * ueta / nu - 1) < 1e-12                    # the Reynolds number of the smallest eddies is 1
    print(f"scale separation L/eta = {L/eta:.0f} = Re_L^(3/4) = {(u_rms*L/nu)**0.75:.0f}")
    assert abs(L / eta - (u_rms * L / nu) ** 0.75) < 1e-6 * L / eta
    # synthetic velocity record at 1 kHz
    dt = 1e-3; u = synthetic_signal(2 ** 16, dt)
    s = spectrum_slope(u, dt, 1.0, 100.0)
    print(f"synthetic record: mean {u.mean():.2e}, rms {u.std():.3f}, fitted spectral slope {s:.4f} (-5/3 = {-5/3:.4f})")
    assert abs(s + 5 / 3) < 0.05
```

:::exercise A kitchen blender
A blender puts 300 W into 1 kg of water. Estimate the Kolmogorov length, time and velocity. How does $\eta$ compare with the Rhine?
---solution---
1. $\varepsilon = 300$ W/kg.
2. $\eta = (10^{-18}/300)^{1/4} = 7.60\times10^{-6}$ m, about 7.6 µm.
3. $\tau_\eta = (10^{-6}/300)^{1/2} = 5.77\times10^{-5}$ s, and $u_\eta = (10^{-6}\cdot300)^{1/4} = 0.1316$ m/s.
4. The blender's smallest eddies are 22 times smaller than the Rhine's. That's why it can break up droplets into an emulsion.
:::
