When a factory upstream on the Limmat spills a pollutant, or hydrologists pour in a fluorescent dye to measure the flow, the question is the same: how fast does the cloud travel, how wide does it spread, and how concentrated will it be at the waterworks downstream? The answer comes from the **advection-dispersion equation**, the simplest model of mixing, and it rests on one exact property: whatever goes in stays in.

## Advection and diffusion

A concentration $c(x,t)$ carried by a current $U$ and spread by a diffusivity $D$ obeys

$$
\frac{\partial c}{\partial t} + U\frac{\partial c}{\partial x} = D\frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2} .
$$

The first two terms move the cloud downstream; the right side spreads it. In conservation form, $\partial c/\partial t + \partial F/\partial x = 0$ with flux $F = Uc - D\,\partial c/\partial x$: the change in a stretch of river equals what flows in minus what flows out. Integrated over the whole river, the total mass $M = A\int c\,dx$ never changes.

## Dispersion in a river

Molecular diffusion is far too slow to matter. Turbulence mixes across the river, and the velocity difference between fast mid-channel water and slow water near the banks stretches the cloud lengthwise. Taylor showed that, once the cloud is mixed across the section, the combination acts like a much larger **longitudinal dispersion coefficient**. Fischer's estimate:

$$
D_L \approx 0.011\,\frac{U^2W^2}{h\,u_*} ,
$$

with width $W$, depth $h$ and shear velocity $u_*$. For an instantaneous release of mass $M$ into a river of cross-section $A$, the solution is a Gaussian that moves and spreads:

$$
c(x,t) = \frac{M}{A\sqrt{4\pi D_L t}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - Ut)^2}{4D_L t}\right) .
$$

The width grows as $\sqrt{t}$ and the peak falls as $1/\sqrt t$. The area under the curve stays the same.

:::example A dye test below Zürich
The Limmat: 40 m wide, 2.5 m deep, 1 m/s, $u_* = 0.08$ m/s, so $A = 100$ m².

1. Fischer: $D_L = 0.011\cdot1\cdot1600/(2.5\cdot0.08) = 88.0$ m²/s.
2. 10 kg of dye: after 500 s the cloud is centred 0.5 km downstream with a peak of 134.48 µg/L and a 4σ width of 1187 m; after 2000 s, 2.0 km, 67.24 µg/L, 2373 m; after 8000 s, 8.0 km, 33.62 µg/L, 4746 m. Four times the time halves the peak and doubles the width.
3. Conservative finite volumes (limited upwind advection, central diffusion, 2000 cells over 20 km), started from the Gaussian at 500 s: after 8000 s the mass on the grid is 9.996257366825 kg, identical to the start to 12 digits. (The 0.004 kg short of 10 kg is the tail upstream of the grid at the start.) The peak is 33.607 µg/L against the exact 33.621, and the largest error anywhere is 0.05% of the peak.
:::

```python file=plume.py
"""A dye slug in the Limmat: 1D advection-dispersion, finite volumes against the Gaussian solution, with mass conservation."""
import math
import numpy as np


def gaussian(x, t, M, A, U, D, x0=0.0):
    return M / (A * math.sqrt(4 * math.pi * D * t)) * np.exp(-(x - x0 - U * t) ** 2 / (4 * D * t))


def simulate(M, A, U, D, L, n, t_end, x0, t0):
    """Conservative finite volumes: upwind advection (U > 0) with a second-order flux limiter, central diffusion,
    zero flux at both ends. Starts from the Gaussian at time t0 so that the initial condition is smooth."""
    dx = L / n; x = (np.arange(n) + 0.5) * dx
    c = gaussian(x, t0, M, A, U, D, x0)
    dt = 0.4 * min(dx / U, dx * dx / (2 * D)); steps = int(math.ceil((t_end - t0) / dt)); dt = (t_end - t0) / steps
    for _ in range(steps):
        # face fluxes F[i] between cells i-1 and i (F[0] and F[n] are the closed ends)
        dc = np.diff(c)
        r = np.zeros(n - 1); r[1:] = dc[:-1] / np.where(np.abs(dc[1:]) > 1e-300, dc[1:], 1e-300)
        phi = np.maximum(0, np.minimum(1, r))                        # minmod limiter
        cface = c[:-1] + 0.5 * phi * dc * (1 - U * dt / dx)
        F = np.zeros(n + 1)
        F[1:-1] = U * cface - D * dc / dx
        c = c - dt / dx * (F[1:] - F[:-1])
    return x, c, dx


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Limmat below Zurich: 40 m wide, 2.5 m deep, 1 m/s, shear velocity 0.08 m/s
    W, h, U, ustar = 40.0, 2.5, 1.0, 0.08
    A = W * h
    D = 0.011 * U ** 2 * W ** 2 / (h * ustar)                      # Fischer's longitudinal dispersion estimate
    print(f"longitudinal dispersion D = {D:.1f} m^2/s")
    M = 10.0                                                      # 10 kg of tracer dye
    for t in (500.0, 2000.0, 8000.0):
        cmax = M / (A * math.sqrt(4 * math.pi * D * t))
        print(f"t = {t:6.0f} s: cloud centre {U*t/1000:.1f} km downstream, peak {cmax*1e6:.2f} mg/m^3 (ug/L), width 4 sigma = {4*math.sqrt(2*D*t):.0f} m")
    x, c, dx = simulate(M, A, U, D, L=20000.0, n=2000, t_end=8000.0, x0=500.0, t0=500.0)
    mass = c.sum() * dx * A
    exact = gaussian(x, 8000.0, M, A, U, D, 500.0)
    print(f"finite volumes after 8000 s: mass {mass:.12f} kg, peak {c.max()*1e6:.3f} vs Gaussian {exact.max()*1e6:.3f} ug/L, "
          f"max error {np.abs(c-exact).max()/exact.max()*100:.2f} % of the peak")
    m0 = gaussian(x, 500.0, M, A, U, D, 500.0).sum() * dx * A
    assert abs(mass - m0) < 1e-12 * M                            # total mass conserved to rounding
    assert np.abs(c - exact).max() < 0.005 * exact.max()          # and the shape matches the Gaussian (domain long enough that no dye reaches the ends)
    print(f"initial mass on the grid {m0:.12f} kg")
```

:::exercise The waterworks intake
A 25 kg spill enters the same river 6 km above a waterworks intake. When does the peak arrive, how concentrated is it, and for how long is the concentration above half the peak?
---solution---
1. Arrival: $t = 6000/1 = 6000$ s, 100 minutes.
2. Peak: $25/(100\sqrt{4\pi\cdot88\cdot6000}) = 25/(100\cdot2575.9) = 9.705\times10^{-5}$ kg/m³, about 97.06 µg/L.
3. Above half the peak while $(x - Ut)^2 < 4D_Lt\ln2$: a spatial width of $2\sqrt{4\cdot88\cdot6000\cdot\ln2} = 2\cdot1209.9 = 2420$ m, which passes the intake in about 2420 s, roughly 40 minutes. (The cloud also keeps spreading as it passes, so this is a good first estimate.)
:::
