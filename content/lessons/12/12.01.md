The Navier-Stokes equations rarely have exact solutions, but a handful exist, and they teach a lot. When the flow is parallel, the nonlinear convection term vanishes and the equations become linear. Phase 11 solved two steady cases, Couette and Poiseuille flow. This lesson adds a time-dependent one: a plate suddenly set in motion. It shows how viscosity spreads momentum, and why the layer it affects grows as $\sqrt{\nu t}$.

## Parallel flows

Take $\mathbf u = (u(y,t), 0, 0)$. Continuity, $\partial u/\partial x = 0$, holds automatically, and $(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u = u\,\partial u/\partial x = 0$. The $x$-momentum equation reduces to

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -\frac1\rho\frac{\partial p}{\partial x} + \nu\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} .
$$

- Steady, no pressure gradient, moving top wall: **Couette**, a linear profile.
- Steady, constant pressure gradient, fixed walls: **Poiseuille**, a parabola.
- Unsteady, no pressure gradient: a **diffusion equation** for momentum, with $\nu$ as the diffusivity.

## Stokes' first problem

Fluid lies at rest above a plate that is suddenly set moving at speed $U$ at $t = 0$. The boundary conditions are $u(0,t) = U$ and $u \to 0$ far away. There's no length scale in the problem except $\sqrt{\nu t}$, so the solution must depend only on $\eta = y/(2\sqrt{\nu t})$. The PDE becomes an ODE, $u'' + 2\eta u' = 0$, with the solution

$$
\frac{u}{U} = \operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{y}{2\sqrt{\nu t}}\right) .
$$

The velocity falls to 1% of $U$ where $\operatorname{erfc}(\eta) = 0.01$, at $\eta = 1.8214$. So the layer of moving fluid is

$$
\delta_{99} = 3.643\sqrt{\nu t} .
$$

Four times the time gives twice the thickness. The wall shear stress is $\tau_w = \mu U/\sqrt{\pi\nu t}$: large at first, then dying away as the gradient spreads out. The same $\sqrt{\nu t}$ law sets the thickness of boundary layers (lesson 12.03) and, with thermal diffusivity in place of $\nu$, how deep heat soaks into a wall.

:::example Water above a jerked plate
Water ($\nu = 10^{-6}$ m²/s) above a plate set moving at 0.5 m/s. Explicit finite differences on 400 cells over 6 cm, compared with the erfc solution:

1. At t = 2.5, 10 and 40 s the largest error is $2.3\times10^{-4}$, $5.8\times10^{-5}$ and $1.4\times10^{-5}$ m/s.
2. The 99% layer is 5.759, 11.519 and 23.039 mm, against $3.643\sqrt{\nu t}$ = 5.760, 11.520 and 23.040 mm.
3. Each fourfold increase in time doubles the layer: ratios 2.0000 and 2.0001.
4. Wall shear at 10 s: $\tau_w = 10^{-3}\cdot0.5/\sqrt{\pi\cdot10^{-5}} = 0.0892$ Pa.
:::

```python file=stokes1.py
"""Stokes' first problem: a plate suddenly set moving under still fluid. Finite differences versus the erfc solution."""
import math
import numpy as np


def exact(y, t, U, nu):
    return U * np.array([math.erfc(yy / (2 * math.sqrt(nu * t))) for yy in np.atleast_1d(y)])


def simulate(U, nu, t_end, H, n=400):
    """Explicit FTCS for u_t = nu u_yy on 0 <= y <= H, u(0) = U, u(H) = 0."""
    y = np.linspace(0, H, n + 1); dy = y[1]; dt = 0.4 * dy * dy / nu
    steps = int(math.ceil(t_end / dt)); dt = t_end / steps
    u = np.zeros(n + 1); u[0] = U
    r = nu * dt / dy ** 2
    for _ in range(steps):
        u[1:-1] = u[1:-1] + r * (u[2:] - 2 * u[1:-1] + u[:-2])
    return y, u


def thickness99(y, u, U):
    """Depth where the velocity has fallen to 1 % of the plate speed (linear interpolation)."""
    i = np.argmax(u < 0.01 * U)
    return y[i - 1] + (0.01 * U - u[i - 1]) * (y[i] - y[i - 1]) / (u[i] - u[i - 1])


if __name__ == "__main__":
    U, nu = 0.5, 1.0e-6                     # water above a plate jerked to 0.5 m/s
    deltas = []
    for t in (2.5, 10.0, 40.0):
        y, u = simulate(U, nu, t, H=0.06)
        err = np.max(np.abs(u - exact(y, t, U, nu)))
        d = thickness99(y, u, U); deltas.append(d)
        print(f"t = {t:5.1f} s: max error vs erfc {err:.2e} m/s, 99 % layer {d*1000:.3f} mm, 3.643 sqrt(nu t) = {3.643*math.sqrt(nu*t)*1000:.3f} mm")
        assert err < 2e-3
    print(f"ratios for 4x the time: {deltas[1]/deltas[0]:.4f}, {deltas[2]/deltas[1]:.4f}")
    assert all(abs(deltas[i + 1] / deltas[i] - 2) < 0.01 for i in range(2))    # the layer grows as sqrt(nu t)
    # the exact 1 % point: erfc(eta) = 0.01 at eta = 1.8214, so delta = 2 * 1.8214 sqrt(nu t)
    lo, hi = 0.0, 5.0
    for _ in range(100):
        m = (lo + hi) / 2
        lo, hi = (m, hi) if math.erfc(m) > 0.01 else (lo, m)
    print(f"erfc(eta) = 0.01 at eta = {lo:.4f}: delta99 = {2*lo:.4f} sqrt(nu t)")
    mu = 1000 * nu                          # dynamic viscosity of water, Pa s
    print(f"wall shear at t = 10 s: mu U / sqrt(pi nu t) = {mu*U/math.sqrt(math.pi*nu*10):.4f} Pa")
```

:::exercise Honey on a spoon
Honey has $\nu \approx 7\times10^{-3}$ m²/s. How thick is the moving layer 1 s and 10 s after a plate starts moving under it? How long does water take to build a layer of the same thickness as honey does in 1 s?
---solution---
1. $\delta_{99}(1\text{ s}) = 3.643\sqrt{7\times10^{-3}} = 0.3048$ m.
2. $\delta_{99}(10\text{ s}) = 3.643\sqrt{7\times10^{-2}} = 0.9638$ m: the whole jar moves almost at once.
3. Water needs $\nu t$ equal: $t = 7\times10^{-3}/10^{-6} = 7000$ s, nearly 2 hours.
:::
