Work is force integrated over distance; mass is density integrated over volume; impulse is force integrated over time. When the integrand is a measurement or a simulation output, you integrate by adding up thin slices. This lesson defines the integral that way, shows why it equals an antiderivative difference, and measures how fast the slices converge.

## Area as a limit of sums

Split $[a, b]$ into $n$ slices of width $h = (b - a)/n$ and pick a point $x_i^*$ in each. The **Riemann sum** is

$$
S_n = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} f(x_i^*)\,h .
$$

If $S_n$ approaches the same limit for every choice of points as $h \to 0$, that limit is the integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$. Every continuous function on a closed interval is integrable in this sense.

Three common choices: the **left** end ($x_i^* = a + ih$), the **right** end, and the **midpoint** ($x_i^* = a + (i + \tfrac12)h$).

## The fundamental theorem of calculus

If $F' = f$ on $[a, b]$, then

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) .
$$

The reason, in one line: on each slice the mean value theorem gives $F(x_{i+1}) - F(x_i) = f(\xi_i)h$ for some $\xi_i$ in the slice, and those terms telescope to $F(b) - F(a)$. So the antiderivative difference is itself a Riemann sum, for a clever choice of points, at every $n$.

## How fast do the sums converge?

Taylor-expand $f$ around each slice's left end. The left and right sums are off by about $\tfrac12 f'h$ per unit length, so their error is $O(h) = O(1/n)$. For the midpoint the linear term cancels by symmetry, leaving

$$
\int_a^b f\,dx - M_n = \frac{(b - a)h^2}{24} f''(\xi) = O\!\left(\frac{1}{n^2}\right).
$$

Doubling the slices quarters the midpoint error. Same cost, much better answer.

:::widget riemann
Rectangles under a curve. Switch between left, midpoint and right and watch the error; doubling the rectangle count halves the left-rule error and quarters the midpoint error.
:::

:::example Work to compress air in a cylinder
Air at $p_1 = 1\ \mathrm{bar}$ fills $V_1 = 1\ \mathrm{L}$ and is compressed slowly (isothermally) to $V_2 = 0.5\ \mathrm{L}$, so $p = p_1V_1/V$. The work done on the gas is

$$
W = \int_{V_2}^{V_1} p\,dV = p_1 V_1 \ln\frac{V_1}{V_2} = 10^5 \times 10^{-3} \times \ln 2 = 69.31\ \mathrm{J}.
$$

With only four slices of $0.125\ \mathrm{L}$:

1. Left sum: $75.95\ \mathrm{J}$ (error $+6.64$)
2. Midpoint sum: $69.12\ \mathrm{J}$ (error $-0.19$)

The midpoint rule is 34 times more accurate for the same four pressure evaluations.
:::

```python file=riemann.py
"""Left, right and midpoint Riemann sums and their convergence rates."""
import math


def riemann(f, a, b, n, rule="mid"):
    h = (b - a) / n
    off = {"left": 0.0, "mid": 0.5, "right": 1.0}[rule]
    return h * sum(f(a + (i + off) * h) for i in range(n))


def rate(f, a, b, exact, rule, ns=(8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256)):
    """Observed order p in error ~ n^-p, from successive doublings."""
    e = [abs(riemann(f, a, b, n, rule) - exact) for n in ns]
    return sum(math.log2(e[i] / e[i + 1]) for i in range(len(e) - 1)) / (len(e) - 1)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    p1, V1, V2 = 1e5, 1e-3, 0.5e-3
    p = lambda V: p1 * V1 / V
    W = p1 * V1 * math.log(V1 / V2)
    left, mid = riemann(p, V2, V1, 4, "left"), riemann(p, V2, V1, 4, "mid")
    print(f"exact {W:.4f} J, left {left:.4f}, mid {mid:.4f}")
    assert abs(W - 69.3147) < 1e-4 and abs(left - 75.9524) < 1e-4 and abs(mid - 69.1220) < 1e-4
    r_left, r_mid = rate(p, V2, V1, W, "left"), rate(p, V2, V1, W, "mid")
    print(f"observed order: left {r_left:.3f}, midpoint {r_mid:.3f}")
    assert abs(r_left - 1) < 0.05 and abs(r_mid - 2) < 0.02   # midpoint is O(1/n^2)
```

:::exercise Impulse from a force record
A load cell records the force of a hammer blow every $0.5\ \mathrm{ms}$: $0, 120, 380, 520, 410, 190, 40\ \mathrm{N}$. The samples are slice midpoints of seven $0.5\ \mathrm{ms}$ slices. Estimate the impulse.
---solution---
1. Impulse is $J = \int F\,dt$; with midpoint samples, $J \approx h\sum F_i$.
2. Sum: $0 + 120 + 380 + 520 + 410 + 190 + 40 = 1660\ \mathrm{N}$.
3. $J \approx 0.5\times10^{-3} \times 1660 = 0.83\ \mathrm{N\,s}$.
4. For a $0.5\ \mathrm{kg}$ hammer head that's a velocity change of $1.66\ \mathrm{m/s}$, a plausible tap.
:::
