At Neuhausen, the Rhine drops about 23 metres over the Rhine Falls, Europe's most powerful waterfall by flow. Where does a parcel of water leaving the lip come down, and how fast is it moving when it does? This capstone for Phase 01 uses everything so far: a free-body diagram, a pair of differential equations, an exact solution, a numerical check and an honest statement of what the model leaves out.

## The model and its stated simplification

Treat a parcel of water as a point mass leaving the lip horizontally at speed $v_0$. Ignore air drag first; that's a **stated simplification**, and the second half of the lesson removes it. The only force is gravity, so Newton's second law in components is

$$
\ddot x = 0, \qquad \ddot y = -g .
$$

Integrate twice with $x(0) = 0$, $y(0) = H$, $\dot x(0) = v_0\cos\theta$, $\dot y(0) = v_0\sin\theta$:

$$
x(t) = v_0\cos\theta\; t, \qquad y(t) = H + v_0\sin\theta\; t - \tfrac12 g t^2 .
$$

On flat ground ($H = 0$) the parcel lands when $y = 0$ at $t = 2v_0\sin\theta/g$, and the range is

$$
R = \frac{v_0^2 \sin 2\theta}{g},
$$

the textbook result, largest at $\theta = 45^\circ$.

## Adding linear drag

A small droplet in air feels a drag force roughly proportional to its velocity, $\mathbf{F} = -mk\mathbf{v}$. The equations stay linear and decouple:

$$
\dot v_x = -k v_x, \qquad \dot v_y = -g - k v_y .
$$

Each is a first-order linear ODE (lesson 01.15). Solving and integrating once more:

$$
x(t) = \frac{v_{x0}}{k}\left(1 - e^{-kt}\right), \qquad y(t) = H + \frac{1}{k}\left(v_{y0} + \frac{g}{k}\right)\left(1 - e^{-kt}\right) - \frac{g}{k}t .
$$

Horizontal travel can never exceed $v_{x0}/k$, and the fall speed approaches the terminal value $g/k$. As $k \to 0$, Taylor-expanding $e^{-kt}$ (lesson 01.07) recovers the drag-free formulas, which is a good check on the algebra.

:::example Where the water lands
Take $H = 23\ \mathrm{m}$ and water leaving the lip horizontally at $v_0 = 5\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

1. Fall time without drag: $t = \sqrt{2H/g} = \sqrt{46/9.81} = 2.165\ \mathrm{s}$.
2. Horizontal distance: $x = 5 \times 2.165 = 10.8\ \mathrm{m}$ beyond the lip.
3. Impact speed: $\sqrt{v_0^2 + 2gH} = \sqrt{25 + 451.3} = 21.8\ \mathrm{m/s}$, about $79\ \mathrm{km/h}$.
4. With linear drag $k = 0.3\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ (a fine spray droplet), solving $y(t) = 0$ numerically gives $t = 2.43\ \mathrm{s}$ and $x = 8.6\ \mathrm{m}$.

Real waterfall flow is a sheet that breaks up into jets and drops, entrains air and is slowed by quadratic drag. The point-mass model gives the right scale, about 10 m out at about 20 m/s; it can't tell you the shape of the spray cloud. Saying that out loud is part of the answer.
:::

```python file=rhine_falls.py
"""Projectile from the Rhine Falls lip: closed form without and with linear drag, plus RK4 check."""
import math

G = 9.81


def no_drag(v0, theta, H, t):
    return v0 * math.cos(theta) * t, H + v0 * math.sin(theta) * t - 0.5 * G * t * t


def linear_drag(v0, theta, H, k, t):
    vx, vy = v0 * math.cos(theta), v0 * math.sin(theta)
    e = 1 - math.exp(-k * t)
    return vx / k * e, H + (vy + G / k) / k * e - G / k * t


def landing(traj, t_hi=60.0):
    """Bisection on y(t) = 0 (lesson 01.02)."""
    lo, hi = 0.0, t_hi
    for _ in range(100):
        m = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        lo, hi = (m, hi) if traj(m)[1] > 0 else (lo, m)
    return lo, traj(lo)[0]


def rk4_drag(v0, theta, H, k, dt=1e-3):
    s = [0.0, H, v0 * math.cos(theta), v0 * math.sin(theta)]
    f = lambda s: [s[2], s[3], -k * s[2], -G - k * s[3]]
    t = 0.0
    while s[1] > 0:
        prev = s
        k1 = f(s); k2 = f([a + dt / 2 * b for a, b in zip(s, k1)])
        k3 = f([a + dt / 2 * b for a, b in zip(s, k2)]); k4 = f([a + dt * b for a, b in zip(s, k3)])
        s = [a + dt / 6 * (b + 2 * c + 2 * d + e) for a, b, c, d, e in zip(s, k1, k2, k3, k4)]
        t += dt
    w = prev[1] / (prev[1] - s[1])                     # interpolate to y = 0
    return prev[0] + w * (s[0] - prev[0])


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # the lesson's test: no-drag range on flat ground matches v^2 sin(2 theta)/g
    for v0, deg in [(20, 30), (12, 45), (8, 70)]:
        th = math.radians(deg)
        _, R = landing(lambda t: no_drag(v0, th, 1e-12, t))
        assert abs(R - v0**2 * math.sin(2 * th) / G) < 1e-6
    t, x = landing(lambda t: no_drag(5.0, 0.0, 23.0, t))
    print(f"no drag: lands after {t:.3f} s, {x:.2f} m out, at {math.hypot(5, G * t):.1f} m/s")
    assert abs(t - 2.1654) < 1e-3 and abs(x - 10.827) < 1e-2
    td, xd = landing(lambda t: linear_drag(5.0, 0.0, 23.0, 0.3, t))
    print(f"linear drag k=0.3: {td:.3f} s, {xd:.2f} m out")
    assert abs(xd - 8.621) < 1e-2 and abs(rk4_drag(5.0, 0.0, 23.0, 0.3) - xd) < 1e-6
    # small k recovers the drag-free answer
    assert abs(landing(lambda t: linear_drag(5.0, 0.0, 23.0, 1e-6, t))[1] - x) < 1e-3
```

:::exercise A fountain on flat ground
A jet leaves a fountain nozzle at ground level at $20\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $30^\circ$ above horizontal. Ignoring drag, how far away does it land, how high does it rise, and what angle would maximise the range?
---solution---
1. Range: $R = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin2\theta}{g} = \dfrac{400 \times \sin60^\circ}{9.81} = \dfrac{346.4}{9.81} = 35.3\ \mathrm{m}$.
2. Peak height: vertical speed $v_0\sin\theta = 10\ \mathrm{m/s}$ drops to zero, so $h = \dfrac{10^2}{2 \times 9.81} = 5.10\ \mathrm{m}$.
3. Maximum range at $\theta = 45^\circ$: $R_\text{max} = 400/9.81 = 40.8\ \mathrm{m}$.
4. With real drag the best angle is a few degrees lower than $45^\circ$, because time in the air costs more horizontal speed.
:::
