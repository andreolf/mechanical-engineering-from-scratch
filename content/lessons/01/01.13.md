Some of the most useful integrals in engineering run to infinity or through a singularity: the energy needed to escape Earth's gravity, the stress near a crack tip, the pressure under a point load. These are improper integrals. They have finite values when the integrand dies off, or blows up, slowly enough, and you can compute them reliably once you change variables to remove the trouble.

## Two kinds of improper

An integral over an infinite range is defined as a limit:

$$
\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx = \lim_{R\to\infty}\int_a^R f(x)\,dx .
$$

So is an integral whose integrand is unbounded at an end point, say at $x = 0$:

$$
\int_0^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{\delta\to 0^+}\int_\delta^b f(x)\,dx .
$$

If the limit exists and is finite, the integral **converges**.

## The $p$-test

The benchmark family is $1/x^p$:

$$
\int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{x^p} = \frac{1}{p - 1} \;\;(p > 1), \qquad \int_0^1 \frac{dx}{x^p} = \frac{1}{1 - p} \;\;(p < 1) .
$$

Both diverge at $p = 1$, where the antiderivative is $\ln x$. Tails must die faster than $1/x$; singularities must be milder than $1/x$. To test any other integrand, compare it with a suitable $1/x^p$.

## Removing the trouble by substitution

A computer can't sample to infinity or at a singularity, so change variables first:

- **Infinite range**: $x = 1/t$ maps $[1, \infty)$ to $(0, 1]$, with $dx = -dt/t^2$. Then $\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx = \int_0^1 f(1/t)\,t^{-2}\,dt$, which is often perfectly smooth.
- **Square-root singularity**: $x = u^2$ turns $\int_0^1 g(x)/\sqrt{x}\,dx$ into $\int_0^1 2g(u^2)\,du$, with no singularity at all.

After the substitution, Simpson's rule from lesson 01.10 works at full fourth-order speed.

:::example Escape speed from Earth
The work to lift a mass $m$ from Earth's surface $R$ to infinity against gravity $GMm/r^2$ is

$$
W = \int_R^\infty \frac{GMm}{r^2}\,dr = GMm\left[-\frac{1}{r}\right]_R^\infty = \frac{GMm}{R} .
$$

The integral converges because the force dies off like $1/r^2$, with $p = 2 > 1$.

1. With $GM = 3.986\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}$ and $R = 6.371\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m}$: $W/m = 6.26\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{J/kg}$.
2. Setting kinetic energy equal to that: $\tfrac12 v^2 = GM/R$, so $v = \sqrt{2GM/R} = 11.19\ \mathrm{km/s}$.

An infinite distance costs finite energy: $62.6\ \mathrm{MJ}$ per kilogram, about the chemical energy in 1.4 kg of diesel (at roughly $45\ \mathrm{MJ/kg}$).
:::

```python file=improper.py
"""Improper integrals made proper by substitution, then integrated with Simpson's rule."""
import math


def simpson(f, a, b, n=200):
    h = (b - a) / n
    s = f(a) + f(b) + 4 * sum(f(a + i * h) for i in range(1, n, 2)) + 2 * sum(f(a + i * h) for i in range(2, n, 2))
    return h / 3 * s


def integral_to_infinity(f, a, n=200):
    """int_a^inf f(x) dx with x = a/t, t in (0, 1]: dx = -a/t^2 dt."""
    def g(t):
        t = max(t, 1e-8)   # the value at t = 0 is a limit; evaluate just inside it
        x = a / t
        return f(x) * a / (t * t)
    return simpson(g, 0.0, 1.0, n)


def integral_sqrt_singular(g, n=200):
    """int_0^1 g(x)/sqrt(x) dx with x = u^2: becomes int_0^1 2 g(u^2) du."""
    return simpson(lambda u: 2.0 * g(u * u), 0.0, 1.0, n)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    I1 = integral_to_infinity(lambda x: 1 / x**2, 1.0)
    print(f"int_1^inf 1/x^2 = {I1:.12f}")
    assert abs(I1 - 1.0) < 1e-8                                   # the lesson's test
    I2 = integral_sqrt_singular(lambda x: 1.0)
    assert abs(I2 - 2.0) < 1e-12
    I3 = integral_sqrt_singular(math.cos)                         # int_0^1 cos(x)/sqrt(x) dx
    assert abs(I3 - 1.8090484758005) < 1e-9
    GM, R = 3.986e14, 6.371e6
    w = integral_to_infinity(lambda r: GM / r**2, R)
    v = math.sqrt(2 * w)
    print(f"escape: {w / 1e6:.2f} MJ/kg, {v / 1000:.2f} km/s")
    assert abs(v - 11186.1) < 1
```

:::exercise Does it converge?
Decide whether each integral converges, and give its value if it does: (a) $\int_1^\infty x^{-3/2}\,dx$, (b) $\int_0^1 x^{-2}\,dx$, (c) $\int_2^\infty \dfrac{dx}{x^2 + 1}$.
---solution---
1. (a) $p = 3/2 > 1$ on an infinite range: converges to $\dfrac{1}{p - 1} = 2$.
2. (b) $p = 2 \ge 1$ at a singularity: diverges, since $\int_\delta^1 x^{-2}\,dx = 1/\delta - 1 \to \infty$.
3. (c) $\dfrac{1}{x^2 + 1} < \dfrac{1}{x^2}$ and $\int_2^\infty x^{-2}\,dx$ converges, so (c) converges by comparison.
4. Its value: $\left[\arctan x\right]_2^\infty = \dfrac{\pi}{2} - \arctan 2 = 0.4636$.
:::
