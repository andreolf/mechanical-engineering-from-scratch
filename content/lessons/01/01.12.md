Whenever a quantity changes at a rate proportional to itself, you get an exponential: a cooling cup, a discharging capacitor, a decaying vibration, the tension in a rope wrapped around a bollard. The logarithm undoes it, and turns exponential data into straight lines you can fit. This lesson builds both from one differential equation.

## $e^x$ from $y' = y$

Define $\exp$ as the function that equals its own derivative and starts at 1: $y' = y$, $y(0) = 1$. Its Taylor series is forced by that, since every derivative at 0 equals 1:

$$
e^x = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^k}{k!} .
$$

From the equation, $\frac{d}{dx}[e^{x}e^{a-x}] = 0$, so $e^{x}e^{a-x} = e^{a}$, which is the law $e^{x+y} = e^x e^y$. The natural logarithm is the inverse, $\ln(e^x) = x$, and differentiating that identity gives $(\ln x)' = 1/x$.

## Newton's law of cooling

A body at temperature $T$ in surroundings at $T_a$ loses heat at a rate proportional to the difference:

$$
\frac{dT}{dt} = -\frac{T - T_a}{\tau} .
$$

Put $u = T - T_a$; then $u' = -u/\tau$, a scaled version of $y' = y$, so

$$
T(t) = T_a + (T_0 - T_a)\,e^{-t/\tau} .
$$

The **time constant** $\tau$ is the time to close 63% of the gap ($1 - e^{-1}$). After $3\tau$ it's 95% closed, after $5\tau$ more than 99%.

## Logs turn exponentials into lines

Take logs of the cooling law:

$$
\ln(T - T_a) = \ln(T_0 - T_a) - \frac{t}{\tau} .
$$

Plot $\ln(T - T_a)$ against $t$ and you get a straight line with slope $-1/\tau$. Fitting that line by least squares (lesson 00.06) recovers $\tau$ from noisy data.

:::example A cup of coffee
Coffee at $85\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ in a $22\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ room has cooled to $60\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$ after 10 minutes. When will it be at a drinkable $50\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$?

1. From the law: $\dfrac{60 - 22}{85 - 22} = e^{-10/\tau}$, so $\tau = \dfrac{10}{\ln(63/38)} = \dfrac{10}{0.5056} = 19.78\ \mathrm{min}$.
2. Solve for $50\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$: $t = \tau\ln\dfrac{63}{28} = 19.78 \times 0.8109 = 16.0\ \mathrm{min}$.

The first $25\ \mathrm{K}$ took 10 minutes; the next $10\ \mathrm{K}$ take 6 more, and each further kelvin takes longer still as the gap shrinks.
:::

```python file=cooling.py
"""Fit a cooling time constant by linear regression on ln(T - T_ambient)."""
import math
import random


def fit_line(xs, ys):
    mx, my = sum(xs) / len(xs), sum(ys) / len(ys)
    b = sum((x - mx) * (y - my) for x, y in zip(xs, ys)) / sum((x - mx) ** 2 for x in xs)
    return my - b * mx, b


def fit_tau(ts, Ts, Ta):
    a, b = fit_line(ts, [math.log(T - Ta) for T in Ts])
    return -1.0 / b, Ta + math.exp(a)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = random.Random(7)
    Ta, T0, tau = 22.0, 85.0, 19.78
    ts = [0.5 * k for k in range(0, 61)]                     # 30 minutes, every 30 s
    Ts = [Ta + (T0 - Ta) * math.exp(-t / tau) + rng.gauss(0, 0.15) for t in ts]
    tau_fit, T0_fit = fit_tau(ts, Ts, Ta)
    print(f"fitted tau = {tau_fit:.2f} min (true {tau}), T0 = {T0_fit:.2f} C")
    assert abs(tau_fit / tau - 1) < 0.02                     # the lesson's test
    tau_c = 10 / math.log(63 / 38)
    assert abs(tau_c - 19.7805) < 1e-4 and abs(tau_c * math.log(63 / 28) - 16.0406) < 1e-4
    assert abs(math.exp(1.5) * math.exp(0.7) - math.exp(2.2)) < 1e-12
```

Only temperatures well above ambient carry information about $\tau$: near the end, $T - T_a$ is comparable to the sensor noise and its logarithm is wild. Real fits weight points or stop once the gap falls below a few times the noise.

:::exercise The rope around a bollard
A rope wrapped around a fixed post holds a large load with a small pull, because friction builds tension exponentially with wrap angle (the capstan equation $T_\text{load} = T_\text{hold}\,e^{\mu\varphi}$). With $\mu = 0.3$, what holding force secures a $5\ \mathrm{kN}$ boat line with three full turns?
---solution---
1. Wrap angle: $\varphi = 3 \times 2\pi = 18.85\ \mathrm{rad}$.
2. Amplification: $e^{\mu\varphi} = e^{0.3 \times 18.85} = e^{5.655} = 285.6$.
3. Holding force: $5000 / 285.6 = 17.5\ \mathrm{N}$, less than the weight of a 2 kg bag.
4. The derivation is the same as for cooling: tension changes along the rope at a rate proportional to itself, $dT/d\varphi = \mu T$.
:::
