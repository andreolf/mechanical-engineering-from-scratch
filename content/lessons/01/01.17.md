Adding two vibrations of the same frequency with trigonometric identities takes a page of algebra. With complex numbers it takes one addition. Engineers use complex "phasors" for vibrations, AC circuits and frequency response because they turn calculus on sinusoids into arithmetic. This lesson builds them from Euler's formula.

## Complex numbers as points and arrows

A complex number $z = a + ib$, with $i^2 = -1$, is a point in the plane. In polar form it has a length $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ and an angle $\arg z = \operatorname{atan2}(b, a)$. Multiplying two complex numbers multiplies their lengths and adds their angles, so multiplying by $i$ is a quarter turn.

## Euler's formula

Put $i\theta$ into the exponential series from lesson 01.12 and sort real and imaginary terms. The even powers give the series of $\cos$, the odd ones the series of $\sin$:

$$
e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta .
$$

So $e^{i\theta}$ is the unit arrow at angle $\theta$, and every complex number is $z = |z|e^{i\arg z}$.

## Phasors

A sinusoid of fixed angular frequency $\omega$ is the real part of a rotating arrow:

$$
A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) = \operatorname{Re}\left[A e^{i\varphi}\,e^{i\omega t}\right] .
$$

The complex amplitude $\hat x = A e^{i\varphi}$ is the **phasor**. It holds amplitude and phase in one number. Because every signal at frequency $\omega$ shares the factor $e^{i\omega t}$, summing signals is summing phasors, and differentiating a signal is multiplying its phasor by $i\omega$:

$$
x_1 + x_2 \;\leftrightarrow\; \hat x_1 + \hat x_2, \qquad \dot x \;\leftrightarrow\; i\omega\,\hat x .
$$

:::example Two vibration sources on one machine
A motor and a fan on the same frame both shake it at $50\ \mathrm{Hz}$. The motor alone gives $2.0\ \mathrm{mm}$ amplitude; the fan gives $1.5\ \mathrm{mm}$, leading by $60^\circ$. What's the combined amplitude?

1. Phasors: $\hat x_1 = 2.0$, $\hat x_2 = 1.5e^{i\pi/3} = 0.750 + 1.299i$.
2. Sum: $\hat x = 2.750 + 1.299i$.
3. Amplitude: $|\hat x| = \sqrt{2.750^2 + 1.299^2} = 3.04\ \mathrm{mm}$; phase $\operatorname{atan2}(1.299, 2.750) = 25.3^\circ$.

Not $3.5\ \mathrm{mm}$, the sum of the amplitudes, because the peaks don't coincide. Shift the fan's phase to $180^\circ$ and the two would partly cancel to $0.5\ \mathrm{mm}$; that's the principle of active vibration cancellation.
:::

```python file=phasor.py
"""Phasor arithmetic for same-frequency oscillations, checked sample by sample."""
import cmath
import math


def phasor(amplitude, phase_deg):
    return amplitude * cmath.exp(1j * math.radians(phase_deg))


def signal(ph, w, t):
    return (ph * cmath.exp(1j * w * t)).real    # A cos(w t + phi)


def amp_phase(ph):
    return abs(ph), math.degrees(cmath.phase(ph))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    w = 2 * math.pi * 50
    p1, p2 = phasor(2.0, 0), phasor(1.5, 60)
    total = p1 + p2
    A, phi = amp_phase(total)
    print(f"sum: {A:.4f} mm at {phi:.2f} deg")
    assert abs(A - 3.0414) < 1e-4 and abs(phi - 25.285) < 1e-3
    # the lesson's test: phasor sum equals the sum of time signals at every sample
    for k in range(1000):
        t = k * 1e-5
        assert abs(signal(total, w, t) - (signal(p1, w, t) + signal(p2, w, t))) < 1e-12
    # 3 cos(wt) + 4 sin(wt): sin is cos lagging 90 degrees
    A2, phi2 = amp_phase(phasor(3, 0) + phasor(4, -90))
    assert abs(A2 - 5) < 1e-12 and abs(phi2 + 53.130) < 1e-3
    # derivative: velocity phasor is i*w times displacement phasor
    v = 1j * w * total
    t, h = 1.234e-3, 1e-8
    num = (signal(total, w, t + h) - signal(total, w, t - h)) / (2 * h)
    assert abs(num - signal(v, w, t)) < 1e-4 * abs(v)
```

:::exercise Rewrite as one cosine
Write $x(t) = 3\cos\omega t + 4\sin\omega t$ as a single $A\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$.
---solution---
1. $\sin\omega t = \cos(\omega t - 90^\circ)$, so its phasor is $4e^{-i\pi/2} = -4i$.
2. Total phasor: $3 - 4i$.
3. $A = |3 - 4i| = 5$ and $\varphi = \operatorname{atan2}(-4, 3) = -53.13^\circ$.
4. So $x(t) = 5\cos(\omega t - 53.13^\circ)$. Check at $t = 0$: $5\cos(-53.13^\circ) = 3$, matching $3\cos0 + 4\sin0$.
:::
