A derivative is a rate: velocity from position, strain rate from strain, heat flux from a temperature gradient. Measured data and simulation output rarely come with a formula, so you will compute derivatives from samples. This lesson derives the finite-difference formulas from the definition, predicts their error with Taylor's theorem, and shows why making the step smaller eventually makes things worse.

## The definition

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} .
$$

Geometrically, the secant slope through $(x, f(x))$ and $(x+h, f(x+h))$ turns into the tangent slope. Numerically, you can't take the limit, so you pick a small $h$ and live with the error.

## Three difference formulas

$$
D_+ f = \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}, \qquad D_- f = \frac{f(x) - f(x-h)}{h}, \qquad D_0 f = \frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h} .
$$

Expand $f(x \pm h)$ in a Taylor series (lesson 01.07 proves this works):

$$
f(x \pm h) = f(x) \pm h f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{2} f''(x) \pm \frac{h^3}{6} f'''(x) + \dots
$$

Substituting gives the **truncation errors**:

$$
D_+ f - f'(x) = \frac{h}{2} f''(x) + O(h^2), \qquad D_0 f - f'(x) = \frac{h^2}{6} f'''(x) + O(h^4).
$$

The forward difference is first order: halve $h$, halve the error. The central difference is second order: halve $h$, quarter the error. In the central formula the even terms cancel by symmetry, for the same price of two function evaluations.

## Round-off pushes back

Each function value carries a rounding error of about $\varepsilon |f|$. The numerator subtracts two nearly equal numbers (lesson 00.07), so the round-off error in $D_+ f$ is about $\varepsilon |f| / h$, which grows as $h$ shrinks. Balancing it against the truncation error $\tfrac{h}{2}|f''|$:

$$
h_\text{opt} \approx \sqrt{\varepsilon} \approx 10^{-8} \;\;(\text{forward}), \qquad h_\text{opt} \approx \varepsilon^{1/3} \approx 6\times10^{-6} \;\;(\text{central}).
$$

:::example Velocity from position samples
A position sensor reads $x(1.9) = 7.22\ \mathrm{m}$, $x(2.0) = 8.00\ \mathrm{m}$ and $x(2.1) = 8.82\ \mathrm{m}$. Estimate the velocity at $t = 2.0\ \mathrm{s}$.

1. Forward: $\dfrac{8.82 - 8.00}{0.1} = 8.2\ \mathrm{m/s}$.
2. Backward: $\dfrac{8.00 - 7.22}{0.1} = 7.8\ \mathrm{m/s}$.
3. Central: $\dfrac{8.82 - 7.22}{0.2} = 8.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

The data came from $x = 2t^2$, whose true velocity at $t = 2$ is $4t = 8\ \mathrm{m/s}$. The central difference is exact here because $f''' = 0$ for a quadratic; the one-sided formulas are off by $\tfrac{h}{2}f'' = 0.05 \times 4 = 0.2\ \mathrm{m/s}$, exactly as predicted.
:::

```python file=finite_diff.py
"""Forward, backward and central differences, and their error slopes on a log-log plot."""
import math


def forward(f, x, h):  return (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h
def backward(f, x, h): return (f(x) - f(x - h)) / h
def central(f, x, h):  return (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)


def slope(hs, errs):
    """Least-squares slope of log(err) against log(h)."""
    X = [math.log(h) for h in hs]; Y = [math.log(e) for e in errs]
    mx, my = sum(X) / len(X), sum(Y) / len(Y)
    return sum((a - mx) * (b - my) for a, b in zip(X, Y)) / sum((a - mx) ** 2 for a in X)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    f, x, exact = math.sin, 1.0, math.cos(1.0)
    hs = [10 ** (-k / 4) for k in range(4, 17)]      # 1e-1 .. 1e-4, truncation-dominated
    ef = [abs(forward(f, x, h) - exact) for h in hs]
    ec = [abs(central(f, x, h) - exact) for h in hs]
    pf, pc = slope(hs, ef), slope(hs, ec)
    print(f"forward slope {pf:.3f}, central slope {pc:.3f}")
    assert abs(pf - 1) < 0.05 and abs(pc - 2) < 0.05   # the lesson's test

    # predicted truncation error at h = 0.1
    assert abs(abs(forward(f, x, 0.1) - exact) / (0.05 * math.sin(1)) - 1) < 0.05
    # round-off: forward error at h = 1e-14 is far worse than at h = 1e-8
    assert abs(forward(f, x, 1e-14) - exact) > 100 * abs(forward(f, x, 1e-8) - exact)

    pos = lambda t: 2 * t * t
    assert abs(central(pos, 2.0, 0.1) - 8.0) < 1e-12
    assert abs(forward(pos, 2.0, 0.1) - 8.2) < 1e-9
```

:::exercise Strain rate from a tensile test
An extensometer records strain $\varepsilon$ every $0.5\ \mathrm{s}$: $0.00120$ at $t = 10.0\ \mathrm{s}$, $0.00131$ at $10.5\ \mathrm{s}$ and $0.00143$ at $11.0\ \mathrm{s}$. Estimate the strain rate at $t = 10.5\ \mathrm{s}$ and say which formula you used and why.
---solution---
1. A sample exists on both sides, so use the second-order central difference.
2. $\dot\varepsilon \approx \dfrac{0.00143 - 0.00120}{2 \times 0.5} = \dfrac{0.00023}{1.0} = 2.3\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$.
3. One-sided estimates would give $2.4\times10^{-4}$ (forward) and $2.2\times10^{-4}$ (backward); their spread hints at the curvature of the data.
4. Sanity check: tensile standards specify strain rates of order $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ in the elastic range, so the value is plausible.
:::
