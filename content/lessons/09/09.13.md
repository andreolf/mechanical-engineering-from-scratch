Bend a paper clip once and it's fine. Bend it back and forth twenty times and it snaps, although no single bend was strong enough to break it. Most mechanical failures in service are like this: a bike crank, an axle, a wind turbine bolt, each loaded far below its tensile strength, but millions of times. That's **fatigue**. A tiny crack starts at a stress concentration (a scratch, a sharp corner, a weld toe), grows a little with each cycle, and the part breaks suddenly once the crack reaches its critical size (09.12).

## The S-N curve

Test many specimens at different fully reversed stress amplitudes $\sigma_a$ and record the cycles to failure $N$. On log-log axes the data fall on a straight line, **Basquin's law**:

$$
\sigma_a = \sigma_f'\,(2N)^b ,
$$

with $b$ typically between −0.05 and −0.12. Steels show an apparent **endurance limit** near $10^6$ to $10^7$ cycles, often about half the tensile strength for polished specimens. Aluminium has none: it always fails eventually, which is why aircraft structures have a finite certified life.

## Mean stress

Most real loads aren't fully reversed. A bolt is preloaded, then vibrates. A tensile mean stress $\sigma_m$ opens cracks and shortens life. The **Goodman** line converts the pair to an equivalent fully reversed amplitude:

$$
\frac{\sigma_a}{\sigma_{ar}} + \frac{\sigma_m}{\sigma_u} = 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma_{ar} = \frac{\sigma_a}{1 - \sigma_m/\sigma_u} .
$$

## Variable loading: Miner's rule

If a part sees $n_i$ cycles at amplitude $s_i$, where it would last $N_i$ cycles, each block uses up a fraction $n_i/N_i$ of its life. It fails when

$$
D = \sum_i \frac{n_i}{N_i} = 1 .
$$

Miner's rule is crude (actual failures range from $D = 0.3$ to 3) but it's the standard design starting point.

## Designing against fatigue

Avoid sharp corners, polish surfaces, and put critical welds in low-stress regions. Shot peening adds compressive residual stress at the surface. Keep the stress range low, and remember that a few large cycles can do more damage than many small ones.

:::example Fitting and using an S-N curve
Steel with $\sigma_f' = 1225$ MPa, $b = -0.095$, $\sigma_u = 725$ MPa.

1. Six synthetic tests from 650 MPa (575 cycles) to 350 MPa (256 000 cycles), with scatter of 0.08 in log₁₀N. A log-log least-squares fit gives $\sigma_f' = 1232$ MPa and $b = -0.0959$, close to the true values.
2. Strength at $10^6$ cycles: $1225\,(2\times10^6)^{-0.095} = 308.7$ MPa, 0.43 times the UTS.
3. A shaft with amplitude 250 MPa and mean 150 MPa: Goodman gives $250/(1 - 150/725) = 315.2$ MPa. The life is $8.0\times10^5$ cycles, against $9.2\times10^6$ with zero mean. A modest mean stress cuts the life more than tenfold.
4. Daily loading of 20 cycles at 450 MPa, 300 at 380 and 3000 at 320: the damage per day is $8.11\times10^{-3}$, so Miner predicts 123 days. The 3000 small cycles do 54 % of the damage and the 20 big ones 13 %.
:::

```python file=fatigue.py
"""Fatigue: fit Basquin's law to S-N data, correct for mean stress with Goodman, and sum damage with Miner's rule."""
import math
import numpy as np

SF, B = 1225.0, -0.095       # fatigue strength coefficient (MPa) and exponent, roughly AISI 1045 steel
SU = 725.0                  # ultimate tensile strength, MPa


def basquin_N(sa, sf=SF, b=B):
    """Cycles to failure at fully reversed amplitude sa: sa = sf (2N)^b."""
    return 0.5 * (sa / sf) ** (1 / b)


def goodman_equiv(sa, sm, su=SU):
    """Fully reversed amplitude that does the same damage as amplitude sa with mean sm."""
    return sa / (1 - sm / su)


def fit(N, S):
    """Least squares on log10 S = log10 sf + b log10(2N)."""
    X = np.log10(2 * np.asarray(N)); Y = np.log10(np.asarray(S))
    b, a = np.polyfit(X, Y, 1)
    return 10**a, b


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
    S = np.array([650, 580, 500, 440, 390, 350])
    N = np.array([basquin_N(s) for s in S]) * 10 ** rng.normal(0, 0.08, len(S))
    for s, n in zip(S, N):
        print(f"  {s} MPa: failed after {n:.3g} cycles")
    sf, b = fit(N, S)
    print(f"fit: sigma_f' = {sf:.0f} MPa, b = {b:.4f} (true 1225, -0.095)")
    assert abs(b - B) < 0.01 and abs(sf / SF - 1) < 0.15
    Se = SF * (2e6) ** B
    print(f"strength at 10^6 cycles: {Se:.1f} MPa (ratio to UTS {Se/SU:.2f})")
    # a shaft with mean 200 MPa and amplitude 250 MPa
    seq = goodman_equiv(250, 150)
    print(f"amplitude 250 with mean 150: Goodman equivalent {seq:.1f} MPa, life {basquin_N(seq):.3g} cycles (without mean: {basquin_N(250):.3g})")
    assert abs(250 / seq + 150 / SU - 1) < 1e-12
    # Miner: blocks of loading per day
    blocks = [(450, 20), (380, 300), (320, 3000)]
    D = sum(n / basquin_N(s) for s, n in blocks)
    print(f"daily damage {D:.4e}; Miner life {1/D:.0f} days ({1/D/365:.1f} years)")
    for s, n in blocks:
        print(f"  {s} MPa x {n}: {n/basquin_N(s)/D*100:.1f} % of the damage")
    # Basquin is exactly invertible
    assert abs(SF * (2 * basquin_N(333.0)) ** B - 333.0) < 1e-9
```

:::exercise A preloaded bolt
A bolt of the same steel has a mean stress of 400 MPa from preload and a vibrating amplitude of 60 MPa. What's the equivalent fully reversed amplitude, and is the life above $10^7$ cycles?
---solution---
1. $\sigma_{ar} = 60/(1 - 400/725) = 60/0.4483 = 133.8$ MPa.
2. $N = \tfrac12(133.8/1225)^{1/(-0.095)}$. $\log_{10}(133.8/1225) = -0.9617$, divided by $-0.095$ gives 10.121, so $N = 0.5\times10^{10.121} = 6.6\times10^9$ cycles.
3. Yes, well above $10^7$. High preload is good for bolts because the bolt then sees only a small share of the external load variation (Phase 06).
:::
