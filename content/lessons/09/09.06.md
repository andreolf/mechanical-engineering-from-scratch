Melt copper and nickel together and let the mixture cool. Unlike a pure metal, it doesn't freeze at one temperature. Over a range of about 25 °C, solid crystals richer in nickel grow inside a liquid richer in copper. Phase diagrams are the maps that tell you, for any composition and temperature, which phases are present, what they're made of and how much of each there is.

## Reading a binary diagram

The horizontal axis is composition (here, % nickel), the vertical one temperature. Copper and nickel dissolve in each other completely in both liquid and solid, so their diagram is a simple **lens** between two curves:

- above the **liquidus**, all liquid;
- below the **solidus**, all solid;
- between them, liquid and solid coexist.

Inside the two-phase region, draw a horizontal **tie line** through your point. Its ends, on the liquidus and solidus, give the compositions $x_L$ and $x_S$ of the liquid and the solid.

## The lever rule

How much of each phase? Conservation of nickel: if a fraction $f_L$ is liquid and $f_S = 1 - f_L$ is solid, then $f_L x_L + f_S x_S = x_0$. Solving,

$$
f_L = \frac{x_S - x_0}{x_S - x_L}, \qquad f_S = \frac{x_0 - x_L}{x_S - x_L} .
$$

It's like a lever balanced at $x_0$: each phase's fraction is the length of the arm on the *opposite* side, divided by the whole tie line.

## Where the curves come from

For an ideal solution, each component's equilibrium between liquid and solid depends only on its own melting point $T_m$ and heat of fusion $\Delta H$:

$$
\frac{x_L}{x_S} = \exp\left[\frac{\Delta H}{R}\left(\frac1{T_m} - \frac1T\right)\right] .
$$

Writing that for both copper and nickel gives two equations for $x_L$ and $x_S$ at each temperature, so the whole lens follows from four handbook numbers. Real alloys deviate somewhat, and many systems have eutectics, intermediate compounds and solid-state changes (09.07), but the tie-line and lever-rule reasoning is the same everywhere.

:::widget leverrule
An ideal-solution Cu-Ni diagram. Move the alloy's composition and temperature. Inside the lens you get the tie line (lime) and the lever-rule fractions.
:::

:::example A 40 % nickel alloy
Copper: $T_m = 1358$ K, $\Delta H = 13.05$ kJ/mol. Nickel: 1728 K, 17.48 kJ/mol.

1. Phase boundaries from the ideal-solution model (% Ni in liquid / solid): 1100 °C, 3.3 / 4.5; 1200 °C, 26.9 / 33.3; 1300 °C, 53.5 / 60.3; 1400 °C, 82.8 / 86.2.
2. At 1235 °C: liquid with 35.9 % Ni and solid with 42.9 % Ni. Lever rule: 41.2 % liquid and 58.8 % solid. The two fractions sum to 1 exactly, and they reproduce the overall 40 % Ni to $10^{-12}$.
3. Bisection on each boundary: the alloy starts to freeze at 1250.6 °C and is fully solid at 1224.5 °C.
4. Cooled fast, the first solid (richer in Ni) has no time to even out, giving composition gradients called **coring**. A homogenising anneal fixes it.
:::

```python file=lever.py
"""Phase diagrams: an ideal-solution Cu-Ni diagram from melting data, and the lever rule."""
import math
import numpy as np

R = 8.314
TA, HA = 1358.0, 13.05e3       # copper: melting point (K), heat of fusion (J/mol)
TB, HB = 1728.0, 17.48e3       # nickel


def solidus_liquidus(T):
    """Ideal liquid and solid solutions: x_l / x_s = exp(H / R (1/Tm - 1/T)) for each component. Returns Ni fractions (x_s, x_l)."""
    kA = math.exp(HA / R * (1 / TA - 1 / T)); kB = math.exp(HB / R * (1 / TB - 1 / T))
    xs = (1 - kA) / (kB - kA); xl = kB * xs
    return xs, xl


def lever(x0, xs, xl):
    """Fractions of liquid and solid for overall composition x0 between the two phase boundaries."""
    fl = (xs - x0) / (xs - xl); return fl, 1 - fl


if __name__ == "__main__":
    for Tc in (1100, 1200, 1250, 1300, 1400):
        xs, xl = solidus_liquidus(Tc + 273.15)
        print(f"{Tc} C: liquid has {xl*100:.1f} % Ni, solid has {xs*100:.1f} % Ni")
    x0, T = 0.40, 1235 + 273.15
    xs, xl = solidus_liquidus(T)
    fl, fs = lever(x0, xs, xl)
    print(f"alloy with 40 % Ni at 1235 C: {fl*100:.1f} % liquid ({xl*100:.1f} % Ni) and {fs*100:.1f} % solid ({xs*100:.1f} % Ni)")
    assert abs(fl + fs - 1) < 1e-15 and abs(fl * xl + fs * xs - x0) < 1e-12
    # temperatures where the 40 % alloy starts and finishes solidifying (bisection on each boundary)
    def bisect(f, lo, hi):
        for _ in range(60):
            m = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
            if f(lo) * f(m) <= 0: hi = m
            else: lo = m
        return 0.5 * (lo + hi)
    T_liq = bisect(lambda T: solidus_liquidus(T)[1] - x0, TA + 1e-6, TB - 1e-6)
    T_sol = bisect(lambda T: solidus_liquidus(T)[0] - x0, TA + 1e-6, TB - 1e-6)
    print(f"40 % Ni alloy: starts to freeze at {T_liq-273.15:.1f} C, fully solid at {T_sol-273.15:.1f} C")
    # pure ends melt at their melting points
    assert abs(solidus_liquidus(TA + 1e-3)[0]) < 1e-3 and abs(solidus_liquidus(TB - 1e-3)[1] - 1) < 1e-3
    for T in np.linspace(T_sol + 1, T_liq - 1, 5):
        xs, xl = solidus_liquidus(T); fl, fs = lever(x0, xs, xl)
        assert 0 <= fl <= 1 and abs(fl + fs - 1) < 1e-15
```

:::exercise Reading a tie line
On a measured Cu-Ni diagram at 1250 °C, the liquidus is at 32 wt% Ni and the solidus at 43 wt% Ni. An alloy has 35 wt% Ni. What fractions (by mass) are liquid and solid?
---solution---
1. $f_L = (43 - 35)/(43 - 32) = 8/11 = 0.727$.
2. $f_S = (35 - 32)/11 = 0.273$.
3. Check: $0.727\cdot32 + 0.273\cdot43 = 35.0$ wt% Ni.
:::
