Pure annealed iron yields at about 100 MPa. The steel in a Swiss watch spring yields at over 1500 MPa. It's mostly the same iron atoms, with the same Young's modulus. The difference is how hard it is for dislocations to move. Every strengthening method puts obstacles in their way.

## Four ways to block dislocations

1. **Grain boundaries.** A dislocation can't easily cross from one crystal orientation to the next, so dislocations pile up at boundaries. Smaller grains mean shorter pile-ups and more boundaries. This gives the **Hall-Petch** relation

$$
\sigma_y = \sigma_0 + \frac{k}{\sqrt d} ,
$$

with $d$ the grain size, $\sigma_0$ the friction stress of the lattice, and $k$ a material constant (about 0.74 MPa√m for mild steel, 0.07 for aluminium). It's the only mechanism that raises strength without losing toughness.

2. **Solid solution.** Dissolved atoms of a different size strain the lattice around them and pin dislocations. Brass (copper with zinc) and Al-Mg alloys use it. The effect grows roughly with the square root of concentration.

3. **Precipitation.** Fine particles of a second phase. A dislocation either cuts through them or bows out between them and leaves a loop behind (Orowan). Bowing between particles spaced $L$ apart needs a shear stress of

$$
\tau \approx \frac{G\,b}{L} ,
$$

so closer particles mean higher strength. Age-hardened aluminium bike frames (6061-T6, 7075-T6) get most of their strength this way.

4. **Work hardening.** Plastic deformation multiplies dislocations, which tangle and block each other. Cold-rolled sheet and drawn wire are strengthened this way; annealing undoes it.

To convert a shear stress on the slip plane to a tensile yield stress for a polycrystal, multiply by the **Taylor factor**, about 3.06 for FCC metals.

:::widget hallpetch
Yield strength of a steel against grain size (log scale). Change the grain size and the Hall-Petch constant.
:::

:::example Fitting Hall-Petch data
Synthetic data for a mild steel with $\sigma_0 = 70$ MPa and $k = 0.74$ MPa√m, at eight grain sizes from 5 to 200 µm, with 4 MPa of scatter.

1. Measured yields: 400.9 MPa at 5 µm, down to 127.7 MPa at 200 µm.
2. Least squares on $\sigma_y$ against $d^{-1/2}$: $\sigma_0 = 70.08 \pm 2.32$ MPa and $k = 0.7376 \pm 0.0091$ MPa√m. The fitted slope recovers 0.74 within 0.3 %, well inside its standard error.
3. Predictions: 174 MPa at 50 µm, 303 MPa at 10 µm, 808 MPa at 1 µm. Ultra-fine-grained steels are made by controlled rolling.
4. Orowan in aluminium ($G = 26$ GPa, $b = 0.286$ nm, Taylor factor 3.06): precipitates 1000 nm apart add 23 MPa; 100 nm apart, 228 MPa; 30 nm apart, 758 MPa.
:::

```python file=hallpetch.py
"""Hardening mechanisms: fit Hall-Petch to grain-size data, plus Orowan precipitation strengthening."""
import math
import numpy as np

if __name__ == "__main__":
    s0, k = 70.0, 0.74                                    # MPa, MPa m^0.5 (a mild steel)
    d = np.array([5, 8, 12, 20, 35, 60, 100, 200]) * 1e-6  # grain sizes, m
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    sy = s0 + k / np.sqrt(d) + rng.normal(0, 4.0, d.size)  # measured yield with 4 MPa scatter
    A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(d), d**-0.5])
    (s0_fit, k_fit), res, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, sy, rcond=None)
    resid = sy - A @ np.array([s0_fit, k_fit]); se = math.sqrt(resid @ resid / (len(d) - 2))
    cov = se**2 * np.linalg.inv(A.T @ A)
    print("grain size (um) and yield (MPa):", ", ".join(f"{x*1e6:.0f}: {y:.1f}" for x, y in zip(d, sy)))
    print(f"fit: sigma_0 = {s0_fit:.2f} +- {math.sqrt(cov[0,0]):.2f} MPa, k = {k_fit:.4f} +- {math.sqrt(cov[1,1]):.4f} MPa m^0.5 (input 70, 0.74)")
    assert abs(k_fit / k - 1) < 0.05 and abs(k_fit - k) < 3 * math.sqrt(cov[1, 1])
    for dd in (50e-6, 10e-6, 1e-6):
        print(f"d = {dd*1e6:.0f} um: predicted yield {s0_fit + k_fit/math.sqrt(dd):.0f} MPa")
    # Orowan: dislocations bow between precipitates spaced L apart, tau = G b / L; tensile with Taylor factor M = 3.06
    G, b, M = 26e9, 0.286e-9, 3.06
    for L in (1000e-9, 300e-9, 100e-9, 30e-9):
        print(f"aluminium, precipitate spacing {L*1e9:.0f} nm: Orowan shear {G*b/L/1e6:.1f} MPa, tensile increase {M*G*b/L/1e6:.0f} MPa")
```

:::exercise Grain refinement
An aluminium alloy has $\sigma_0 = 25$ MPa and $k = 0.068$ MPa√m. By how much does refining the grains from 100 µm to 10 µm raise the yield strength?
---solution---
1. At 100 µm: $25 + 0.068/\sqrt{10^{-4}} = 25 + 6.8 = 31.8$ MPa.
2. At 10 µm: $25 + 0.068/\sqrt{10^{-5}} = 25 + 21.5 = 46.5$ MPa.
3. The increase is 14.7 MPa, much less than in steel, because aluminium's $k$ is ten times smaller. Aluminium alloys rely mainly on precipitation instead.
:::
