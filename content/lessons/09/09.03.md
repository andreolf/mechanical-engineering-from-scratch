In the 1920s physicists calculated how strong a perfect metal crystal should be in shear. The answer was thousands of times stronger than any real metal. Either the theory was wrong, or real crystals had a defect that let them slip easily. In 1934 Taylor, Orowan and Polanyi independently proposed it: the **dislocation**. Seen directly in electron microscopes in the 1950s, dislocations explain why metals yield, why they work-harden, and why they can be bent instead of shattering.

## The ideal shear strength

Slide one plane of atoms over another by $x$. The resisting stress must repeat every atomic spacing $b$. Frenkel modelled it as a sine:

$$
\tau(x) = \tau_{max}\sin\frac{2\pi x}{b} .
$$

For small $x$ this must reduce to Hooke's law, $\tau = G\,x/a$ with $a$ the spacing between planes. Matching slopes gives

$$
\tau_{max} = \frac{G\,b}{2\pi a} \approx \frac{G}{2\pi} .
$$

For copper, $G = 48$ GPa gives about 7.6 GPa. A pure copper single crystal actually starts slipping at around 0.5 MPa, some 15,000 times less.

## Dislocations

Instead of a whole plane slipping at once, imagine an extra half-plane of atoms squeezed into the crystal: an **edge dislocation**. Under shear only the bonds near its core need to break and re-form, one row at a time, like moving a ruck along a carpet instead of dragging the whole carpet. The stress needed (the **Peierls stress**) is tiny for metals with wide, smeared-out dislocation cores. When it has crossed the crystal, the top has moved by one spacing $b$, the **Burgers vector**.

Most metallurgy is the art of making dislocations harder to move: grain boundaries, dissolved atoms, fine precipitates and tangles of other dislocations all get in their way (09.05).

## A one-dimensional model

The Frenkel-Kontorova chain captures the idea. A row of atoms joined by springs $k$ rests on a wavy substrate with wells one spacing apart. Push every atom with force $F$:

- A perfect row slips only when $F$ exceeds the substrate's maximum restoring force.
- Put one extra atom in (a **kink**, the 1D analogue of a dislocation). It glides at a far smaller push, because the atoms near it sit partway up the wells already.

:::widget fkchain
A Frenkel-Kontorova chain with one extra atom. Increase the push and the time, and watch the kink (amber) glide while the rest of the row barely moves.
:::

:::example Ideal strength and a gliding kink
1. Copper: $G/(2\pi) = 7.64$ GPa. Measured single-crystal yield is about 0.5 MPa, a ratio of 15,279.
2. The chain: 60 sites, spring $k = 4$, substrate amplitude $V_0 = 0.2$ (units where $a = 1$). A perfect chain slips at $F = V_0\pi = 0.6283$ per atom, verified: it holds at 95 % of that and slips at 105 %.
3. With one extra atom, relaxed to a kink centred at site 29.5 (tightest spacing 0.715), bisection on $F$ finds that the kink starts to glide at $F = 0.00766$, 82 times less than rigid slip.
4. Softer springs make a narrower kink that is harder to move: $k = 2$ needs 0.06016 (10.4 times less than rigid slip) and $k = 1$ needs 0.18940 (3.3 times less). Wide dislocation cores, as in FCC metals, are why those metals are so easy to deform.
:::

```python file=dislocation.py
"""Defects and dislocations: Frenkel's ideal shear strength, and a Frenkel-Kontorova chain showing why a dislocation
moves at a tiny fraction of the stress needed for rigid slip."""
import math
import numpy as np


def ideal_shear_strength(G, b=1.0, a=1.0):
    """Frenkel: tau(x) = (G b / (2 pi a)) sin(2 pi x / b), so tau_max = G b / (2 pi a)."""
    return G * b / (2 * math.pi * a)


def relax(x, k, V0, F, a=1.0, steps=20000, dt=None, pinned=False):
    """Overdamped dynamics of a chain on a sinusoidal substrate under a uniform push F per atom.
    Energy: sum k/2 (x[i+1] - x[i] - a)^2 + V0/2 (1 - cos(2 pi x/a)) - F x. Free ends, or both end atoms held."""
    dt = dt or 0.4 / (k + 2 * math.pi**2 * V0)            # stable explicit step
    x = x.copy()
    for _ in range(steps):
        s = np.diff(x) - a
        f = F - V0 * math.pi / a * np.sin(2 * math.pi * x / a)
        f[:-1] += k * s; f[1:] -= k * s
        if pinned: f[0] = f[-1] = 0.0
        x += dt * f
    return x


def kink_centre(x):
    """Weighted position of the compressed bonds (spacing below 1), or None if there are none left."""
    w = np.clip(1.0 - np.diff(x), 0, None)
    return None if w.sum() < 0.3 else float(np.sum(np.arange(len(w)) * w) / w.sum())


def moves(x0, k, V0, F):
    """True if the push makes the chain slip: the kink travels more than 5 sites (or leaves),
    or, for a perfect chain, the atoms advance by more than half a spacing."""
    pinned = kink_centre(x0) is not None
    x = relax(x0, k, V0, F, pinned=pinned)
    c0, c1 = kink_centre(x0), kink_centre(x)
    if c0 is None:
        return np.mean(x - x0) > 0.5
    return c1 is None or abs(c1 - c0) > 5


def threshold(x0, k, V0, hi, n=12):
    lo = 0.0
    for _ in range(n):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if moves(x0, k, V0, mid): hi = mid
        else: lo = mid
    return hi


def kinked(N, k, V0):
    """One extra atom squeezed into the middle: the 1D analogue of an edge dislocation, relaxed at zero load
    with the end atoms held (otherwise the kink simply escapes through a free end)."""
    i = np.arange(N + 1, dtype=float)                       # N + 1 atoms over N lattice sites
    x = i - (2 / math.pi) * np.arctan(np.exp((i - N / 2) / 2.0))  # displacement goes smoothly from 0 to -1 (sine-Gordon kink)
    x[-1] = N - 1.0
    return relax(x, k, V0, 0.0, pinned=True)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    G = 48e9                                    # copper
    tau_ideal = ideal_shear_strength(G)
    print(f"copper: G = {G/1e9:.0f} GPa, ideal shear strength G/(2 pi) = {tau_ideal/1e9:.2f} GPa; measured critical resolved shear stress of a pure single crystal ~ 0.5 MPa, ratio {tau_ideal/0.5e6:.0f}")
    assert tau_ideal / 0.5e6 > 1e3
    k, V0, N = 4.0, 0.2, 60                     # chain in units of the lattice spacing a = 1
    F_rigid = V0 * math.pi                      # largest substrate force per atom: a perfect chain slips only above this
    perfect = np.arange(N, dtype=float)
    assert not moves(perfect, k, V0, 0.95 * F_rigid) and moves(perfect, k, V0, 1.05 * F_rigid)
    xk = kinked(N, k, V0)
    print(f"relaxed kink: centred at site {kink_centre(xk):.1f}, tightest spacing {np.diff(xk).min():.3f}")
    F_kink = threshold(xk, k, V0, F_rigid, n=16)
    print(f"chain k = {k}, V0 = {V0}: rigid slip needs F = {F_rigid:.4f} per atom; the kink moves at F = {F_kink:.5f}, {F_rigid/F_kink:.0f} times less")
    assert F_kink < F_rigid / 20
    for k2 in (2.0, 1.0):
        F2 = threshold(kinked(N, k2, V0), k2, V0, F_rigid, n=16)
        print(f"softer springs k = {k2} (a narrower dislocation): the kink needs F = {F2:.5f}, {F_rigid/F2:.1f} times less than rigid slip")
        assert F2 > F_kink
```

:::exercise Aluminium and iron
Estimate the ideal shear strength of aluminium ($G = 26$ GPa) and iron ($G = 82$ GPa). Annealed pure aluminium yields in tension at about 30 MPa, and soft iron at about 150 MPa. Taking the shear yield as half the tensile yield, how many times below ideal are they?
---solution---
1. Aluminium: $26/2\pi = 4.14$ GPa. Shear yield about 15 MPa, so $4140/15 = 276$ times below ideal.
2. Iron: $82/2\pi = 13.05$ GPa. Shear yield about 75 MPa, so 174 times below.
3. Both are polycrystals with some impurities, so they're far stronger than a pure single crystal, but still two orders of magnitude below ideal. Only tiny defect-free whiskers and nanopillars come close to $G/2\pi$.
:::
