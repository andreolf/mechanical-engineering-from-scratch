A blacksmith heats a knife blade until it glows cherry red, plunges it into water, then reheats it gently until it turns straw coloured. Each step changes the arrangement of carbon atoms inside the iron. Steel is iron with less than about 2 % carbon, and that small amount of carbon, together with the heat treatment, is what lets one alloy family cover everything from soft car body panels to razor blades.

## The steel corner of the Fe-C diagram

Iron changes crystal structure with temperature. Below 912 °C pure iron is BCC **ferrite** (α), which dissolves almost no carbon (0.022 % at most). Above that it's FCC **austenite** (γ), whose larger holes hold up to 2.11 % carbon. Carbon that doesn't fit forms the hard compound **cementite**, Fe₃C, with 6.70 % C.

The key point is the **eutectoid**: at 727 °C and 0.76 % C, austenite turns into ferrite and cementite at the same time, in thin alternating plates called **pearlite**.

- **Hypoeutectoid** steel (less than 0.76 % C): on slow cooling, ferrite grows first along the austenite grain boundaries. The rest of the austenite turns into pearlite at 727 °C.
- **Hypereutectoid** steel (more than 0.76 % C): cementite forms first, then pearlite.

The lever rule (09.06), applied just above 727 °C, gives the fraction of **proeutectoid** ferrite for a hypoeutectoid steel with carbon content $c$:

$$
f_\alpha = \frac{0.76 - c}{0.76 - 0.022}, \qquad f_\text{pearlite} = 1 - f_\alpha .
$$

## Quenching and tempering

Cool austenite fast enough and the carbon has no time to diffuse out and form pearlite. The FCC lattice shears into a strained, body-centred tetragonal structure called **martensite**, which is very hard and brittle. **Tempering** (reheating to 200 to 600 °C) lets a little carbon precipitate as fine carbides. It trades some hardness for a lot of toughness.

Whether a bar becomes martensite depends on how fast it cools between 800 and 500 °C. For a small bar, lumped cooling (as in Phase 07) gives

$$
t_{8/5} = \frac{\rho c_p D}{4h}\ln\frac{800 - T_f}{500 - T_f} ,
$$

with $D$ the diameter, $h$ the film coefficient of the quench fluid and $T_f$ the fluid temperature. Plain carbon steels need a $t_{8/5}$ of about a second, so only water works, and only on thin sections. Alloying with chromium, molybdenum or nickel slows the pearlite reaction, so oil or even air can harden thicker parts. That property is called **hardenability**.

:::example Microstructure and quench times
1. With 0.45 % C (a typical shaft steel), proeutectoid ferrite is $(0.76 - 0.45)/(0.76 - 0.022) = 42.0$ % and pearlite 58.0 %. Overall cementite: $(0.45 - 0.022)/(6.70 - 0.022) = 6.41$ %.
2. Pearlite itself is $(0.76 - 0.022)/(6.70 - 0.022) = 11.1$ % cementite plates.
3. Carbon balance, $f_\alpha\cdot0.022 + f_\text{Fe₃C}\cdot6.70$, gives back the alloy's carbon content to $10^{-12}$ for every composition tested.
4. A 25 mm bar ($\rho = 7850$ kg/m³, $c_p = 600$ J/(kg K)) cooling into fluid at 20 °C: 286 s in still air ($h = 50$), 9.5 s in oil ($h = 1500$), 2.9 s in water ($h = 5000$).
5. The Biot number in water is $hD/(4k) = 0.78$, well above 0.1. The core cools much more slowly than the surface, so even water-quenched plain carbon bars may harden only in a surface shell.
:::

```python file=steel.py
"""Steel: the Fe-C eutectoid, lever-rule microstructure fractions, and how fast a quenched bar cools."""
import math

C_ALPHA, C_EUT, C_CEM = 0.022, 0.76, 6.70   # wt% C: ferrite at 727 C, eutectoid, cementite (Fe3C)


def microstructure(c):
    """Room-temperature fractions after slow cooling: (proeutectoid phase, pearlite, total ferrite, total cementite)."""
    if c <= C_EUT:
        pro = (C_EUT - c) / (C_EUT - C_ALPHA)          # proeutectoid ferrite, lever rule just above 727 C
    else:
        pro = (c - C_EUT) / (C_CEM - C_EUT)            # proeutectoid cementite
    pearlite = 1 - pro
    fe3c = (c - C_ALPHA) / (C_CEM - C_ALPHA)            # lever rule just below 727 C, all phases
    return pro, pearlite, 1 - fe3c, fe3c


def t85(D, h, rho=7850.0, cp=600.0, Tf=20.0):
    """Lumped cooling time from 800 to 500 C for a long bar of diameter D (m) in a fluid with film coefficient h."""
    return rho * cp * D / (4 * h) * math.log((800 - Tf) / (500 - Tf))


def hardness_brinell(c):
    """Rough rule for slowly cooled carbon steel: ferrite about 80 HB, pearlite about 250 HB, mixed by fraction."""
    pro, pearl, _, _ = microstructure(c)
    return 80 * pro + 250 * pearl if c <= C_EUT else 250 * pearl + 650 * pro


if __name__ == "__main__":
    for c in (0.1, 0.2, 0.45, 0.76, 1.0):
        pro, pearl, fa, fc = microstructure(c)
        kind = "ferrite" if c <= C_EUT else "cementite"
        print(f"{c:.2f} %C: proeutectoid {kind} {pro*100:.1f} %, pearlite {pearl*100:.1f} %, overall {fc*100:.2f} % Fe3C, about {hardness_brinell(c):.0f} HB")
    # pearlite itself: lever rule at the eutectoid
    fc_p = (C_EUT - C_ALPHA) / (C_CEM - C_ALPHA)
    print(f"pearlite is {fc_p*100:.1f} % cementite plates in ferrite")
    assert abs(fc_p - 0.1105) < 1e-3
    # carbon balance check for every composition
    for c in (0.05, 0.3, 0.76, 1.2):
        pro, pearl, fa, fc = microstructure(c)
        assert abs(fa * C_ALPHA + fc * C_CEM - c) < 1e-12 and abs(pro + pearl - 1) < 1e-15
    for name, h in (("still air", 50), ("oil", 1500), ("water", 5000)):
        print(f"25 mm bar in {name} (h = {h}): 800 to 500 C in {t85(0.025, h):.1f} s")
    assert abs(t85(0.025, 50) / t85(0.025, 5000) - 100) < 1e-9
    print(f"Biot number of the 25 mm bar in water (k = 40): {5000*0.025/4/40:.2f} (above 0.1, so the core lags the surface)")
```

:::exercise A 0.2 % carbon steel
After slow cooling, what fractions of proeutectoid ferrite and pearlite does a 0.20 % C steel have? Roughly estimate its hardness, taking ferrite as 80 HB and pearlite as 250 HB.
---solution---
1. $f_\alpha = (0.76 - 0.20)/(0.76 - 0.022) = 0.56/0.738 = 0.759$.
2. Pearlite: $0.241$.
3. Hardness: $0.759\cdot80 + 0.241\cdot250 = 60.7 + 60.2 = 121$ HB. That's soft and easy to weld, typical of structural steel.
:::
