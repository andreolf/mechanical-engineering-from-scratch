Tighten a plastic hose clamp and come back a month later: it's loose. Hang a weight from a nylon line and it slowly stretches. Metals at room temperature behave elastically: release the load and they spring back, regardless of how long it was applied. Polymers are long tangled chains that slide past each other given time, so their response depends on **time and temperature**. They're partly solid, partly liquid: **viscoelastic**.

## Chains and temperature

A polymer is thousands of repeating units joined into a chain. Polyethylene is just $-\text{CH}_2-$ repeated. Below its **glass transition temperature** $T_g$, an amorphous polymer is glassy and stiff (acrylic, polystyrene at room temperature). Above $T_g$ the chains can wriggle and it becomes rubbery (rubber, polyethylene). **Thermoplastics** soften on heating and can be remoulded. **Thermosets** (epoxy) and elastomers have chemical cross-links between chains and don't melt.

## Springs and dashpots

Model the elastic part as a spring, $\sigma = E\varepsilon$, and the viscous part as a dashpot, $\sigma = \eta\dot\varepsilon$. The ratio $\tau = \eta/E$ is a time constant.

- **Maxwell model** (spring and dashpot in series). Hold the strain fixed and the stress **relaxes**:

$$
\sigma(t) = E\varepsilon_0\, e^{-t/\tau} .
$$

- **Kelvin-Voigt model** (spring and dashpot in parallel). Apply a constant stress and the strain **creeps** towards $\sigma/E$:

$$
\varepsilon(t) = \frac{\sigma}{E}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right) .
$$

Neither model alone looks like a real polymer: Maxwell flows forever under load, and Kelvin-Voigt has no instant stretch. The **standard linear solid** (a spring $E_1$ in series with a Kelvin-Voigt unit $E_2$, $\eta$) gives an instant strain $\sigma/E_1$ followed by delayed creep up to $\sigma/E_1 + \sigma/E_2$.

Design consequence: for a plastic part under sustained load, use the **creep modulus** at the design life and temperature, which can be half the short-term modulus or less.

:::example Relaxation and creep
Polymer with $E = 2$ GPa and $\eta = 2\times10^{12}$ Pa s, so $\tau = 1000$ s.

1. Held at 1 % strain (initial stress 20 MPa), a fourth-order Runge-Kutta integration gives 7.36 MPa after 1000 s and 1.00 MPa after 3000 s. The exact values are $20e^{-1} = 7.36$ and $20e^{-3} = 0.996$, matched to 0.1 %.
2. Kelvin-Voigt under 10 MPa: strain 0.3161 % after one $\tau$, exactly $0.5\,(1 - e^{-1})$ %.
3. A nylon-like standard linear solid ($E_1 = 3$ GPa, $E_2 = 6$ GPa, retardation time 1 h) under 15 MPa: 0.500 % instantly, 0.658 % after 1 h, 0.738 % after 3 h and 0.750 % after 10 h. The long-term effective modulus is 2 GPa, a third below the instant 3 GPa.
:::

```python file=viscoelastic.py
"""Viscoelastic polymers: Maxwell relaxation, Kelvin-Voigt creep and the standard linear solid, integrated step by step and checked against exact solutions."""
import math


def maxwell_relax(E, eta, eps0, t_end, dt=0.01):
    """Spring and dashpot in series held at strain eps0: d(sigma)/dt = -E/eta * sigma."""
    s, t, out = E * eps0, 0.0, []
    while t < t_end - 1e-12:
        k1 = -E / eta * s; k2 = -E / eta * (s + 0.5 * dt * k1); k3 = -E / eta * (s + 0.5 * dt * k2); k4 = -E / eta * (s + dt * k3)
        s += dt / 6 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4); t += dt
        out.append((t, s))
    return out


def kv_creep(E, eta, sigma, t_end, dt=0.01):
    """Spring and dashpot in parallel under constant stress: eta d(eps)/dt = sigma - E eps."""
    e, t, out = 0.0, 0.0, []
    f = lambda e: (sigma - E * e) / eta
    while t < t_end - 1e-12:
        k1 = f(e); k2 = f(e + 0.5 * dt * k1); k3 = f(e + 0.5 * dt * k2); k4 = f(e + dt * k3)
        e += dt / 6 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4); t += dt
        out.append((t, e))
    return out


def sls_creep(E1, E2, eta, sigma, t):
    """Standard linear solid (spring E1 in series with a Kelvin-Voigt unit E2, eta): instant then delayed strain."""
    return sigma / E1 + sigma / E2 * (1 - math.exp(-E2 * t / eta))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    E, eta = 2.0e9, 2.0e12         # Pa, Pa s: tau = 1000 s
    tau = eta / E
    hist = maxwell_relax(E, eta, 0.01, 3 * tau, dt=1.0)
    for tt, s in hist:
        if abs(tt - tau) < 0.5 or abs(tt - 3 * tau) < 0.5:
            print(f"Maxwell, held at 1 % strain: stress {s/1e6:.2f} MPa after {tt:.0f} s (started at 20 MPa)")
    assert abs(hist[-1][1] - 20e6 * math.exp(-3)) < 1e-3 * 20e6 * math.exp(-3)
    cr = kv_creep(E, eta, 10e6, 5 * tau, dt=1.0)
    for tt, e in cr:
        if abs(tt - tau) < 0.5:
            print(f"Kelvin-Voigt under 10 MPa: strain {e*100:.4f} % after one tau (limit 0.5 %)")
            assert abs(e - 0.005 * (1 - math.exp(-1))) < 1e-9
    # a nylon-like standard linear solid: 3 GPa instant, 6 GPa delayed, retardation 1 hour
    E1, E2 = 3e9, 6e9; eta2 = E2 * 3600
    for th in (0, 1, 3, 10):
        print(f"SLS under 15 MPa after {th} h: strain {sls_creep(E1, E2, eta2, 15e6, th*3600)*100:.3f} %")
    assert abs(sls_creep(E1, E2, eta2, 15e6, 1e9) - 15e6 * (1 / E1 + 1 / E2)) < 1e-12
```

:::exercise A snap-fit losing its grip
A polypropylene snap fit is bent to a fixed strain, producing 12 MPa. Its relaxation time is about 2 days (Maxwell model). What is the holding stress after a week?
---solution---
1. $t/\tau = 7/2 = 3.5$.
2. $\sigma = 12\,e^{-3.5} = 12\cdot0.0302 = 0.36$ MPa.
3. Almost nothing is left. That's why snap fits are designed to be stress-free once engaged, and why plastic parts under constant load use creep data rather than the datasheet modulus.
:::
