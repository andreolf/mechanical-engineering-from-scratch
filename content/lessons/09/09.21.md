Run a fingernail along a freshly turned shaft and you can feel tiny ridges: a fine helical thread left by the tool nose, one ridge per revolution. How rough the surface is matters for bearings, seals and fatigue (09.13), and drawings specify it with a number, usually $R_a$ in micrometres. Turning gives you direct control through two parameters: feed and nose radius.

## Roughness parameters

Measure the height $z(x)$ of the surface along a line, relative to its mean line.

- **Arithmetic mean roughness**: $R_a = \frac1L\int_0^L |z - \bar z|\,dx$.
- **Peak to valley**: $R_t = z_\text{max} - z_\text{min}$.

Typical $R_a$: rough turning 6.3 µm, finish turning 1.6, grinding 0.4, lapping or polishing 0.05. A seal running on a shaft needs about 0.2 to 0.8 µm.

## The geometric roughness

The tool's round nose (radius $r$) moves along by the feed $f$ each revolution, so the surface is a series of overlapping arcs. The cusp height between arcs is

$$
R_t = r - \sqrt{r^2 - (f/2)^2} \approx \frac{f^2}{8r} ,
$$

and since each arc is very nearly a parabola,

$$
R_a \approx \frac{f^2}{32 r} .
$$

(For an exact parabola the constant is $18\sqrt3 = 31.2$; 32 is the usual rounded version.) Halving the feed quarters the roughness. A bigger nose radius helps too, but raises cutting forces and the risk of chatter. Real surfaces are rougher than this ideal because of built-up edge, vibration and tool wear.

The same formula with stepover in place of feed gives the **scallop height** left by a ball-nose end mill when finishing curved surfaces.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 0.100 | 0.098 | 0.391 | 0.391 |
| 0.10 | 0.401 | 0.391 | 1.564 | 1.563 |
| 0.20 | 1.609 | 1.563 | 6.275 | 6.250 |
| 0.30 | 3.634 | 3.516 | 14.19 | 14.06 |

1. $R_t$ matches to 1 %; $R_a$ is 2.5 to 3.5 % above the formula, as expected from the constant 31.2 and the arcs not being exact parabolas.
2. For $R_a = 1.6$ µm: $f = \sqrt{1.6\times10^{-3}\cdot32\cdot0.8} = 0.202$ mm/rev.
3. A 6 mm ball-nose cutter with 0.5 mm stepover leaves 10.43 µm scallops.
:::

```python file=roughness.py
"""Surface finish in turning: build the feed-mark profile left by a round tool nose and compute Ra and Rt, compared with f^2/(32 r) and f^2/(8 r)."""
import math
import numpy as np


def profile(f, r, n_marks=10, pts=20000):
    """Height of the turned surface: overlapping arcs of radius r every f along the axis (mm)."""
    x = np.linspace(0, n_marks * f, n_marks * pts, endpoint=False)
    xc = (np.floor(x / f) + 0.5) * f                       # nearest arc centre
    z = r - np.sqrt(r * r - (x - xc) ** 2)                 # height above the arc bottom
    return x, z


def Ra_Rt(z):
    m = z.mean()
    return float(np.mean(np.abs(z - m))), float(z.max() - z.min())


def scallop(step, R):
    """Cusp height between ball-nose passes of radius R spaced step apart."""
    return R - math.sqrt(R * R - (step / 2) ** 2)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    r = 0.8   # mm tool nose radius
    for f in (0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3):
        x, z = profile(f, r)
        Ra, Rt = Ra_Rt(z)
        print(f"feed {f} mm/rev: Ra {Ra*1000:.3f} um (formula {f*f/(32*r)*1000:.3f}), Rt {Rt*1000:.3f} um (formula {f*f/(8*r)*1000:.3f})")
        assert abs(Rt - f * f / (8 * r)) / Rt < 0.02
        assert abs(Ra - f * f / (32 * r)) / Ra < 0.05
    f_needed = math.sqrt(1.6e-3 * 32 * r)
    print(f"largest feed for Ra 1.6 um with r = 0.8 mm: {f_needed:.3f} mm/rev")
    print(f"ball-nose 6 mm, 0.5 mm stepover: scallop {scallop(0.5, 3.0)*1000:.2f} um")
    assert abs(scallop(0.5, 3.0) - 0.5**2 / (8 * 3.0)) < 1e-4
```

:::exercise A bearing seat
A bearing seat must have $R_a \le 0.8$ µm. With a 0.4 mm nose radius, what is the largest feed?
---solution---
1. $f = \sqrt{32 r R_a} = \sqrt{32\cdot0.4\cdot0.8\times10^{-3}}$.
2. $= \sqrt{0.01024} = 0.101$ mm/rev.
3. In practice use about 0.08 mm/rev for margin, or switch to a 0.8 mm nose and run 0.14.
:::
