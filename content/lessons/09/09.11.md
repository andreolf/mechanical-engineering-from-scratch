In Bali you can see three-storey buildings framed entirely in bamboo. A culm (the stem) grows to full height in a few months, needs no processing beyond drying and treatment, and can rival steel in strength per kilogram. Bamboo and wood are natural fibre composites (09.10), and nature has optimised their structure for bending loads: wind on a tall stem.

## Fibres along the stem

Wood is made of hollow cellulose cells aligned along the trunk, glued by lignin. Bamboo has bundles of stiff fibres (around 40 GPa) embedded in soft parenchyma tissue. Both are strongly anisotropic. Spruce has $E \approx 12$ GPa along the grain but under 1 GPa across it, so timber splits easily along the grain and must be loaded along it.

## The hollow tube

For bending, what matters is the second moment of area $I$ (Phase 05), and material far from the neutral axis counts most. Take a fixed amount of material (area $A$). A solid rod of diameter $d$ has $I = \pi d^4/64$. Spread the same area into a thin tube of outer diameter $D$ and the ratio is

$$
\frac{I_\text{tube}}{I_\text{solid}} = \frac{D^2 + D_i^2}{d^2} ,
$$

which grows without limit as the tube gets wider. The limit is local buckling of a thin wall, which bamboo prevents with **nodes**: solid diaphragms every 20 to 40 cm.

## Functional grading

Bamboo goes further: its fibre fraction rises from about 15 % at the inner wall to 60 % at the outer skin. With modulus $E(r)$ varying through the wall,

$$
EI = \int E(r)\, r^2 \, dA = \int_{r_i}^{r_o} E(r)\,\pi r^3\, dr ,
$$

so moving fibres outward raises the bending stiffness without adding any material.

## Engineering with natural materials

Natural materials vary: density, moisture, knots, splits. Their design values come from statistics (09.30), and they need protection from insects, rot and UV. Bamboo is also hard to join, since you can't weld it and bolts split it, so engineered joints often fill the end internodes with mortar.

:::example A 100 mm culm
Culm with outer diameter 100 mm and wall 10 mm: $A = 2827$ mm², $I = 2.898\times10^6$ mm⁴.

1. A solid rod of the same area has $d = 60.0$ mm and $I = 0.636\times10^6$ mm⁴. The culm is 4.56 times stiffer for the same mass. Check: $(100^2 + 80^2)/60^2 = 4.56$.
2. Fibre fraction rising linearly from 15 % inside to 60 % outside averages 38.3 % over the ring. Integrating $E(r)\pi r^3$ gives $EI = 49.8$ kN m².
3. The same fibres spread evenly give 48.0 kN m², exactly $\bar E\, I$. Grading adds 3.8 % for free.
4. Total advantage over a uniform solid rod of the same mass: $4.56\times1.038 = 4.7$ times.
:::

```python file=bamboo.py
"""Bamboo: why a hollow culm with fibres crowded at the outside beats a solid rod of the same mass in bending."""
import math
import numpy as np

EF, EM = 40e9, 2e9          # bamboo fibre bundles and parenchyma matrix (along the culm), Pa


def tube_I(D, t):
    return math.pi / 64 * (D**4 - (D - 2 * t)**4)


def graded_EI(D, t, v_in, v_out, n=2000):
    """Bending stiffness of a thin-walled culm with fibre fraction varying linearly from v_in (inside) to v_out (outside).
    Integrates E(r) r^2 over the ring: I-contribution of a ring of radius r and width dr is pi r^3 dr."""
    ro, ri = D / 2, D / 2 - t
    r = np.linspace(ri, ro, n); v = v_in + (v_out - v_in) * (r - ri) / t
    E = v * EF + (1 - v) * EM
    f = E * math.pi * r**3
    EI = float(np.sum((f[1:] + f[:-1]) / 2 * np.diff(r)))
    g = v * 2 * math.pi * r
    vbar = float(np.sum((g[1:] + g[:-1]) / 2 * np.diff(r))) / (math.pi * (ro**2 - ri**2))
    return EI, vbar


if __name__ == "__main__":
    D, t = 0.100, 0.010
    A = math.pi / 4 * (D**2 - (D - 2 * t)**2); d_solid = math.sqrt(4 * A / math.pi)
    I_tube, I_solid = tube_I(D, t), math.pi * d_solid**4 / 64
    print(f"culm 100 x 10 mm: A = {A*1e6:.0f} mm^2, I = {I_tube*1e12/1e6:.3f}e6 mm^4")
    print(f"solid rod of the same area: d = {d_solid*1e3:.1f} mm, I = {I_solid*1e12/1e6:.4f}e6 mm^4; tube is {I_tube/I_solid:.2f} times stiffer")
    assert abs(I_tube / I_solid - (D**2 + (D - 2 * t)**2) / d_solid**2) < 1e-9
    EIg, vbar = graded_EI(D, t, 0.15, 0.60)
    EIu, _ = graded_EI(D, t, vbar, vbar)
    print(f"graded fibres (15 % inside to 60 % outside, mean {vbar*100:.1f} %): EI = {EIg/1e3:.1f} kN m^2")
    print(f"same fibres spread evenly: EI = {EIu/1e3:.1f} kN m^2; grading adds {(EIg/EIu-1)*100:.1f} %")
    assert abs(EIu - (vbar * EF + (1 - vbar) * EM) * I_tube) < 1e-6 * EIu and EIg > EIu
    # wood across the grain: the same fibres in the wrong direction
    E_along, E_across = 12e9, 0.8e9
    print(f"spruce: E along the grain {E_along/1e9:.0f} GPa, across {E_across/1e9:.1f} GPa, ratio {E_along/E_across:.0f}")
```

:::exercise A wider culm
Keep the area at 2827 mm² but make the culm 140 mm across. What is the wall thickness and how much stiffer is it than the solid rod (ignore grading)?
---solution---
1. $D_i^2 = 140^2 - 4\cdot2827/\pi = 19600 - 3600 = 16000$, so $D_i = 126.5$ mm and the wall is 6.75 mm.
2. $I_\text{tube}/I_\text{solid} = (19600 + 16000)/3600 = 9.89$.
3. Twice as stiff again as the 100 mm culm, but with a wall of only 6.8 mm it's far more prone to local buckling and crushing at joints.
:::
