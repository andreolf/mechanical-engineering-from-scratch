Design is optimisation with rules: the lightest bracket that doesn't yield, the cheapest tank that holds 1000 litres, the strongest beam that fits in a given depth. Lagrange multipliers turn "optimise $f$ subject to $g = 0$" into a system of equations, and the multiplier itself has a meaning engineers use constantly: the price of the constraint.

## The geometric condition

At a constrained optimum you can't improve $f$ by sliding along the constraint curve $g = 0$. So the gradient of $f$ has no component along the curve: it's perpendicular to it, just like $\nabla g$. The two gradients are parallel:

$$
\nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla g, \qquad g(\mathbf x) = 0 .
$$

Equivalently, look for stationary points of the **Lagrangian** $\mathcal L(\mathbf x, \lambda) = f(\mathbf x) - \lambda g(\mathbf x)$ in all variables, $\mathbf x$ and $\lambda$ together.

## What the multiplier means

If the constraint is $g(\mathbf x) = c$ and you relax $c$ a little, the optimal value changes at the rate

$$
\frac{df^*}{dc} = \lambda .
$$

That's a **shadow price**: how much more performance you'd buy per unit of loosened constraint. In mechanics, multipliers are the reaction forces: minimise energy subject to "this node doesn't move", and $\lambda$ is the support force.

## Inequalities

Real constraints are often inequalities ($\sigma \le \sigma_{\text{allow}}$). The Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions extend Lagrange: either the constraint is inactive and its multiplier is zero, or it's active and behaves like an equality. Lesson 04.19 builds a solver for them.

:::example The cheapest can
Make a closed cylinder holding $V = 1$ litre with the least sheet metal. Area $A = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$; constraint $\pi r^2h = V$.

1. $\partial A/\partial r = 4\pi r + 2\pi h = \lambda\,2\pi rh$ and $\partial A/\partial h = 2\pi r = \lambda\,\pi r^2$.
2. The second equation gives $\lambda = 2/r$. Substitute into the first: $4\pi r + 2\pi h = 4\pi h$, so $h = 2r$. The height equals the diameter.
3. With $V = 10^{-3}$ m³: $2\pi r^3 = V$ gives $r = 54.19$ mm and $h = 108.4$ mm.
4. Area: $A = 6\pi r^2 = 0.05536$ m² $= 553.6$ cm².
5. Multiplier: $\lambda = 2/r = 36.9$ m⁻¹. One more millilitre ($10^{-6}$ m³) costs $36.9\times10^{-6}$ m² $= 0.37$ cm² of extra sheet.

Real cans are taller than this because the lids are thicker, seamed, and more expensive per area than the wall. Put that cost into $A$ and the optimum shifts.
:::

```python file=lagrange.py
"""Lagrange multipliers by Newton's method on the stationarity system, checked by brute force."""
import math
import numpy as np


def solve_lagrange(grad_f, hess_f, g, grad_g, hess_g, x0, lam0=0.0, tol=1e-12):
    """Newton on F(x, lam) = [grad f - lam grad g, g] = 0."""
    z = np.append(np.asarray(x0, float), lam0)
    for _ in range(100):
        x, lam = z[:-1], z[-1]
        F = np.append(grad_f(x) - lam * grad_g(x), g(x))
        if np.linalg.norm(F) < tol:
            break
        n = len(x); J = np.zeros((n + 1, n + 1))
        J[:n, :n] = hess_f(x) - lam * hess_g(x)
        J[:n, n] = -grad_g(x); J[n, :n] = grad_g(x)
        z = z - np.linalg.solve(J, F)
    return z[:-1], z[-1]


if __name__ == "__main__":
    V = 1e-3
    A = lambda x: 2 * math.pi * x[0] ** 2 + 2 * math.pi * x[0] * x[1]
    gA = lambda x: np.array([4 * math.pi * x[0] + 2 * math.pi * x[1], 2 * math.pi * x[0]])
    hA = lambda x: np.array([[4 * math.pi, 2 * math.pi], [2 * math.pi, 0]])
    g = lambda x: math.pi * x[0] ** 2 * x[1] - V
    gg = lambda x: np.array([2 * math.pi * x[0] * x[1], math.pi * x[0] ** 2])
    hg = lambda x: np.array([[2 * math.pi * x[1], 2 * math.pi * x[0]], [2 * math.pi * x[0], 0]])
    (r, h), lam = solve_lagrange(gA, hA, g, gg, hg, [0.05, 0.1], 40.0)   # Newton needs a sensible start
    print(f"r = {r*1e3:.2f} mm, h = {h*1e3:.2f} mm, area = {A([r, h])*1e4:.1f} cm^2, lambda = {lam:.2f} 1/m")
    assert abs(h - 2 * r) < 1e-12 and abs(r - (V / (2 * math.pi)) ** (1 / 3)) < 1e-12 and abs(lam - 2 / r) < 1e-9
    # brute force: eliminate h with the constraint and scan r
    rs = np.linspace(0.02, 0.12, 200001)
    areas = 2 * math.pi * rs ** 2 + 2 * V / rs
    assert abs(rs[np.argmin(areas)] - r) < 1e-6
    # the multiplier is the shadow price dA*/dV
    Astar = lambda V: 6 * math.pi * (V / (2 * math.pi)) ** (2 / 3)
    dV = 1e-9
    assert abs((Astar(V + dV) - Astar(V - dV)) / (2 * dV) - lam) < 1e-4
    print("multiplier matches the sensitivity of the optimal area to the volume")
```

:::exercise The strongest beam from a log
A rectangular beam of width $b$ and depth $h$ is cut from a round log of diameter $D$, so $b^2 + h^2 = D^2$. Bending strength is proportional to the section modulus $bh^2/6$. Find the best ratio $h/b$.
---solution---
1. Maximise $bh^2$ subject to $b^2 + h^2 = D^2$. Lagrange: $h^2 = 2\lambda b$ and $2bh = 2\lambda h$, so $\lambda = b$ and $h^2 = 2b^2$.
2. $h/b = \sqrt2 \approx 1.41$.
3. From the constraint: $b = D/\sqrt3 = 0.577D$ and $h = \sqrt{2/3}\,D = 0.816D$.
4. This is the classic result Parent derived in 1708, and close to the proportions of standard sawn timber joists.
:::
