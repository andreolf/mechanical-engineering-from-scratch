If a flow is both incompressible ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0$) and irrotational ($\nabla\times\mathbf u = \mathbf 0$), its velocity is the gradient of a potential that satisfies Laplace's equation. Laplace's equation is linear, so you can build complicated flows by adding simple ones. That's how the first aerodynamicists understood flow around cylinders and wings, and it still gives the right pressure distribution away from boundary layers.

## Potential and stream function

Irrotational means $\mathbf u = \nabla\phi$. Incompressible then gives

$$
\nabla^2\phi = \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2} = 0 .
$$

In 2D there's also a **stream function** $\psi$ with $u = \partial\psi/\partial y$, $v = -\partial\psi/\partial x$. Lines of constant $\psi$ are streamlines, and the flow rate between two streamlines is the difference in $\psi$. It satisfies Laplace's equation too.

## Building blocks

| Flow | $\psi$ in polar coordinates |
|---|---|
| Uniform stream, speed $U$ | $Ur\sin\theta$ |
| Source of strength $m$ | $\frac{m}{2\pi}\theta$ |
| Doublet of strength $\kappa$ | $-\frac{\kappa\sin\theta}{2\pi r}$ |
| Free vortex, circulation $\Gamma$ | $-\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln r$ |

## Flow past a cylinder

Uniform stream plus a doublet with $\kappa = 2\pi UR^2$:

$$
\psi = U\Big(r - \frac{R^2}{r}\Big)\sin\theta .
$$

$\psi = 0$ on $r = R$, so the circle is a streamline: a solid cylinder. On its surface the speed is $|u_\theta| = 2U\sin\theta$, and Bernoulli gives the pressure coefficient

$$
C_p = \frac{p - p_\infty}{\tfrac12\rho U^2} = 1 - 4\sin^2\theta .
$$

The distribution is symmetric front to back, so the net drag is zero: **d'Alembert's paradox**. Real cylinders have drag because the boundary layer separates (Phase 12). Add a clockwise vortex of circulation $\Gamma$ (flow left to right) and the symmetry top to bottom breaks: the flow speeds up over the top, and the cylinder gets an upward lift per unit span $L' = \rho U\Gamma$ (Kutta-Joukowski). That's how a spinning ball curves.

:::widget cylinder
Streamlines past a cylinder. With Γ = 0 the pattern is symmetric and the two stagnation points (red) sit front and back. Add circulation: the flow speeds up over the top, slows underneath, and the cylinder feels lift.
:::

:::example A cylinder in a 10 m/s breeze
A pole of radius $R = 0.1$ m in air at $U = 10$ m/s, $\rho = 1.2$ kg/m³.

1. Fastest flow at the sides ($\theta = 90^\circ$): $2U = 20$ m/s.
2. There $C_p = 1 - 4 = -3$, so $p - p_\infty = -3\cdot\tfrac12\cdot1.2\cdot10^2 = -180$ Pa.
3. Front stagnation point: $C_p = 1$, $p - p_\infty = +60$ Pa.
4. Potential-flow drag: zero (symmetric pressure). A real pole at $Re = UD/\nu = 10\cdot0.2/1.5\times10^{-5} = 1.3\times10^5$ has $C_D \approx 1.2$, a drag of $1.2\cdot60\cdot0.2 = 14.4$ N per metre of height.
5. Spin it to give $\Gamma = 4$ m²/s: lift $= 1.2\cdot10\cdot4 = 48$ N per metre. That's the Magnus effect, used by Flettner rotor ships.
:::

```python file=potential_flow.py
"""Potential flow past a cylinder: Laplace check, surface speed, zero drag and Kutta-Joukowski lift."""
import math
import numpy as np


def psi_cyl(x, y, U=1.0, R=1.0, Gam=0.0):
    r2 = x * x + y * y
    return U * y * (1 - R * R / r2) + Gam / (2 * math.pi) * 0.5 * np.log(r2 / R ** 2)   # Gam > 0: clockwise vortex


def velocity(psi, x, y, h=1e-5):
    return (psi(x, y + h) - psi(x, y - h)) / (2 * h), -(psi(x + h, y) - psi(x - h, y)) / (2 * h)


def surface_forces(U, R, Gam, rho, n=20000):
    th = 2 * math.pi * (np.arange(n) + 0.5) / n
    ut = -2 * U * np.sin(th) - Gam / (2 * math.pi * R)            # tangential speed; Gam > 0 is clockwise
    p = 0.5 * rho * (U ** 2 - ut ** 2)                             # Bernoulli, p_inf = 0
    ds = R * 2 * math.pi / n
    return -np.sum(p * np.cos(th)) * ds, -np.sum(p * np.sin(th)) * ds   # drag, lift per unit span


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Laplace's equation holds away from the cylinder (5-point stencil)
    h = 1e-3
    for x, y in [(1.5, 0.3), (-2, 1), (0.2, 1.7)]:
        lap = (psi_cyl(x + h, y) + psi_cyl(x - h, y) + psi_cyl(x, y + h) + psi_cyl(x, y - h) - 4 * psi_cyl(x, y)) / h ** 2
        assert abs(lap) < 1e-5
    # the cylinder is a streamline and the surface speed is 2 U sin(theta)
    U, R = 10.0, 0.1
    psi = lambda x, y: psi_cyl(x, y, U, R)
    for th in np.linspace(0.1, 3.0, 7):
        x, y = R * math.cos(th), R * math.sin(th)
        assert abs(psi(x, y)) < 1e-12
        u, v = velocity(psi, 1.0001 * x, 1.0001 * y)
        assert abs(math.hypot(u, v) - 2 * U * math.sin(th)) < 0.01
    print(f"max surface speed {2*U:.0f} m/s, Cp min = {1 - 4:.0f}, suction {-3 * 0.5 * 1.2 * U**2:.0f} Pa")
    D, L = surface_forces(U, R, 0.0, 1.2)
    assert abs(D) < 1e-9 and abs(L) < 1e-9                         # d'Alembert's paradox
    D, L = surface_forces(U, R, 4.0, 1.2)
    print(f"with circulation 4 m^2/s: drag {D:.2e} N/m, lift {L:.4f} N/m (rho U Gamma = {1.2*U*4:.1f})")
    assert abs(D) < 1e-9 and abs(L - 1.2 * U * 4.0) < 1e-6
```

:::exercise Where is the pressure equal to ambient?
On the surface of a cylinder in potential flow, at which angles $\theta$ (from the front stagnation point) is $p = p_\infty$?
---solution---
1. $C_p = 1 - 4\sin^2\theta = 0$ gives $\sin\theta = \pm\tfrac12$.
2. $\theta = 30^\circ, 150^\circ, 210^\circ, 330^\circ$.
3. Between $30^\circ$ and $150^\circ$ (and the mirror range) the pressure is below ambient. Pressure taps at $\pm30^\circ$ read the free-stream static pressure, a trick used in some flow probes.
:::
