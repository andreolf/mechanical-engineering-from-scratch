A periodic force from a cam, a gear mesh or a reciprocating engine is rarely a pure sine. Fourier's idea was that any reasonable periodic signal is a sum of sines and cosines at whole multiples of the base frequency. That matters because linear systems respond to each sine separately: if one harmonic lands on a resonance, it can dominate the response even when it's small in the input.

## The series

For a function with period $2L$:

$$
f(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Big(a_n\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L} + b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\Big),
$$

$$
a_n = \frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx, \qquad b_n = \frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx .
$$

The coefficient formulas come from orthogonality: $\int_{-L}^{L}\sin\frac{m\pi x}{L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx = 0$ for $m \ne n$. Multiply the series by one sine, integrate, and every other term vanishes. It's the projection idea of lesson 02.07 in a space of functions.

## The square wave

A square wave switching between $+1$ and $-1$ is odd, so only sines appear:

$$
f(x) = \frac4\pi\Big(\sin x + \frac{\sin3x}{3} + \frac{\sin5x}{5} + \dots\Big) .
$$

Two things to notice:

1. **Decay rate tracks smoothness.** A jump gives coefficients falling like $1/n$; a kink (continuous, slope jumps) like $1/n^2$; smoother still, faster. Sharp edges need high harmonics.
2. **Gibbs phenomenon.** Near a jump the partial sums overshoot by about 9% of the jump, and adding terms only squeezes the overshoot closer to the jump. The peak tends to $1.179$, not 1.

## Parseval's theorem

The mean square of the signal equals the sum of the squares of its harmonics:

$$
\frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L}f^2\,dx = \frac{a_0^2}{4} + \frac12\sum_{n\ge1}(a_n^2 + b_n^2) .
$$

Energy is shared out among harmonics, which is what a power spectrum shows.

Partial sums (lime) against the target (grey). The square wave keeps its overshoot at the jump however many harmonics you add; the triangle wave converges much faster.

:::example A cam that excites a resonance
A cam pushes a follower with a square-wave force of amplitude 100 N at 2 Hz. The follower assembly has a natural frequency of 6 Hz.

1. Harmonics of a square wave: $b_n = \frac{4\cdot100}{n\pi}$ for odd $n$. Fundamental (2 Hz): 127.3 N. Third (6 Hz): 42.4 N. Fifth (10 Hz): 25.5 N.
2. The third harmonic sits exactly on the 6 Hz resonance. With damping ratio $\zeta = 0.05$, the dynamic amplification at resonance is $1/(2\zeta) = 10$, against about $1/(1 - (2/6)^2) = 1.125$ at the fundamental.
3. Response amplitudes in force-equivalent terms: fundamental $127.3\cdot1.125 = 143$ N, third harmonic $42.4\cdot10 = 424$ N.
4. The "small" third harmonic produces three times the motion of the fundamental, and the follower visibly vibrates at 6 Hz. Fixes: change the cam profile to a smooth one with weak third harmonic, or move the resonance.
:::

```python file=fourier_series.py
"""Fourier coefficients by numerical integration, Parseval's theorem and the Gibbs overshoot."""
import math
import numpy as np


def coefficients(f, L, N, m=20000):
    x = -L + 2 * L * (np.arange(m) + 0.5) / m            # midpoint rule, spectrally accurate for periodic f
    fx = f(x); dx = 2 * L / m
    a = [np.sum(fx * np.cos(n * math.pi * x / L)) * dx / L for n in range(N + 1)]
    b = [0.0] + [np.sum(fx * np.sin(n * math.pi * x / L)) * dx / L for n in range(1, N + 1)]
    return np.array(a), np.array(b)


def partial_sum(a, b, L, x):
    s = a[0] / 2 * np.ones_like(x)
    for n in range(1, len(a)):
        s += a[n] * np.cos(n * math.pi * x / L) + b[n] * np.sin(n * math.pi * x / L)
    return s


if __name__ == "__main__":
    square = lambda x: np.sign(np.sin(x))
    a, b = coefficients(square, math.pi, 15)
    expected = [4 / (math.pi * n) if n % 2 else 0.0 for n in range(1, 16)]
    assert np.allclose(b[1:], expected, atol=1e-6) and np.allclose(a, 0, atol=1e-9)
    print("square wave b_1..b_5:", np.round(b[1:6], 4))
    # Parseval: mean square of the square wave is 1
    a, b = coefficients(square, math.pi, 4001)
    assert abs(0.5 * np.sum(b[1:] ** 2) - 1) < 1e-3
    # Gibbs: the peak of the partial sum tends to 1.1790, not 1
    x = np.linspace(1e-6, math.pi - 1e-6, 200001)
    peaks = [partial_sum(*coefficients(square, math.pi, 2 * N), math.pi, x).max() for N in (10, 50)]
    print("Gibbs peaks:", np.round(peaks, 4))
    assert all(abs(p - 1.1790) < 2e-3 for p in peaks)
    # coefficient decay: jump -> 1/n, kink -> 1/n^2
    tri = lambda x: 2 / math.pi * np.arcsin(np.sin(x))
    _, bt = coefficients(tri, math.pi, 21)
    assert abs(abs(bt[1] / bt[21]) - 21 ** 2) < 1 and abs(b[1] / b[21] - 21) < 0.01
    # the cam example: third harmonic at resonance dominates
    F3, F1 = 400 / (3 * math.pi), 400 / math.pi
    print(f"fundamental x 1.125 = {F1 * 1.125:.0f} N, third harmonic x 10 = {F3 * 10:.0f} N")
    assert F3 * 10 > 2.9 * F1 * 1.125
```

:::exercise The sawtooth
Find the Fourier series of $f(x) = x$ on $(-\pi, \pi)$, repeated periodically. How fast do the coefficients decay, and why?
---solution---
1. $f$ is odd, so $a_n = 0$.
2. $b_n = \frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x\sin nx\,dx = \frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x\sin nx\,dx$. Integrating by parts gives $\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$.
3. $f(x) = 2\big(\sin x - \frac{\sin2x}{2} + \frac{\sin3x}{3} - \dots\big)$.
4. The coefficients decay like $1/n$ because the periodic extension jumps from $\pi$ to $-\pi$ at $x = \pm\pi$. At $x = \pi/2$ the series gives Leibniz's $\pi/4 = 1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \dots$.
:::
