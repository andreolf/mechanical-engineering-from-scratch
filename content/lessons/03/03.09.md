Stir a cup of coffee and the fluid rotates. Pull the spoon out and a tiny paddle wheel dropped in still spins. **Curl** measures that local spinning, and **circulation** measures the total swirl around a loop. Stokes' theorem ties them together, and they explain lift on a wing, the vortices shed by a bridge, and why some force fields have a potential and others don't.

## Circulation

The circulation of a velocity field around a closed curve $C$ is

$$
\Gamma = \oint_C\mathbf u\cdot d\mathbf r .
$$

It's the line integral of lesson 03.07 around a loop. For a conservative field it's zero.

## Curl

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf F = \Big(\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial z},\ \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}\Big) .
$$

Its component along $\mathbf n$ is the circulation per unit area around a tiny loop perpendicular to $\mathbf n$. In fluids, $\boldsymbol\omega = \nabla\times\mathbf u$ is the **vorticity**, and for a fluid element in rigid rotation at angular velocity $\Omega$ it equals $2\Omega$: the paddle wheel spins at half the vorticity.

## Stokes' theorem

For a surface $S$ bounded by the curve $C$:

$$
\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dA .
$$

In 2D this is **Green's theorem**: $\oint(P\,dx + Q\,dy) = \iint(\partial_xQ - \partial_yP)\,dA$. It's why the test in lesson 03.07 works: zero curl everywhere inside means zero circulation around every loop.

## Two vortices that look alike

- **Forced vortex** (rigid rotation, like a stirred cup): $u_\theta = \Omega r$. Vorticity $2\Omega$ everywhere, and circulation grows with the enclosed area.
- **Free vortex** (a bathtub drain, a tornado's outer region): $u_\theta = \Gamma/(2\pi r)$. Vorticity zero everywhere except at the centre, yet the circulation around any loop enclosing the centre is $\Gamma$. All the curl is concentrated on the axis.

:::example Circulation in a stirred tank
Fluid in a mixing tank rotates rigidly at $\Omega = 3$ rad/s: $\mathbf u = (-\Omega y, \Omega x)$.

1. Curl: $\partial_xu_y - \partial_yu_x = \Omega - (-\Omega) = 2\Omega = 6$ s⁻¹.
2. Circulation around a circle of radius $R = 0.5$ m: speed $\Omega R = 1.5$ m/s along a length $2\pi R = 3.14$ m gives $\Gamma = 4.712$ m²/s.
3. Stokes: vorticity × area $= 6\cdot\pi\cdot0.25 = 4.712$ m²/s. The same.
4. A free vortex with that same $\Gamma$ would have speed $\Gamma/(2\pi r) = 0.75/r$ m/s: 1.5 m/s at $r = 0.5$ m, but 7.5 m/s at 0.1 m. That's why the water near a drain spins so fast.
:::

```python file=curl.py
"""Circulation around loops and numerical curl; Stokes' theorem for forced and free vortices."""
import math
import numpy as np


def circulation(u, cx, cy, R, n=4000):
    t = 2 * math.pi * (np.arange(n) + 0.5) / n
    x, y = cx + R * np.cos(t), cy + R * np.sin(t)
    ux, uy = u(x, y)
    return np.sum(ux * (-R * np.sin(t)) + uy * (R * np.cos(t))) * 2 * math.pi / n


def curl_z(u, x, y, h=1e-5):
    return (u(x + h, y)[1] - u(x - h, y)[1]) / (2 * h) - (u(x, y + h)[0] - u(x, y - h)[0]) / (2 * h)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    Om = 3.0
    forced = lambda x, y: (-Om * y, Om * x)
    G = circulation(forced, 0, 0, 0.5)
    print(f"forced vortex: circulation {G:.6f} m^2/s, curl x area {curl_z(forced, 0.2, -0.1) * math.pi * 0.25:.6f}")
    assert abs(G - 2 * math.pi * 0.25 * Om) < 1e-10 and abs(curl_z(forced, 0.2, -0.1) - 2 * Om) < 1e-8
    # free vortex: zero curl away from the axis, but circulation Gamma around any loop that encloses it
    Gam = 4.712
    free = lambda x, y: (-Gam / (2 * math.pi) * y / (x * x + y * y), Gam / (2 * math.pi) * x / (x * x + y * y))
    for R in (0.1, 1.0, 5.0):
        assert abs(circulation(free, 0, 0, R) - Gam) < 1e-9
    assert abs(circulation(free, 2.0, 0.0, 0.5)) < 1e-9          # a loop that doesn't enclose the axis
    for p in [(0.3, 0.4), (-1, 2), (2, -0.5)]:
        assert abs(curl_z(free, *p)) < 1e-6
    # Green's theorem on a square for a field with non-uniform curl: F = (-y^3, x^3), curl = 3x^2 + 3y^2
    F = lambda x, y: (-y ** 3, x ** 3)
    s = np.linspace(-1, 1, 200001); ds = s[1] - s[0]
    edges = (np.trapezoid(F(s, -1 + 0 * s)[0], s) + np.trapezoid(F(1 + 0 * s, s)[1], s)
             - np.trapezoid(F(s, 1 + 0 * s)[0], s) - np.trapezoid(F(-1 + 0 * s, s)[1], s))
    assert abs(edges - 8.0) < 1e-6          # double integral of 3x^2 + 3y^2 over [-1, 1]^2 is 8
    print("Stokes and Green verified for forced, free and polynomial fields")
```

:::exercise Is it rotating?
A shear flow near a wall has $\mathbf u = (ky, 0)$ with $k = 50$ s⁻¹. The streamlines are straight lines. Does a small paddle wheel placed in the flow spin? If so, which way and how fast?
---solution---
1. Curl: $\partial_xu_y - \partial_yu_x = 0 - k = -50$ s⁻¹.
2. Nonzero vorticity, so yes: the top of the paddle wheel is pushed faster than the bottom.
3. Negative means clockwise (for $y$ up, $x$ to the right), at an angular speed of half the vorticity: 25 rad/s.
4. Straight streamlines don't mean irrotational flow. Boundary layers are full of vorticity even though the fluid moves in straight lines.
:::
