A car engine's crankshaft does not run on balls. It runs on **plain bearings**: smooth shells with a film of oil a few hundredths of a millimetre thick. When the shaft turns, it drags oil into a narrowing gap, the oil pressure rises, and the shaft floats with no metal contact at all. This is **hydrodynamic lubrication**, and its governing equation was written down by Osborne Reynolds in 1886.

## The Reynolds equation

In a thin film the flow is viscous and nearly parallel to the surfaces (Phase 12 met this as lubrication theory). Integrating the Navier-Stokes equations across the film thickness $h(x)$, and asking that the flow rate be the same at every $x$, gives the one-dimensional **Reynolds equation** for a surface sliding at speed $U$ over a stationary one:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(h^3\frac{dp}{dx}\right) = 6\,\mu\,U\,\frac{dh}{dx}, \qquad p(0) = p(L) = 0 .
$$

The right-hand side is the source of pressure. If $h$ is constant, $dh/dx = 0$ and there is no pressure: **parallel surfaces carry no load**. If the gap narrows in the direction of motion, oil is squeezed into a wedge and the pressure rises.

For a plane pad tapering from $h_{\text{in}}$ to $h_{\text{out}}$, with $K = h_{\text{in}}/h_{\text{out}}$, the load per unit width has a closed form:

$$
W' = \frac{6\mu U L^2}{h_{\text{out}}^2 (K - 1)^2}\left[\ln K - \frac{2(K - 1)}{K + 1}\right] .
$$

The bracket is positive only for $K > 1$ (converging). It peaks at $K \approx 2.2$, where $W' h_{\text{out}}^2/(\mu U L^2) = 0.160$. A diverging film predicts suction, which real oil cannot sustain: it cavitates, so the diverging part simply carries nothing.

The load grows with viscosity and speed and falls with the square of the film thickness. Turn that around: for a given load, a faster or more viscous film floats higher. That is why a bearing that starts and stops under load (with $U \to 0$) is where wear happens.

Assumptions: an infinitely wide pad (no side leakage), constant viscosity, rigid surfaces. A square pad loses roughly half its load through the sides; real design uses 2D charts or numerical solutions.

:::example A tilting-pad thrust bearing
One pad: length 50 mm in the sliding direction, surface speed 10 m/s, hot SAE 30 oil $\mu = 0.03$ Pa·s, minimum film 20 μm, taper $K = 2.2$.

1. Finite differences on 2001 points give a peak pressure of 9.588 MPa and a load of 300.45 kN per metre of width, the same as the closed form to five figures.
2. For a 50 mm wide pad that is about 15.0 kN, before the side-leakage correction.
3. With a parallel film ($K = 1$) the load is zero; with a diverging film the solver gives a minimum of −37.5 MPa of suction, which cavitates in practice.
4. Double the speed to 20 m/s: the load doubles to 600.9 kN/m. Double the film to 40 μm at the same speed: the load falls by 4 to 75.1 kN/m.
:::

```python file=reynolds.py
"""One-dimensional Reynolds equation for a plane slider bearing: finite-difference pressure against the closed-form solution, and why only a converging film carries load."""
import math
import numpy as np


def film(x, L, h_in, h_out):
    return h_in + (h_out - h_in) * x / L


def reynolds_fd(L, U, mu, h_in, h_out, n=2001):
    """d/dx(h^3 dp/dx) = 6 mu U dh/dx with p = 0 at both ends (surface moving at U in +x)."""
    x = np.linspace(0, L, n)
    dx = x[1] - x[0]
    h = film(x, L, h_in, h_out)
    hm = film(0.5 * (x[1:] + x[:-1]), L, h_in, h_out) ** 3  # h^3 at mid-points
    A = np.zeros((n, n)); b = np.zeros(n)
    A[0, 0] = A[-1, -1] = 1.0
    for i in range(1, n - 1):
        A[i, i - 1] = hm[i - 1]
        A[i, i] = -(hm[i - 1] + hm[i])
        A[i, i + 1] = hm[i]
        b[i] = 6 * mu * U * (film(x[i] + dx / 2, L, h_in, h_out) - film(x[i] - dx / 2, L, h_in, h_out)) * dx
    p = np.linalg.solve(A, b)
    return x, p


def trapz(y, x):
    return float(np.sum(0.5 * (y[1:] + y[:-1]) * np.diff(x)))


def load_closed_form(L, U, mu, h_in, h_out):
    """Load per unit width (N/m) of an inclined pad, K = h_in/h_out."""
    K = h_in / h_out
    if abs(K - 1) < 1e-12:
        return 0.0
    return 6 * mu * U * L**2 / (h_out**2 * (K - 1) ** 2) * (math.log(K) - 2 * (K - 1) / (K + 1))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    L, U, mu, h_out = 0.05, 10.0, 0.03, 20e-6  # 50 mm pad, 10 m/s, hot SAE 30 oil, 20 um minimum film
    for K in (2.2, 1.0, 0.5):
        x, p = reynolds_fd(L, U, mu, K * h_out, h_out)
        W = trapz(p, x)
        Wc = load_closed_form(L, U, mu, K * h_out, h_out)
        print(f"K = {K}: peak pressure {p.max()/1e6:.3f} MPa, min {p.min()/1e6:.3f} MPa, load {W/1000:.2f} kN per m width (closed form {Wc/1000:.2f})")
        assert abs(W - Wc) <= 1e-4 * max(abs(Wc), 1.0)
        if K > 1:
            assert W > 0 and p.min() >= -1e-6
        elif K == 1:
            assert abs(W) < 1e-6 * 1e6
        else:
            assert W < 0  # diverging film: suction, which real oil cannot hold (it cavitates)
    # best taper ratio: the load coefficient W h_out^2/(mu U L^2) peaks near K = 2.2
    Ks = np.linspace(1.05, 5, 4000)
    coef = [load_closed_form(1, 1, 1, k, 1) for k in Ks]
    kbest = Ks[int(np.argmax(coef))]
    print(f"best K = {kbest:.3f}, load coefficient {max(coef):.5f}")
    assert 2.1 < kbest < 2.3
```

:::exercise A different taper
For the same pad, what is the load per metre width for $K = 1.5$ and for $K = 3$? Why is the best taper a compromise?
---solution---
1. Use $W' = 6\mu U L^2/(h_{\text{out}}^2 (K-1)^2)\,[\ln K - 2(K-1)/(K+1)]$ with $6\mu U L^2/h_{\text{out}}^2 = 6\cdot0.03\cdot10\cdot0.0025/(4\times10^{-10}) = 1.125\times10^7$ N/m.
2. $K = 1.5$: bracket $0.405465 - 0.4 = 0.005465$, divided by 0.25: 245.9 kN/m.
3. $K = 3$: bracket $1.098612 - 1 = 0.098612$, divided by 4: 277.3 kN/m.
4. A small taper builds little pressure; a large taper lets most of the oil flow back out of the wide inlet. The optimum, $K \approx 2.2$, gives 300.4 kN/m.
:::
