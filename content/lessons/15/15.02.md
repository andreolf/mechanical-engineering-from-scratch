A **safety factor** is the ratio of strength to stress, $n = R/S$. Codes give values such as 1.5 or 2, and for a part with well-known loads and material that is fine. But strength and stress are both **scattered**: one batch of aluminium is stronger than the next, and one rider hits a pothole harder than another. Two designs with the same safety factor can have very different chances of failing. **Stress-strength interference** puts a number on that chance.

## Two overlapping distributions

Model strength $R$ and stress $S$ as independent normal variables with means $\mu_R, \mu_S$ and standard deviations $\sigma_R, \sigma_S$. The part fails when $R < S$, that is when the margin $M = R - S$ is negative. A difference of independent normals is normal, with

$$
\mu_M = \mu_R - \mu_S, \qquad \sigma_M = \sqrt{\sigma_R^2 + \sigma_S^2} .
$$

So the probability of failure is

$$
P_f = P(M < 0) = \Phi(-z), \qquad z = \frac{\mu_R - \mu_S}{\sqrt{\sigma_R^2 + \sigma_S^2}},
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution and $z$ is the **reliability index**. The central safety factor $\mu_R/\mu_S$ does not appear on its own: scatter matters as much as the mean margin.

**Monte Carlo** checks this without any formula: draw a few million $(R, S)$ pairs and count how often $R < S$. The count has a binomial standard error $\sqrt{P_f(1-P_f)/N}$, and the estimate should land within a few of those of $\Phi(-z)$.

Assumptions: normal distributions, independence, and a single failure mode. Real strength data has a lower tail that is often thinner than normal (materials are proof-tested), and loads often have a fatter one, so for very small $P_f$ the tails matter more than the formula suggests.

:::example An aluminium handlebar clamp
Strength 300 MPa with 8% scatter ($\sigma_R = 24$ MPa); peak stress in use 180 MPa with 15% scatter ($\sigma_S = 27$ MPa).

1. Central safety factor $300/180 = 1.667$.
2. $z = 120/\sqrt{24^2 + 27^2} = 120/36.12 = 3.3218$, so $P_f = \Phi(-3.3218) = 4.47\times10^{-4}$: about 1 clamp in 2240.
3. Two million Monte Carlo pairs give $4.68\times10^{-4} \pm 0.15\times10^{-4}$, within 1.4 standard errors.
4. Double both scatters, keep the means: the safety factor is still 1.667, but $z = 1.661$ and $P_f = 0.0484$, about 1 in 21.
5. For $P_f = 10^{-6}$ we need $z = 4.7534$. With strength scatter fixed at 8% of its mean, the mean strength must be 370.6 MPa, a safety factor of 2.06.
:::

```python file=interference.py
"""Stress-strength interference: probability of failure from two normal distributions, analytic against Monte Carlo, and why the same safety factor can hide very different risks."""
import math
import numpy as np


def Phi(x):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2)))


def Phi_inv(p, lo=-12.0, hi=12.0):
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if Phi(mid) < p:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return 0.5 * (lo + hi)


def pf_normal(muR, sR, muS, sS):
    """Strength R and stress S independent normals: failure when R - S < 0."""
    z = (muR - muS) / math.hypot(sR, sS)
    return z, Phi(-z)


def pf_monte_carlo(muR, sR, muS, sS, n=2_000_000, seed=1):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    R = rng.normal(muR, sR, n)
    S = rng.normal(muS, sS, n)
    p = np.mean(R < S)
    return p, math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)


def strength_needed(pf, sR_cov, muS, sS):
    """Mean strength giving failure probability pf when strength scatter is a fixed fraction of its mean."""
    z = -Phi_inv(pf)
    lo, hi = muS, 20 * muS
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if (mid - muS) / math.hypot(sR_cov * mid, sS) < z:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return 0.5 * (lo + hi)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # aluminium handlebar clamp: strength 300 MPa, 8% scatter; peak stress 180 MPa, 15% scatter
    muR, sR, muS, sS = 300.0, 24.0, 180.0, 27.0
    z, pf = pf_normal(muR, sR, muS, sS)
    pmc, se = pf_monte_carlo(muR, sR, muS, sS)
    print(f"central safety factor {muR/muS:.3f}, reliability index z = {z:.4f}")
    print(f"analytic P_f = {pf:.3e}, Monte Carlo {pmc:.3e} +- {se:.1e}")
    assert abs(pmc - pf) < 4 * se
    # same central safety factor, twice the scatter: the risk explodes
    z2, pf2 = pf_normal(muR, 2 * sR, muS, 2 * sS)
    p2, se2 = pf_monte_carlo(muR, 2 * sR, muS, 2 * sS, seed=2)
    print(f"double scatter, same factor: z = {z2:.4f}, P_f = {pf2:.4f} (Monte Carlo {p2:.4f})")
    assert abs(p2 - pf2) < 4 * se2 and pf2 > 50 * pf
    # design target: one failure in a million clamps
    m = strength_needed(1e-6, 0.08, muS, sS)
    z6, pf6 = pf_normal(m, 0.08 * m, muS, sS)
    print(f"strength for P_f = 1e-6: {m:.1f} MPa (safety factor {m/muS:.3f}, z = {z6:.4f})")
    assert abs(pf6 - 1e-6) < 1e-9
```

:::exercise A heavier rider
In the clamp example the mean stress rises to 200 MPa, everything else unchanged. Find $z$ and $P_f$.
---solution---
1. $z = (300 - 200)/36.12 = 2.768$.
2. $P_f = \Phi(-2.768) = 2.82\times10^{-3}$, about 1 in 355.
3. An 11% rise in load raised the failure probability more than sixfold. That steep tail is why reliability targets need margin.
:::
