A brake and a clutch are the same device used two ways: two surfaces pressed together so that friction carries a torque. A bicycle disc brake converts the bike's kinetic energy into heat in the rotor; a car clutch connects a spinning engine to a stationary gearbox. The design question is the same for both: how much torque does a given clamping force produce?

## Friction torque on an annulus

A friction face between radii $r_i$ and $r_o$ carries a contact pressure $p(r)$. The clamp force and the torque are

$$
F = \int_{r_i}^{r_o} p\,2\pi r\,dr, \qquad T = \int_{r_i}^{r_o} \mu\,p\,r\,2\pi r\,dr .
$$

The answer depends on how the pressure is distributed, and there are two classic assumptions:

1. **Uniform pressure** (new, perfectly flat, rigid surfaces): $p$ is constant, and
$$
T = \frac{2}{3}\,\mu F\,\frac{r_o^3 - r_i^3}{r_o^2 - r_i^2} .
$$
2. **Uniform wear** (after running in): wear is proportional to pressure times sliding speed, and speed is proportional to $r$. Faces that stay flat wear equally everywhere only if $p\,r$ is constant, so the outer region carries less pressure. Then
$$
T = \mu F\,\frac{r_o + r_i}{2} .
$$

Uniform wear always gives the smaller torque, because it shifts pressure inwards to smaller radii. Designers use it because it is the conservative, long-term value. The difference is small for narrow faces ($r_i/r_o$ near 1) and grows for wide ones. A clutch with several plates multiplies the torque by the number of friction faces. A disc brake has two faces, one on each side of the rotor. A pad covers only a sector of the annulus, but the sector angle cancels from both formulas.

**Hydraulic actuation.** The lever multiplies the hand force, and the hydraulic system multiplies it again by the ratio of piston areas: $F_{\text{pad}} = F_{\text{hand}}\cdot(\text{lever ratio})\cdot A_{\text{caliper}}/A_{\text{master}}$.

:::example A hydraulic bicycle disc brake
160 mm rotor with pads from 62 to 78 mm radius; 100 N at the lever, 4 : 1 lever ratio, 10 mm master piston, two 22 mm caliper pistons on each side; resin pads with $\mu = 0.40$.

1. Line pressure $400/(\pi\cdot10^2/4) = 5.093$ MPa; clamp force per side $5.093\cdot2\cdot380.1 = 3872.0$ N.
2. Uniform wear: $T = 2\cdot0.40\cdot3872.0\cdot0.070 = 216.83$ N·m (two faces, mean radius 70 mm).
3. Uniform pressure: effective radius $\tfrac23(78^3 - 62^3)/(78^2 - 62^2) = 70.30$ mm, so $T = 217.78$ N·m. Numerical integration of both pressure laws matches to six figures.
4. At the tyre (radius 0.35 m): $216.83/0.35 = 620$ N, a deceleration of 6.88 m/s² for 90 kg. That is close to the limit at which the rear wheel lifts, so a front brake never needs more.
5. Stopping from 40 km/h dissipates $\tfrac12\cdot90\cdot11.11^2 = 5.56$ kJ, mostly in the small steel rotor. That heat is why long descents fade brakes.
:::

```python file=brake.py
"""Disc brakes and clutches: torque under uniform pressure and under uniform wear, by integrating the pressure over the friction face, for a hydraulic bicycle disc brake."""
import math
import numpy as np


def torque_numeric(F, mu, ri, ro, law, n=20000):
    """Integrate dT = mu p r dA over an annulus (or any sector: the angle cancels). law: 'pressure' or 'wear'."""
    r = ri + (ro - ri) * (np.arange(n) + 0.5) / n
    dr = (ro - ri) / n
    shape = np.ones_like(r) if law == "pressure" else 1 / r  # uniform wear: p r = const
    p = F * shape / np.sum(shape * 2 * math.pi * r * dr)        # scale so the pressure integrates to F
    return float(np.sum(mu * p * r * 2 * math.pi * r * dr))


def torque_uniform_pressure(F, mu, ri, ro):
    return 2 / 3 * mu * F * (ro**3 - ri**3) / (ro**2 - ri**2)


def torque_uniform_wear(F, mu, ri, ro):
    return mu * F * (ro + ri) / 2


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # 160 mm rotor, pads from 62 to 78 mm radius; lever 100 N with a 4:1 lever, 10 mm master piston, 2 x 22 mm pistons per side
    lever, ratio, d_master, d_caliper, mu = 100.0, 4.0, 10.0, 22.0, 0.40
    p = lever * ratio / (math.pi * d_master**2 / 4)                 # MPa
    F = p * 2 * math.pi * d_caliper**2 / 4                          # clamp force on each pad, N
    ri, ro = 62.0, 78.0
    Tw = 2 * torque_uniform_wear(F, mu, ri, ro)                     # two friction faces
    Tp = 2 * torque_uniform_pressure(F, mu, ri, ro)
    Tw_n = 2 * torque_numeric(F, mu, ri, ro, "wear")
    Tp_n = 2 * torque_numeric(F, mu, ri, ro, "pressure")
    print(f"line pressure {p:.3f} MPa, clamp force {F:.1f} N per side")
    print(f"brake torque: uniform wear {Tw/1000:.3f} N m (numeric {Tw_n/1000:.3f}), uniform pressure {Tp/1000:.3f} N m (numeric {Tp_n/1000:.3f})")
    assert abs(Tw_n / Tw - 1) < 1e-6 and abs(Tp_n / Tp - 1) < 1e-6
    assert Tw <= Tp  # the worn-in (uniform wear) estimate is always the lower, safer one
    for ri2 in (10.0, 40.0, 70.0):
        assert torque_uniform_wear(1, 1, ri2, ro) <= torque_uniform_pressure(1, 1, ri2, ro)
    R_wheel, m = 0.35, 90.0
    Fx = Tw / 1000 / R_wheel
    print(f"tyre braking force {Fx:.0f} N, deceleration {Fx/m:.2f} m/s^2 for 90 kg with this one brake")
    # stopping from 40 km/h: the rotor absorbs the kinetic energy
    E = 0.5 * m * (40 / 3.6) ** 2
    print(f"energy to stop from 40 km/h: {E/1000:.2f} kJ")
```

:::exercise A car clutch
A single-plate dry clutch has friction faces from 75 to 120 mm radius, a spring force of 4 kN and $\mu = 0.3$, with two faces. Find the torque capacity under both assumptions.
---solution---
1. Uniform wear: $T = 2\cdot0.3\cdot4000\cdot(0.120 + 0.075)/2 = 234.0$ N·m.
2. Uniform pressure: effective radius $\tfrac23(120^3 - 75^3)/(120^2 - 75^2) = 99.23$ mm, so $T = 2\cdot0.3\cdot4000\cdot0.09923 = 238.2$ N·m.
3. Design on 234 N·m. The wider face makes the difference larger than on the brake pad (1.8% here, against 0.4%).
:::
