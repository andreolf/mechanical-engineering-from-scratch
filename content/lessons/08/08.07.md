On its opening day in 2000, London's Millennium Bridge began to sway sideways under the crowd and was closed two days later. The fix included dozens of tuned mass dampers. Taipei 101 has a 660 t steel ball hanging near its top for the same reason. A tuned mass damper (TMD) is a small oscillator attached to a big one, tuned so that it absorbs the big one's resonance.

## How it works

Attach a mass $m_2$ on a spring $k_2$ and damper $c_2$ to the structure's modal mass $m_1$. Near the structure's natural frequency the TMD swings strongly and out of phase. Its spring then pushes back on the structure, cancelling much of the forcing, and its damper turns that motion into heat. The pair is a two-DOF system (08.06) with two resonances instead of one tall one.

With the **mass ratio** $\mu = m_2/m_1$, tuning ratio $f = \omega_2/\omega_1$ and TMD damping $\zeta_2$, the structure's response comes from the 2×2 complex system

$$
\begin{bmatrix} k_1 + k_2 - m_1\omega^2 + i\omega(c_1 + c_2) & -k_2 - i\omega c_2 \\ -k_2 - i\omega c_2 & k_2 - m_2\omega^2 + i\omega c_2 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} X_1 \\ X_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} F_0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} .
$$

## Den Hartog's tuning

For an undamped structure, J. P. Den Hartog found in 1934 that every response curve passes through two fixed points, whatever the TMD damping. The best design makes them equal in height and puts the peaks on them:

$$
f_{opt} = \frac{1}{1+\mu}, \qquad \zeta_{2,opt} = \sqrt{\frac{3\mu}{8(1+\mu)^3}}, \qquad \left(\frac{X_1}{F_0/k_1}\right)_{max} \approx \sqrt{1 + \frac2\mu} .
$$

A heavier TMD works better and is less sensitive to mistuning, but it costs space and money. Typical mass ratios are 1 to 5 %.

:::example A footbridge TMD
First vertical mode at 2.0 Hz (walking pace), modal mass 50 t, structural damping $\zeta_1 = 0.5$ %.

1. Without a TMD the peak amplification is $1/(2\zeta_1) = 100$.
2. $\mu = 0.02$, a 1000 kg TMD. Den Hartog: tuned to $2.0/1.02 = 1.9608$ Hz with $\zeta_2 = 0.0841$. The peak drops to 9.34 (the undamped-bridge formula gives 10.05).
3. SciPy's Nelder-Mead, minimising the peak directly with the bridge's own damping included, finds $f = 0.9794$, $\zeta_2 = 0.0866$ and a peak of 9.25. Den Hartog is within 1 %.
4. If the bridge turns out to be at 2.10 Hz instead of 2.00 Hz, the same TMD gives a peak of 14.22. A TMD tuned to three times the bridge frequency does nothing: 99.62.
5. Mass ratio 1 % gives a peak of 12.74; 5 % gives 6.14.
:::

```python file=tmd.py
"""Tuned mass damper on a footbridge: Den Hartog tuning, then a numerical optimum with SciPy."""
import math
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize


def response(r, mu, f, zt, zs=0.0):
    """|X1| / (F0 / k1) of the structure (mass ratio mu, TMD frequency ratio f, TMD damping zt, structural damping zs)
    at forcing ratio r = w / w1, from the 2x2 complex frequency-response solve."""
    out = np.empty_like(r)
    for i, ri in enumerate(r):
        # units: m1 = 1, k1 = 1, w1 = 1
        m2 = mu; k2 = mu * f**2; c2 = 2 * zt * mu * f; c1 = 2 * zs
        A = np.array([[1 + k2 - ri**2 + 1j * ri * (c1 + c2), -k2 - 1j * ri * c2],
                      [-k2 - 1j * ri * c2, k2 - m2 * ri**2 + 1j * ri * c2]])
        out[i] = abs(np.linalg.solve(A, [1.0, 0.0])[0])
    return out


R = np.linspace(0.7, 1.3, 3001)
peak = lambda mu, f, zt, zs: response(R, mu, f, zt, zs).max()

if __name__ == "__main__":
    M1, f1, zs = 50e3, 2.0, 0.005             # modal mass (kg), first vertical mode (Hz), steel bridge damping
    mu = 0.02
    print(f"no TMD: peak amplification 1/(2 zeta) = {1/(2*zs):.1f}")
    f_dh = 1 / (1 + mu); z_dh = math.sqrt(3 * mu / (8 * (1 + mu)**3))
    p_dh = peak(mu, f_dh, z_dh, zs)
    print(f"Den Hartog, mu = {mu}: TMD mass {mu*M1:.0f} kg, tuned to {f_dh*f1:.4f} Hz, damping {z_dh:.4f}; peak {p_dh:.2f} (formula sqrt(1 + 2/mu) = {math.sqrt(1+2/mu):.2f} for an undamped bridge)")
    res = minimize(lambda q: peak(mu, q[0], q[1], zs), [f_dh, z_dh], method="Nelder-Mead", options={"xatol": 1e-5, "fatol": 1e-4})
    print(f"numerical optimum: f = {res.x[0]:.4f}, zeta = {res.x[1]:.4f}, peak {res.fun:.2f}")
    assert res.fun <= p_dh + 1e-9 and res.fun < 1 / (2 * zs) / 5
    # mistuning: the TMD loses much of its effect if the bridge frequency is off by 5 %
    p_off = peak(mu, f_dh / 1.05, z_dh, zs)
    print(f"TMD tuned for 2.00 Hz on a bridge that is really at 2.10 Hz: peak {p_off:.2f}")
    # untuned (TMD at the wrong frequency or locked) for comparison
    p_lock = peak(mu, 3.0, z_dh, zs)
    print(f"TMD tuned to 3 times the bridge frequency: peak {p_lock:.2f}")
    assert p_dh < p_off < p_lock
    for m in (0.01, 0.05):
        print(f"mu = {m}: Den Hartog peak {peak(m, 1/(1+m), math.sqrt(3*m/(8*(1+m)**3)), zs):.2f}")
```

:::exercise Sizing a TMD
A floor has a 1.5 Hz mode with a modal mass of 30 t. You want a peak amplification of no more than about 12. What mass ratio, TMD mass, tuned frequency and TMD damping does Den Hartog suggest?
---solution---
1. $\sqrt{1 + 2/\mu} = 12$ gives $2/\mu = 143$, so $\mu = 0.01399$, about 1.4 %.
2. TMD mass $0.01399\cdot30000 = 420$ kg.
3. Tuning $1.5/1.01399 = 1.479$ Hz.
4. $\zeta_2 = \sqrt{3\cdot0.01399/(8\cdot1.01399^3)} = 0.0709$.
:::
