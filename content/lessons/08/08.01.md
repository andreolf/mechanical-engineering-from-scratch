Pluck a guitar string, tap a wine glass, hit a bronze key of a Balinese gamelan or drive a car over a speed bump. Each one rings at its own frequency. Almost every vibration problem in engineering starts from one model: a mass on a spring.

## Equation of motion

A mass $m$ sits on a spring of stiffness $k$. Measure $x$ from the static equilibrium position, so gravity is already balanced by the spring's preload and drops out. Displace the mass and the spring pushes back with $-kx$. Newton's second law gives

$$
m\ddot x + kx = 0 .
$$

Try $x = A\cos(\omega t) + B\sin(\omega t)$. Substituting gives $(k - m\omega^2)x = 0$, so motion is possible only at

$$
\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad f_n = \frac{\omega_n}{2\pi}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} .
$$

$A$ and $B$ come from the start: $A = x_0$ and $B = v_0/\omega_n$.

## What the formula says

- Stiffer means higher frequency; heavier means lower, both as square roots. Doubling the mass lengthens the period by $\sqrt2$.
- The frequency doesn't depend on the amplitude. A linear spring gives the same period for small and large swings, which is why a tuning fork holds its pitch as it fades.
- Energy swaps between the spring and the mass: $\tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2$ stays constant.

## The static-deflection shortcut

Under gravity the spring sags by $\delta = mg/k$. So $k/m = g/\delta$ and

$$
f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}} .
$$

Measure how much something sags and you know its natural frequency without weighing it. A sag of 25 mm gives about 3.2 Hz; 250 mm gives 1 Hz.

:::example A quarter of a car
One corner of a car carries $m = 300$ kg on a spring of $k = 30$ kN/m (ignore the damper for now).

1. $\omega_n = \sqrt{30000/300} = 10.000$ rad/s, so $f_n = 1.5915$ Hz and $T = 0.6283$ s.
2. Simulating 10 s with velocity Verlet at $\Delta t = 1$ ms and measuring zero crossings gives 0.628316 s against $2\pi\sqrt{m/k} = 0.628319$ s.
3. Released from 50 mm the energy is 37.5 J, conserved to $2.5\times10^{-5}$. Releasing from 1 mm or 200 mm gives the same period.
4. Static sag $300\cdot9.81/30000 = 98.1$ mm, and $\sqrt{9.81/0.0981}/2\pi = 1.5915$ Hz, the same answer.
5. Doubling the mass to 600 kg gives a period of 0.8886 s, a ratio of 1.4142.

Car suspensions are tuned to about 1 to 1.5 Hz, close to the rhythm of walking, which people find comfortable.
:::

```python file=oscillator.py
"""The undamped harmonic oscillator: simulate m x'' + k x = 0 and measure the period."""
import math
import numpy as np


def simulate(m, k, x0, v0, t_end, dt):
    """Velocity Verlet: second order and energy-stable for oscillators."""
    n = int(round(t_end / dt)); x = np.empty(n + 1); v = np.empty(n + 1)
    x[0], v[0] = x0, v0; a = -k / m * x0
    for i in range(n):
        x[i + 1] = x[i] + dt * v[i] + 0.5 * dt * dt * a
        a_new = -k / m * x[i + 1]
        v[i + 1] = v[i] + 0.5 * dt * (a + a_new); a = a_new
    return np.arange(n + 1) * dt, x, v


def measured_period(t, x):
    """Average spacing of upward zero crossings, with linear interpolation between samples."""
    i = np.where((x[:-1] < 0) & (x[1:] >= 0))[0]
    tc = t[i] - x[i] * (t[i + 1] - t[i]) / (x[i + 1] - x[i])
    return np.mean(np.diff(tc))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    m, k = 300.0, 30e3                     # quarter of a car on its spring
    wn = math.sqrt(k / m)
    t, x, v = simulate(m, k, 0.05, 0.0, 10.0, 1e-3)
    T = measured_period(t, x)
    print(f"m = {m:.0f} kg, k = {k/1e3:.0f} kN/m: omega_n = {wn:.3f} rad/s, f_n = {wn/(2*math.pi):.4f} Hz")
    print(f"measured period {T:.6f} s, formula 2 pi sqrt(m/k) = {2*math.pi*math.sqrt(m/k):.6f} s")
    assert abs(T - 2 * math.pi * math.sqrt(m / k)) < 1e-5
    E = 0.5 * m * v**2 + 0.5 * k * x**2
    print(f"energy {E[0]:.4f} J, worst relative change {np.max(np.abs(E / E[0] - 1)):.1e}")
    assert np.max(np.abs(E / E[0] - 1)) < 1e-4
    # amplitude does not change the period (linear system)
    for x0 in (0.001, 0.2):
        assert abs(measured_period(*simulate(m, k, x0, 0, 10, 1e-3)[:2]) - T) < 1e-9
    # static deflection shortcut: f_n = sqrt(g / delta) / (2 pi)
    delta = m * 9.81 / k
    print(f"static sag {delta*1000:.1f} mm gives f_n = {math.sqrt(9.81/delta)/(2*math.pi):.4f} Hz")
    # doubling the mass lowers the frequency by sqrt 2
    T2 = measured_period(*simulate(2 * m, k, 0.05, 0, 10, 1e-3)[:2])
    print(f"double the mass: period {T2:.4f} s, ratio {T2/T:.4f}")
    assert abs(T2 / T - math.sqrt(2)) < 1e-4
```

:::exercise A gamelan key
A bronze gamelan key rings at 440 Hz. Treat its first mode as a mass-spring with an effective mass of 0.12 kg. What effective stiffness does that imply? If the maker grinds the key so its effective mass drops by 4 % with the same stiffness, what is the new pitch?
---solution---
1. $k = m\omega_n^2 = 0.12\cdot(2\pi\cdot440)^2 = 9.17\times10^5$ N/m.
2. $f \propto 1/\sqrt m$, so $f = 440/\sqrt{0.96} = 449.1$ Hz.
3. Real tuning is subtler: grinding the middle of a bar reduces its stiffness more than its mass and lowers the pitch, while grinding the ends raises it.
:::
