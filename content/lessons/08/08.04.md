Often the force doesn't act on the mass. Instead the ground moves: a bike wheel rolling over cobbles, a car on a rough road, a laboratory microscope on a floor shaken by traffic. The spring and damper carry that motion up to the mass. Choose them well and most of the shaking never arrives. That's **vibration isolation**.

## Equation of motion

The base moves as $y(t)$ and the mass as $x(t)$. The spring and damper respond to the relative motion:

$$
m\ddot x + c(\dot x - \dot y) + k(x - y) = 0 .
$$

For $y = Y\sin\omega t$ the mass moves at the same frequency with amplitude $X$. The **transmissibility** is

$$
T = \frac{X}{Y} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}{(1-r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}, \qquad r = \frac{\omega}{\omega_n} .
$$

The same formula gives the force passed into the ground by a vibrating machine on mounts, $F_T/F_0$. So isolating a sensitive instrument from the floor, and the floor from a noisy machine, are the same problem.

## The √2 rule

Set $T = 1$ and solve: $r = \sqrt2$, whatever the damping. Below $\sqrt2\,\omega_n$ the mount amplifies the motion; above it, it isolates. So an isolator must be **soft**: its natural frequency well below the disturbing frequency, ideally $r \ge 3$.

Damping cuts both ways. It limits the peak at resonance (which every machine passes through as it starts) but makes isolation worse above $\sqrt2$, because the damper itself pushes the mass along with the base.

:::example A mountain bike on cobbles
Rider and frame $m = 80$ kg on a suspension of $k = 25$ kN/m with $\zeta = 0.3$. Cobbles 0.25 m apart, 10 mm from peak to trough ($Y = 5$ mm).

1. $f_n = \sqrt{25000/80}/2\pi = 2.813$ Hz; $c = 849$ N s/m.
2. At 2 m/s the cobbles come at 8 Hz, $r = 2.843$: $T = 0.2714$. Simulating 6 s with RK4 gives 0.2714. At 5 m/s (20 Hz, $r = 7.109$): $T = 0.0881$, simulated 0.0881.
3. Damping trade-off. At resonance $T$ = 5.10, 1.94 and 1.30 for $\zeta$ = 0.1, 0.3 and 0.6. At $r = 3$: 0.145, 0.251 and 0.426.
4. A single 30 mm half-sine bump 0.3 m long: the rider rises 39.8 mm at 1 m/s (slow bump, overshoot), 25.3 mm at 3 m/s and only 10.2 mm at 8 m/s. Fast riding over sharp bumps is smoother.
:::

```python file=isolation.py
"""Base excitation: transmissibility of a mountain-bike suspension, checked by time simulation."""
import math
import numpy as np


def transmissibility(r, zeta):
    """|X / Y| for a mass on a spring and damper whose base moves as Y sin(w t)."""
    return np.sqrt((1 + (2 * zeta * r)**2) / ((1 - r**2)**2 + (2 * zeta * r)**2))


def simulate_base(m, c, k, y, yd, t_end, dt):
    """RK4 on m x'' = -c (x' - y') - k (x - y) for a prescribed base motion y(t)."""
    f = lambda t, s: np.array([s[1], (-c * (s[1] - yd(t)) - k * (s[0] - y(t))) / m])
    s = np.zeros(2); t = 0.0; xs = [0.0]
    for _ in range(int(round(t_end / dt))):
        k1 = f(t, s); k2 = f(t + dt/2, s + dt/2*k1); k3 = f(t + dt/2, s + dt/2*k2); k4 = f(t + dt, s + dt*k3)
        s = s + dt/6*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4); t += dt; xs.append(s[0])
    return np.arange(len(xs)) * dt, np.array(xs)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    m, k, zeta = 80.0, 25e3, 0.3               # rider and frame on the suspension
    wn = math.sqrt(k / m); c = 2 * zeta * math.sqrt(k * m)
    print(f"f_n = {wn/(2*math.pi):.3f} Hz, c = {c:.0f} N s/m")
    # the crossover at r = sqrt 2 holds for every damping ratio
    for z in (0.05, 0.3, 0.7):
        assert abs(transmissibility(math.sqrt(2), z) - 1) < 1e-12
        r = np.linspace(1.4143, 10, 2000); assert np.all(transmissibility(r, z) < 1)
    # cobbles 0.25 m apart, 10 mm high, ridden at 2 and 5 m/s
    for v in (2.0, 5.0):
        w = 2 * math.pi * v / 0.25; Y = 0.005
        Tr = transmissibility(w / wn, zeta)
        t, x = simulate_base(m, c, k, lambda t: Y * math.sin(w * t), lambda t: Y * w * math.cos(w * t), 6.0, 2e-4)
        Xs = (x[t > 4].max() - x[t > 4].min()) / 2
        print(f"{v} m/s: cobble frequency {w/(2*math.pi):.1f} Hz, r = {w/wn:.3f}, transmissibility {Tr:.4f} (simulated {Xs/Y:.4f})")
        assert abs(Xs / Y / Tr - 1) < 5e-3
    # more damping helps at resonance and hurts above sqrt 2
    for z in (0.1, 0.3, 0.6):
        print(f"zeta {z}: T at resonance {transmissibility(1.0, z):.3f}, at r = 3 {transmissibility(3.0, z):.4f}")
    # a single 30 mm half-sine bump 0.3 m long
    for v in (1.0, 3.0, 8.0):
        L, H = 0.3, 0.03; tb = L / v
        y = lambda t: H * math.sin(math.pi * t / tb) if 0 <= t <= tb else 0.0
        yd = lambda t: H * math.pi / tb * math.cos(math.pi * t / tb) if 0 <= t <= tb else 0.0
        t, x = simulate_base(m, c, k, y, yd, 2.0, 1e-4)
        print(f"bump at {v} m/s: rider rises {x.max()*1000:.1f} mm (bump is 30 mm)")
```

:::exercise Isolating a compressor
A 120 kg compressor runs at 1800 rpm. You want only 10 % of its shaking force to reach the floor. Ignoring damping, what natural frequency, and so what total mount stiffness, do you need? How much will the mounts sag?
---solution---
1. With $\zeta = 0$, $T = 1/(r^2 - 1) = 0.1$, so $r^2 = 11$ and $r = 3.317$.
2. Running frequency 30 Hz, so $f_n = 30/3.317 = 9.045$ Hz.
3. $k = m(2\pi f_n)^2 = 120\cdot(56.83)^2 = 3.876\times10^5$ N/m in total.
4. Static sag $mg/k = 3.04$ mm. Isolators are often chosen straight from a sag chart for this reason.
:::
