A violin string bowed steadily, a car brake squealing, a wet finger singing on a wine glass, power lines galloping in freezing wind, the Tacoma Narrows bridge twisting itself apart in 1940. None of these has a periodic force pushing it. The energy comes from a steady source, and the vibration itself controls when it's taken in. That's **self-excited vibration**.

## Negative damping

Write the motion as $m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = 0$. If something makes the effective $c$ negative, a small disturbance grows exponentially instead of dying out. Friction that falls with sliding speed (brakes, bowed strings, stick-slip in machine slides) does this. So do aerodynamic forces that grow with the motion (galloping, flutter).

But growth can't go on for ever. At larger amplitude the extra energy input saturates, or other losses grow faster. The motion settles on a **limit cycle**: a closed orbit in the phase plane that attracts every nearby trajectory, from inside and outside.

## The Van der Pol oscillator

The classic model, first written for vacuum-tube circuits in 1926:

$$
\ddot x - \mu(1 - x^2)\dot x + x = 0 .
$$

For $|x| < 1$ the damping term is negative and pumps energy in; for $|x| > 1$ it is positive and takes energy out. Over one cycle on the limit cycle the two balance exactly.

- Small $\mu$: nearly sinusoidal, amplitude 2, period $2\pi$.
- $\mu = 1$: amplitude 2.0086, period 6.6633.
- Large $\mu$: a **relaxation oscillation** of slow creeps and sudden snaps. The period approaches $(3 - 2\ln2)\mu$. Squeaking doors and stick-slip look like this.

The amplitude is set by the system, not the start. That's the signature of self-excitation, and the opposite of a linear oscillator, whose amplitude is whatever you started it with.

:::example Converging to the limit cycle
$\mu = 1$, RK4 with $\Delta t = 10^{-3}$ for 80 s, measured after 50 s.

1. Starts at (0.01, 0), (0.5, 0), (3, 0), (−4, 4) and (0, −6) all settle to amplitude 2.0086 and period 6.6633, identical to four decimals.
2. Over 20 s on the cycle, the energy pumped in while $|x| < 1$ is 16.988 and the energy removed while $|x| > 1$ is 16.995. Net $-8\times10^{-3}$, which is the leftover part of an unfinished cycle.
3. $\mu = 0.1$: amplitude 2.0000, period 6.2871, close to $2\pi = 6.2832$. $\mu = 5$: amplitude 2.0215, period 11.6122. The large-$\mu$ estimate $(3 - 2\ln2)\cdot5 = 8.069$ only becomes accurate for even larger $\mu$.
:::

```python file=vanderpol.py
"""Self-excited vibration: the Van der Pol oscillator and its limit cycle."""
import math
import numpy as np


def vdp(mu, s0, t_end, dt=1e-3):
    f = lambda s: np.array([s[1], mu * (1 - s[0]**2) * s[1] - s[0]])
    s = np.array(s0, float); out = [s.copy()]
    for _ in range(int(round(t_end / dt))):
        k1 = f(s); k2 = f(s + dt/2*k1); k3 = f(s + dt/2*k2); k4 = f(s + dt*k3)
        s = s + dt/6*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4); out.append(s.copy())
    return np.arange(len(out)) * dt, np.array(out)


def cycle_stats(t, s, t_from):
    x = s[:, 0]; sel = t > t_from
    i = np.where((x[:-1] < 0) & (x[1:] >= 0) & sel[:-1])[0]
    tc = t[i] - x[i] * (t[i+1] - t[i]) / (x[i+1] - x[i])
    return x[sel].max(), np.mean(np.diff(tc))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    mu = 1.0
    starts = [(0.01, 0.0), (0.5, 0.0), (3.0, 0.0), (-4.0, 4.0), (0.0, -6.0)]
    res = []
    for s0 in starts:
        t, s = vdp(mu, s0, 80.0)
        A, T = cycle_stats(t, s, 50.0); res.append((A, T))
        print(f"start {s0}: limit-cycle amplitude {A:.4f}, period {T:.4f}")
    A0, T0 = res[0]
    assert all(abs(A - A0) < 1e-3 and abs(T - T0) < 1e-3 for A, T in res)
    assert abs(A0 - 2.0086) < 2e-3 and abs(T0 - 6.6633) < 2e-3
    # energy balance: negative damping inside |x| < 1 pumps energy in, positive damping outside takes it out
    t, s = vdp(mu, (0.01, 0.0), 80.0)
    sel = t > 60; x, v = s[sel, 0], s[sel, 1]; dt = t[1] - t[0]
    pumped = np.sum(mu * (1 - x**2) * v * v * (np.abs(x) < 1)) * dt
    removed = np.sum(mu * (1 - x**2) * v * v * (np.abs(x) >= 1)) * dt
    print(f"over 20 s on the cycle: energy pumped in {pumped:.3f}, removed {-removed:.3f} (net {pumped + removed:.1e})")
    for m in (0.1, 5.0):
        t, s = vdp(m, (0.5, 0.0), 120.0, 5e-4)
        A, T = cycle_stats(t, s, 80.0)
        print(f"mu = {m}: amplitude {A:.4f}, period {T:.4f} (small mu: 2 and 2 pi; large mu: relaxation, T ~ (3 - 2 ln 2) mu = {(3-2*math.log(2))*m:.3f})")
```

:::exercise A squealing brake
A brake pad's tangential mode has $m = 0.2$ kg, $k = 4\times10^6$ N/m and structural damping $c = 20$ N s/m. The friction force falls with sliding speed with slope $dF/dv = -35$ N s/m. Does the pad squeal? At what frequency?
---solution---
1. Effective damping $c_{eff} = c + dF/dv = 20 - 35 = -15$ N s/m. Negative, so small vibrations grow: it squeals.
2. $f \approx \sqrt{k/m}/(2\pi) = \sqrt{2\times10^7}/(2\pi) = 711.8$ Hz.
3. Cures: add damping (shims, damping pastes) so $c > 35$ N s/m, or change the pad material to one whose friction doesn't fall with speed.
:::
