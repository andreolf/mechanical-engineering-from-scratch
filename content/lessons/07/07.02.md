Kinematics describes motion; Newton's second law explains it. The method is the same one used in statics (Phase 05): cut the body free, draw every force acting on it, and add them up. The only difference is that the sum is no longer zero. It equals mass times acceleration.

## The law

For a particle of constant mass $m$:

$$
\sum \mathbf F = m\,\mathbf a .
$$

It holds in an **inertial frame**, one that isn't accelerating or rotating (the ground is close enough for most engineering). Written in components it gives one equation per direction, the same as equilibrium equations but with $ma_x$, $ma_y$ on the right.

## The recipe

1. Choose the body and draw its free-body diagram: gravity, contact forces, friction, drag, thrust.
2. Choose coordinates (Cartesian, path or polar: whichever makes the acceleration simple).
3. Write $\sum F = ma$ in each direction.
4. Solve: either algebraically, or, if forces depend on velocity or position, as a differential equation integrated in time.

## Drag

Air drag on a fast body grows with the square of speed and points against the velocity:

$$
\mathbf F_D = -\tfrac12\rho\,C_DA\,|\mathbf v|\,\mathbf v .
$$

Now the equations couple $x$ and $y$ and have no simple closed-form solution. Integrate them numerically, with the RK4 method from Phase 04.

## A ski jumper

Cut the jumper free in flight: gravity $mg$ down and drag against the velocity (real jumpers also generate lift, which we leave out). Without drag, the path is the parabola of Phase 02. With drag, the jumper lands sooner and slower. The parabola is a test case: the simulation with $C_DA = 0$ must reproduce it exactly.

:::widget skijump
A 65 kg jumper leaves the table at 90 km/h, 11° below horizontal, over a 35° landing slope. Change the take-off speed and the drag area. The dashed curve is the drag-free parabola.
:::

:::example Drag and the ski jumper
$m = 65$ kg, $v_0 = 25$ m/s, take-off 11° down, air density 1.2 kg/m³, landing slope 35°.

1. With zero drag, RK4 with a 1 ms step matches the parabola $y = v_{y0}t - gt^2/2$ to $2.5\times10^{-12}$ m.
2. Hand check for no drag: the jumper hits the slope when $v_{y0}t - gt^2/2 = -\tan35°\cdot v_{x0}t$, giving $t = 2.531$ s and 75.82 m along the slope, exactly what the simulation finds.
3. With drag: $C_DA = 0.3$ m² lands at 67.49 m (118 km/h), 0.5 m² at 63.07 m (108 km/h), 0.8 m² at 57.62 m (97 km/h).
4. A tighter tuck pays off. Jumpers flying well beyond 100 m on big hills do it with lift from their V-style skis.
:::

```python file=skijump.py
"""A ski jumper in flight: gravity plus quadratic drag, integrated with RK4."""
import math
import numpy as np


def rk4(f, y0, t_end, dt):
    ys, t, y = [np.array(y0, float)], 0.0, np.array(y0, float)
    while t < t_end - 1e-12:
        h = min(dt, t_end - t)
        k1 = f(y); k2 = f(y + h / 2 * k1); k3 = f(y + h / 2 * k2); k4 = f(y + h * k3)
        y = y + h / 6 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4); t += h; ys.append(y)
    return np.array(ys)


def jumper(m=65.0, cdA=0.5, rho=1.2, g=9.81):
    """State (x, y, vx, vy); drag force 0.5 rho CdA |v| v opposes the velocity (no lift here)."""
    def f(s):
        vx, vy = s[2], s[3]; v = math.hypot(vx, vy)
        k = 0.5 * rho * cdA * v / m
        return np.array([vx, vy, -k * vx, -g - k * vy])
    return f


def landing(traj, slope_deg, dt):
    """First crossing of a straight landing slope y = -tan(slope) x through the take-off point."""
    s = math.tan(math.radians(slope_deg))
    gap = traj[:, 1] + s * traj[:, 0]
    i = int(np.argmax(gap < 0))
    a = gap[i - 1] / (gap[i - 1] - gap[i])             # linear interpolation inside the step
    return traj[i - 1] + a * (traj[i] - traj[i - 1]), (i - 1 + a) * dt


if __name__ == "__main__":
    v0, ang, dt = 25.0, -11.0, 0.001                  # take-off at 90 km/h, table angled 11 deg down
    s0 = [0, 0, v0 * math.cos(math.radians(ang)), v0 * math.sin(math.radians(ang))]
    # zero drag must reproduce the parabola x = vx t, y = vy t - g t^2 / 2
    tr = rk4(jumper(cdA=0.0), s0, 3.0, dt)
    t = np.arange(len(tr)) * dt
    para_y = s0[3] * t - 9.81 * t**2 / 2
    print(f"no drag: max |y - parabola| = {np.max(np.abs(tr[:, 1] - para_y)):.2e} m")
    assert np.max(np.abs(tr[:, 1] - para_y)) < 1e-9 and np.max(np.abs(tr[:, 0] - s0[2] * t)) < 1e-9
    for cdA in (0.0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8):
        tr = rk4(jumper(cdA=cdA), s0, 8.0, dt)
        p, tl = landing(tr, 35.0, dt)
        dist = math.hypot(p[0], p[1])
        v = math.hypot(p[2], p[3])
        print(f"CdA = {cdA:.1f} m^2: lands {dist:6.2f} m down a 35 deg slope after {tl:.2f} s at {v*3.6:5.1f} km/h")
    # hand check of the no-drag landing: y = -tan(35) x with x = vx t, y = vy t - g t^2/2
    vx, vy = s0[2], s0[3]
    tl = 2 * (vy + vx * math.tan(math.radians(35))) / 9.81
    print(f"hand, no drag: t = {tl:.4f} s, distance {vx * tl / math.cos(math.radians(35)):.2f} m")
```

:::exercise A lift in a tall building
A person of 75 kg stands on a scale in a lift. What does the scale read (in newtons and in "kg") when the lift (a) accelerates upward at 1.5 m/s², (b) moves at constant speed, (c) slows down while going up at 2 m/s²?
---solution---
1. Free body: weight $mg$ down, scale force $N$ up. $N - mg = ma$, so $N = m(g + a)$ with $a$ up positive.
2. (a) $75\cdot(9.81 + 1.5) = 848$ N, reads 86.5 kg.
3. (b) $a = 0$: $N = 736$ N, reads 75 kg.
4. (c) $a = -2$: $N = 75\cdot7.81 = 586$ N, reads 59.7 kg.
:::
