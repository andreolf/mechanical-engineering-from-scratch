A car is thousands of parts, a rocket sheds exhaust, a gymnast moves her arms in mid-air. Treating each as one particle seems too crude. Yet one result makes it legitimate for the overall motion: the centre of mass moves as if all the mass were concentrated there and all external forces acted on it.

## The centre of mass theorem

For particles $i$ with masses $m_i$ at positions $\mathbf r_i$, define the total mass $M$ and the centre of mass

$$
\mathbf r_G = \frac{1}{M}\sum_i m_i\mathbf r_i .
$$

Write Newton's law for each particle, splitting forces into external $\mathbf F_i$ and internal $\mathbf f_{ij}$ (from particle $j$ on $i$):

$$
m_i\ddot{\mathbf r}_i = \mathbf F_i + \sum_{j\ne i}\mathbf f_{ij} .
$$

Add all of them. By Newton's third law $\mathbf f_{ij} = -\mathbf f_{ji}$, so the internal forces cancel in pairs:

$$
M\ddot{\mathbf r}_G = \sum_i \mathbf F_i .
$$

## Consequences

- With no external force, the centre of mass moves in a straight line at constant speed, and total momentum $M\dot{\mathbf r}_G$ is conserved. That's what made the collision problems of 07.04 solvable.
- A diver can't change the path of her centre of mass in the air: it follows a parabola whatever she does with her body.
- An exploding firework's fragments scatter, but their centre of mass continues along the original parabola (until fragments hit the ground).

## N-body simulation

A good test of any simulation of interacting particles: the internal forces must sum to zero at every step, and momentum must stay constant to rounding error. The **velocity Verlet** method, used for molecular dynamics and planetary motion, keeps this property exactly.

Bodies attracting each other like tiny planets. Move the time slider. However tangled their paths, the centre of mass (lime, dashed) runs straight. Switch on gravity and it follows a parabola instead.
:::

:::example Five bodies
Five masses between 0.5 and 2.0 (total 5.200) with random positions and velocities, attracting each other.

1. Initial momentum (0.302751, 0.538379), so the centre of mass moves at (0.0582, 0.1035).
2. After 5000 velocity-Verlet steps the momentum is unchanged to 6 decimals and beyond, and the centre of mass is within $3\times10^{-14}$ of the straight line.
3. The sum of $m_i\mathbf a_i$ at the start is $(-2\times10^{-15}, -1\times10^{-15})$: zero up to rounding.
4. With a uniform external gravity of 1 downward, the centre of mass lands at $(-0.059615, -11.959423)$ after 5 time units, exactly the single-particle prediction $\mathbf r_0 + \mathbf v_G t + \tfrac12\mathbf g t^2$.
:::

```python file=particles.py
"""A system of particles: internal forces cancel, so the centre of mass moves uniformly."""
import numpy as np


def accelerations(x, m, G=1.0, soft=0.05):
    """Pairwise gravity-like attraction (softened). Each pair pushes equally and oppositely."""
    d = x[None, :, :] - x[:, None, :]                       # d[i, j] = x_j - x_i
    r2 = (d**2).sum(-1) + soft**2
    np.fill_diagonal(r2, np.inf)
    return G * (m[None, :, None] * d / r2[..., None] ** 1.5).sum(1)


def simulate(x, v, m, dt, steps):
    """Velocity Verlet."""
    a = accelerations(x, m); com = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        v = v + 0.5 * dt * a; x = x + dt * v; a = accelerations(x, m); v = v + 0.5 * dt * a
        com.append((m[:, None] * x).sum(0) / m.sum())
    return x, v, np.array(com)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
    n = 5
    m = rng.uniform(0.5, 2.0, n)
    x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, (n, 2))
    v = rng.normal(0, 0.3, (n, 2))
    P0 = (m[:, None] * v).sum(0); M = m.sum()
    vcom = P0 / M; com0 = (m[:, None] * x).sum(0) / M
    dt, steps = 1e-3, 5000
    x1, v1, com = simulate(x, v, m, dt, steps)
    t = dt * np.arange(1, steps + 1)
    drift = np.max(np.linalg.norm(com - (com0 + np.outer(t, vcom)), axis=1))
    P1 = (m[:, None] * v1).sum(0)
    print(f"{n} bodies, total mass {M:.3f}; COM velocity {vcom.round(4)}")
    print(f"momentum before {P0.round(6)}, after {P1.round(6)}; COM departs from a straight line by at most {drift:.1e}")
    assert np.allclose(P0, P1, atol=1e-12) and drift < 1e-10
    # the internal forces sum to zero at every instant
    a = accelerations(x, m)
    print(f"sum of m a over all particles: {(m[:, None] * a).sum(0)}")
    assert np.allclose((m[:, None] * a).sum(0), 0, atol=1e-12)
    # add an external force (uniform gravity -1 in y): the COM then falls like a single particle
    g = np.array([0.0, -1.0])
    xg, vg = x.copy(), v.copy(); ag = accelerations(xg, m) + g
    for _ in range(steps):
        vg = vg + 0.5 * dt * ag; xg = xg + dt * vg; ag = accelerations(xg, m) + g; vg = vg + 0.5 * dt * ag
    T = dt * steps
    comg = (m[:, None] * xg).sum(0) / M
    print(f"with gravity: COM at {comg.round(6)}, single-particle prediction {(com0 + vcom * T + 0.5 * g * T**2).round(6)}")
    assert np.allclose(comg, com0 + vcom * T + 0.5 * g * T**2, atol=1e-9)
```

:::exercise A boat and a walker
A 70 kg person walks 4 m from the stern to the bow of a 210 kg boat floating at rest. Ignoring water resistance, how far does the boat move?
---solution---
1. No horizontal external force, and everything starts at rest: the centre of mass stays put.
2. If the boat moves $x$ backwards, the person moves $4 - x$ forwards relative to the water: $70(4 - x) = 210x$.
3. $x = 280/280 = 1.0$ m. The person moves 3.0 m relative to the water.
:::
