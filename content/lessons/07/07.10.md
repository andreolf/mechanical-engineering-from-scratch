Mass tells you how hard it is to push something into motion; the moment of inertia tells you how hard it is to spin it. A flywheel stores energy by having a large one, a racing wheel accelerates quickly by having a small one, and a figure skater changes hers by moving her arms. It depends on where the mass sits, not only how much there is.

## Definition

For a body rotating about an axis, each bit of mass $dm$ at distance $r$ from the axis contributes $r^2\,dm$:

$$
I = \int r^2\,dm .
$$

The **radius of gyration** $k$ is defined by $I = mk^2$: the radius at which all the mass could sit to give the same $I$.

## Standard results

| Body (axis) | $I$ |
|---|---|
| Thin ring, radius $R$ | $mR^2$ |
| Solid disc or cylinder | $\tfrac12 mR^2$ |
| Thick ring, radii $R_1$, $R_2$ | $\tfrac12 m(R_1^2 + R_2^2)$ |
| Solid sphere | $\tfrac25 mR^2$ |
| Slender rod, about its centre | $\tfrac1{12}mL^2$ |

## The parallel-axis theorem

If $I_G$ is about an axis through the centre of mass, then about a parallel axis a distance $d$ away:

$$
I = I_G + md^2 .
$$

A small mass far from the axis matters far more than its own spin.

## From a drawing

For a wheel symmetric about its axis with thickness $t(r)$, a thin ring at radius $r$ has $dm = \rho\,t(r)\,2\pi r\,dr$, so $I = \int \rho\,t(r)\,2\pi r^3\,dr$. That one integral handles any profile. It's what CAD mass-properties tools compute, and a check against simple shapes proves the code.

:::example Checking shapes, then an engine flywheel
Steel, $\rho = 7850$ kg/m³.

1. Solid disc 400 × 30 mm: $m = 29.594$ kg, $I = 0.591876$ kg m², equal to $\tfrac12 mR^2$.
2. Ring from 300 to 400 mm diameter, 30 mm thick: $m = 12.947$ kg, $I = 0.404603$ kg m², equal to $\tfrac12 m(R_1^2 + R_2^2)$.
3. A flywheel for a stationary engine: hub 60 mm wide from a 30 mm bore to 60 mm diameter, a 20 mm web out to 320 mm diameter, then a 60 mm rim to 400 mm. $m = 34.49$ kg, $I = 0.86087$ kg m², radius of gyration 158 mm.
4. The rim is 62 % of the mass but 81 % of the inertia.
5. A 0.5 kg balance mass bolted on at 150 mm adds $0.5\cdot0.15^2 = 0.01125$ kg m² by the parallel-axis term; its spin about its own axis adds only $2.5\times10^{-5}$.
6. At 3000 rpm the flywheel stores $\tfrac12 I\omega^2 = 42.48$ kJ.
:::

```python file=flywheel_inertia.py
"""Moment of inertia of a flywheel from its profile: I = integral of r^2 dm, plus the parallel-axis theorem."""
import math
import numpy as np


def inertia_from_profile(r, t, rho):
    """Axisymmetric wheel with thickness t(r) (m) at radius r (m): dm = rho t(r) 2 pi r dr, I = integral r^2 dm."""
    m = np.trapezoid(rho * t * 2 * math.pi * r, r)
    I = np.trapezoid(rho * t * 2 * math.pi * r**3, r)
    return m, I


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rho = 7850.0
    # check simple shapes against the formulas a CAD package would give
    r = np.linspace(0, 0.2, 200001)
    m, I = inertia_from_profile(r, np.full_like(r, 0.03), rho)
    print(f"solid disc 400 x 30 mm: m = {m:.4f} kg, I = {I:.6f} kg m^2 (m R^2 / 2 = {m * 0.2**2 / 2:.6f})")
    assert abs(I - m * 0.04 / 2) < 1e-9
    r = np.linspace(0.15, 0.2, 200001)
    m, I = inertia_from_profile(r, np.full_like(r, 0.03), rho)
    print(f"ring 300-400 mm: m = {m:.4f} kg, I = {I:.6f} (m (R1^2 + R2^2) / 2 = {m * (0.15**2 + 0.2**2) / 2:.6f})")
    assert abs(I - m * (0.15**2 + 0.2**2) / 2) < 1e-9
    # an engine flywheel: 20 mm web out to 160 mm, then a 60 mm wide rim to 200 mm, with a 60 mm hub (bore 30 mm)
    r = np.linspace(0.015, 0.2, 400001)
    t = np.where(r < 0.03, 0.06, np.where(r < 0.16, 0.02, 0.06))
    m, I = inertia_from_profile(r, t, rho)
    rim = np.where(r >= 0.16, t, 0)
    mr, Ir = inertia_from_profile(r, rim, rho)
    print(f"engine flywheel: m = {m:.3f} kg, I = {I:.5f} kg m^2; the rim is {100 * mr / m:.0f} % of the mass but {100 * Ir / I:.0f} % of the inertia")
    print(f"radius of gyration k = sqrt(I/m) = {math.sqrt(I / m) * 1000:.1f} mm")
    # parallel axis: a 0.5 kg bolt-on balance mass 150 mm off-axis, treated as a 20 mm diameter disc about its own axis
    mb, rb = 0.5, 0.15
    Ib = mb * 0.01**2 / 2 + mb * rb**2
    print(f"balance mass about the shaft: {Ib:.6f} kg m^2 (own axis only {mb * 0.01**2 / 2:.2e})")
    # energy stored at 3000 rpm
    w = 3000 * 2 * math.pi / 60
    print(f"kinetic energy at 3000 rpm: {0.5 * I * w**2 / 1e3:.2f} kJ")
```

:::exercise A bicycle wheel
A bicycle wheel has a 0.45 kg rim and tyre at an effective radius of 0.33 m, 32 spokes of 6 g each running from the hub (radius 0.03 m) to the rim, and a 0.3 kg hub of radius 0.03 m. Estimate its moment of inertia, treating the spokes as thin rods.
---solution---
1. Rim and tyre: $0.45\cdot0.33^2 = 0.0490$ kg m².
2. Spokes: a rod from 0.03 to 0.33 m has $I = \tfrac{m}{3}\cdot\tfrac{r_2^3 - r_1^3}{r_2 - r_1} = \tfrac{0.006}{3}\cdot\tfrac{0.035937 - 0.000027}{0.3} = 2.394\times10^{-4}$ kg m² each; 32 of them: 0.00766 kg m².
3. Hub, as a solid cylinder: $\tfrac12\cdot0.3\cdot0.03^2 = 0.000135$ kg m².
4. Total about 0.0568 kg m². The rim dominates, which is why light rims matter so much to climbers.
:::
