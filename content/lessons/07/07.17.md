A flywheel stores energy as rotation. Spin it up with spare power, draw the power back when needed. Modern ones smooth the grid, recover braking energy in buses and racing cars, and keep data centres running for the seconds before a generator starts. How much they can store is limited by one thing: how fast the rim can go before it flies apart.

## Energy

$$
E = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 .
$$

For a thin rim of mass $m$ at radius $R$, $I = mR^2$ and $E = \tfrac12 mv^2$ with rim speed $v = \omega R$. Energy grows with the square of speed, so flywheels spin fast: 20,000 to 50,000 rpm.

## Rim stress

Cut a thin spinning rim in half. Each half needs a centripetal force, supplied by tension in the two cut faces, just like the pressure vessel of 06.21. A rim element of mass $\rho A\,R\,d\theta$ needs $\rho AR\,d\theta\cdot R\omega^2$ inward. Summing the components across the cut gives $2\rho AR^2\omega^2 = 2\sigma A$, so

$$
\sigma = \rho R^2\omega^2 = \rho v^2 .
$$

The hoop stress depends only on the rim speed and the density. Setting $\sigma$ to the material strength gives a **burst speed** $v_{max} = \sqrt{\sigma/\rho}$, the same for a small or a large wheel.

## Specific energy

At the limit, the energy per kilogram of rim is

$$
e = \frac{v_{max}^2}{2} = \frac{\sigma}{2\rho} .
$$

Size doesn't matter; only the material's strength-to-density ratio does. That's why high-performance flywheels use carbon fibre rather than steel. And since stress grows with speed squared, a 10 % overspeed means 21 % more stress. Flywheels sit in strong containment housings.

:::example Steel against carbon fibre
1. High-strength steel, $\rho = 7850$ kg/m³, usable stress 800 MPa: $v_{max} = 319.2$ m/s. At $R = 0.2$, 0.5 and 1.0 m that is 15,242, 6097 and 3048 rpm. Cutting the rim in half and integrating the centrifugal load numerically gives exactly 800.00 MPa each time.
2. Specific energy at the limit: steel 51.0 kJ/kg (14.2 Wh/kg); carbon fibre at 1.5 GPa and 1600 kg/m³: 468.8 kJ/kg (130.2 Wh/kg).
3. A 60 kg carbon rim at 0.25 m radius, with a safety factor of 1.5 on stress: top speed 30,198 rpm (rim speed 791 m/s), storing 5.21 kWh. Running between 50 % and 100 % speed releases three quarters of that: 3.91 kWh.
4. These are ideal thin-rim figures. Hubs, bearings and the containment cut the real system's energy density several times.
:::

```python file=flywheel_storage.py
"""Flywheel energy storage: E = I w^2 / 2 and the thin-rim hoop stress sigma = rho v^2."""
import math
import numpy as np


def rim_stress_by_equilibrium(rho, R, t, w, n=200001):
    """Cut a thin rim (radius R, cross-section area A = t * 1) in half: the centrifugal pull on the half ring
    must be carried by the two cut faces: 2 sigma A = integral rho A R w^2 R sin(th) dth = 2 rho A R^2 w^2."""
    th = np.linspace(0, np.pi, n)
    A = t * 1.0
    F = np.trapezoid(rho * A * R * w**2 * np.sin(th) * R, th)
    return F / (2 * A)


def burst_speed(sigma_allow, rho, R):
    """Thin rim: sigma = rho v^2, so v_max = sqrt(sigma / rho) regardless of R; w_max = v_max / R."""
    v = math.sqrt(sigma_allow / rho)
    return v, v / R


if __name__ == "__main__":
    rho_steel, s_steel = 7850.0, 800e6          # high-strength steel, usable stress 800 MPa
    rho_cf, s_cf = 1600.0, 1500e6               # carbon-fibre rim, usable hoop stress 1.5 GPa
    for R in (0.2, 0.5, 1.0):
        v, w = burst_speed(s_steel, rho_steel, R)
        s_num = rim_stress_by_equilibrium(rho_steel, R, 0.01, w)
        print(f"steel rim R = {R} m: limit rim speed {v:.1f} m/s, {w * 60 / (2 * math.pi):8.0f} rpm; stress check {s_num / 1e6:.2f} MPa")
        assert abs(s_num - s_steel) < 1e-6 * s_steel
    # energy per unit mass of a thin rim at its limit: e = v^2 / 2 = sigma / (2 rho), again independent of R
    for name, rho, s in (("steel", rho_steel, s_steel), ("carbon fibre", rho_cf, s_cf)):
        e = s / (2 * rho)
        print(f"{name:12s}: specific energy at the stress limit {e / 1e3:.1f} kJ/kg = {e / 3600:.1f} Wh/kg")
    # a carbon-fibre rim for a bus: 60 kg, mean radius 0.25 m, run between 50 % and 100 % of top speed
    m, R = 60.0, 0.25
    v, w = burst_speed(s_cf / 1.5, rho_cf, R)               # safety factor 1.5 on stress
    I = m * R**2
    E_full = 0.5 * I * w**2
    print(f"bus flywheel: top speed {w * 60 / (2 * math.pi):.0f} rpm, rim {v:.0f} m/s, stored {E_full / 3.6e6:.3f} kWh; usable between 50 % and 100 % speed: {0.75 * E_full / 3.6e6:.3f} kWh")
    # stress grows with the square of speed: 10 % overspeed means 21 % more stress
    print(f"10 % overspeed raises rim stress by {(1.1**2 - 1) * 100:.0f} %")
```

:::exercise A cast-iron flywheel
An old engine has a cast-iron flywheel ($\rho = 7200$ kg/m³, safe hoop stress 20 MPa) with a rim of mean radius 0.9 m. What is the safe maximum speed in rpm? If the rim has mass 400 kg, how much energy does it hold at that speed?
---solution---
1. $v_{max} = \sqrt{20\times10^6/7200} = 52.7$ m/s.
2. $\omega = 52.7/0.9 = 58.6$ rad/s, which is 559 rpm.
3. $E = \tfrac12\cdot400\cdot52.7^2 = 556$ kJ, about 0.15 kWh. Cast iron is weak in tension, so these flywheels ran slowly.
:::
