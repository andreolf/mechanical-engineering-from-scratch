Hold a spinning bicycle wheel by one end of its axle and let go of the other. Instead of falling, it swings slowly round in a horizontal circle. That is precession, and it's what stabilises spinning tops, points ship gyrocompasses north, steers satellites with control moment gyros, and partly explains why a moving bike is easy to balance.

## The fast-top approximation

A rotor spins fast at $\omega$ about its axis, with moment of inertia $I_3$, so it carries angular momentum $H = I_3\omega$ along the axis. Its centre of mass is a distance $l$ from a pivot, with the axis horizontal. Gravity makes a torque $M = mgl$, horizontal and perpendicular to the axis.

By $\dot{\mathbf H} = \mathbf M$, the torque changes $\mathbf H$ sideways, not downwards. The tip of $\mathbf H$ moves in a horizontal circle at rate $\Omega$, with $|\dot{\mathbf H}| = \Omega H$:

$$
\Omega = \frac{mgl}{I_3\,\omega} .
$$

Faster spin means slower precession. Double the torque and it precesses twice as fast.

## Nutation

The formula assumes all the angular momentum comes from the spin. Released from rest, the gyroscope first dips slightly, then bobs up and down while it precesses. This wobble, **nutation**, has an amplitude roughly proportional to $1/\omega^2$. It fades quickly for a fast rotor and the steady precession takes over. Simulating the full equations of the heavy top (from its Lagrangian, as in 07.18) shows both effects.

## Gyroscopic moments in machines

When a spinning part is forced to turn its axis at rate $\Omega$, the bearings must supply a moment $I_3\omega\Omega$. Turbine rotors in turning ships, propellers on manoeuvring aircraft and car wheels during steering all feel it.

:::example A toy gyroscope
$m = 0.15$ kg, $l = 0.04$ m, $I_3 = 6\times10^{-5}$ kg m², $I_1 = 4\times10^{-5}$ kg m² about the pivot, released horizontally from rest.

1. Formula at 1000 rad/s: $\Omega = 0.15\cdot9.81\cdot0.04/(6\times10^{-5}\cdot1000) = 0.981$ rad/s.
2. Simulated average precession over 2 s: 9.702 rad/s at 100 rad/s spin (ratio 0.989), 0.98092 at 1000 rad/s (0.9999), 0.49055 at 2000 rad/s (1.0001).
3. Nutation amplitude: 7.39° at 100 rad/s, 0.30° at 500, 0.019° at 2000. Four times the spin gives about one sixteenth of the wobble.
4. At 2000 rad/s (19,100 rpm) it precesses once every 12.8 s.
:::

```python file=gyro.py
"""A gyroscope on a pivot: full rigid-body simulation against the fast-top precession rate m g r / (I omega)."""
import math
import numpy as np




def simulate(I1, I3, m, g, l, theta0, spin, t_end, dt=1e-4):
    """Lagrangian of the heavy symmetric top: L = I1/2 (th'^2 + ph'^2 sin^2 th) + I3/2 (ps' + ph' cos th)^2 - m g l cos th.
    Conserved: w3 = ps' + ph' cos th (spin) and p_phi = I1 ph' sin^2 th + I3 w3 cos th.
    Start with th' = 0, ph' = 0 (released from rest in precession): the top nutates and precesses on average."""
    w3 = spin
    p_phi = I3 * w3 * math.cos(theta0)                     # ph'(0) = 0
    def f(st):
        th, thd, ph = st
        phd = (p_phi - I3 * w3 * math.cos(th)) / (I1 * math.sin(th) ** 2)
        thdd = (phd**2 * math.sin(th) * math.cos(th) * I1 - I3 * w3 * phd * math.sin(th) + m * g * l * math.sin(th)) / I1
        return np.array([thd, thdd, phd])
    st = np.array([theta0, 0.0, 0.0]); t = 0.0; ths = [theta0]
    n = int(round(t_end / dt))
    for _ in range(n):
        k1 = f(st); k2 = f(st + dt/2*k1); k3 = f(st + dt/2*k2); k4 = f(st + dt*k3)
        st = st + dt/6*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4); ths.append(st[0])
    return st, np.array(ths)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # a toy gyroscope: 0.15 kg rotor, I3 = 6e-5 kg m^2 (spin axis), I1 = 4e-5 about the pivot, centre of mass 0.04 m out, horizontal
    m, g, l, I1, I3 = 0.15, 9.81, 0.04, 4.0e-5, 6.0e-5
    theta0 = math.pi / 2
    print("spin (rad/s)  simulated precession  formula m g l/(I3 w)  ratio   nutation amplitude (deg)")
    for spin in (100.0, 200.0, 500.0, 1000.0, 2000.0):
        T = 2.0
        st, ths = simulate(I1, I3, m, g, l, theta0, spin, T)
        Om_sim = st[2] / T
        Om_fast = m * g * l / (I3 * spin)
        print(f"{spin:10.0f}  {Om_sim:18.5f}  {Om_fast:20.5f}  {Om_sim / Om_fast:6.4f}   {math.degrees(ths.max() - ths.min()):.4f}")
    st, _ = simulate(I1, I3, m, g, l, theta0, 2000.0, 2.0)
    assert abs(st[2] / 2.0 / (m * g * l / (I3 * 2000.0)) - 1) < 0.01
    # the precession formula is the angular momentum law: torque m g l turns H = I3 w at rate Omega, so m g l = Omega I3 w
    print(f"at 2000 rad/s (19,100 rpm) the gyroscope precesses once every {2 * math.pi / (m * g * l / (I3 * 2000)):.2f} s")
```

:::exercise A ship's turbine
A ship's turbine rotor has $I = 900$ kg m² and spins at 3600 rpm with its axis fore and aft. The ship turns at 2° per second. What gyroscopic moment do the bearings carry?
---solution---
1. $\omega = 3600\cdot2\pi/60 = 377$ rad/s, so $H = 900\cdot377 = 3.39\times10^5$ kg m²/s.
2. $\Omega = 2° \text{ per s} = 0.0349$ rad/s.
3. $M = H\Omega = 3.39\times10^5\cdot0.0349 = 11.8$ kN m, acting in the vertical plane through the axis (it tries to pitch the rotor).
:::
