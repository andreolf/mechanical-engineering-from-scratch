A bridge girder sits on a pin at one end and a roller at the other, not on two pins. A motorbike's rear suspension is a linkage with exactly one way to move. Both choices come from counting freedoms and constraints. Before you write any equilibrium equation, you should know whether the body *can* be held still by its supports, and whether the supports fight each other. That's what this lesson is about.

## Idealised supports

Each planar support stops some motions and supplies one reaction component for each motion it stops:

| Support | Prevents | Reactions |
|---|---|---|
| Roller (or link, smooth surface) | motion normal to the surface | 1 force, normal to the surface |
| Pin (hinge) | both translations | 2 force components |
| Clamp (fixed, built-in) | translations and rotation | 2 forces and 1 moment |
| Cable | motion away from the anchor | 1 tension, along the cable, pulling only |

A real support is somewhere in between: a bolted connection is partly fixed, a bearing pad resists sliding a little. Choosing the idealisation is part of the modelling.

## Counting

A free rigid body in the plane has 3 degrees of freedom. With $r$ reaction components the count is

$$
\text{DOF} = 3 - r .
$$

- $r < 3$: a **mechanism**; the body can move.
- $r = 3$ and the reactions are properly arranged: **statically determinate**; the three equilibrium equations give all reactions.
- $r > 3$: **statically indeterminate**; there are more unknowns than equations, and you need deformation (Phase 06) to solve.

That's why bridges use a pin and a roller: three reactions, and the roller lets the girder expand with temperature without building up force.

## Counting is not enough: the rank

Three reactions can still fail. Three vertical rollers have $r = 3$ but nothing resists horizontal sliding. A pin plus a roller whose reaction line passes through the pin can't resist rotation about that pin. Write each unit reaction as a row $(F_x, F_y, M_O)$; the supports work only if this matrix has **rank 3**. A rank below 3 is called improper (or geometric) instability, and it's a classic exam trap and a classic real failure.

## Linkages: Grübler's formula

For $n$ links (counting the ground) joined by $j_1$ pins or sliders (each removing 2 freedoms) and $j_2$ rolling or cam contacts (each removing 1):

$$
M = 3(n-1) - 2j_1 - j_2 .
$$

A four-bar linkage has $M = 3\cdot3 - 2\cdot4 = 1$: one input drives the whole thing. A triangle of three bars has $M = 0$, which is why trusses are made of triangles.

:::example A footbridge and some linkages
1. Pin at the left end, roller at the right: $r = 3$, DOF $= 0$, rank 3: determinate.
2. Pin at both ends: $r = 4$, DOF $= -1$: one degree indeterminate. Heating the girder would build up axial force.
3. Three vertical rollers: $r = 3$ but rank 2: a mechanism that slides sideways.
4. Grübler: four-bar 1, slider-crank 1 (the slider counts as a joint), five-bar 2, triangle 0, Stephenson six-bar with 6 links and 7 joints gives 1.
:::

```python file=supports_dof.py
"""Supports and constraints: count reactions, Grubler mobility of planar linkages, and the rank check behind it."""
import numpy as np

REACTIONS = {"roller": 1, "pin": 2, "clamp": 3}           # planar: unknown reaction components per support


def beam_dof(supports):
    """A single rigid beam in the plane has 3 DOF; each support removes as many as it has reactions (if they're independent)."""
    r = sum(REACTIONS[s] for s in supports)
    return 3 - r, r


def grubler(n_links, j1, j2=0):
    """Planar mobility M = 3(n - 1) - 2 j1 - j2, counting the ground as a link; j1 = pins/sliders, j2 = cam/rolling contacts."""
    return 3 * (n_links - 1) - 2 * j1 - j2


def support_rank(points_dirs):
    """Rank of the reaction matrix: rows are (Fx, Fy, M about origin) of each unit reaction. Rank < 3 means a mechanism."""
    rows = []
    for (x, y), (dx, dy) in points_dirs:
        rows.append([dx, dy, x * dy - y * dx])
    return np.linalg.matrix_rank(np.array(rows, float))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    for s in (["pin", "roller"], ["clamp"], ["pin", "pin"], ["roller", "roller"]):
        dof, r = beam_dof(s)
        print(f"beam on {' + '.join(s):15s}: {r} reactions, DOF = {dof}")
    assert beam_dof(["pin", "roller"]) == (0, 3)
    # rank catches what counting misses: three rollers all vertical give 3 reactions but cannot stop sliding
    vertical = [((0, 0), (0, 1)), ((2, 0), (0, 1)), ((4, 0), (0, 1))]
    pin_roller = [((0, 0), (1, 0)), ((0, 0), (0, 1)), ((4, 0), (0, 1))]
    concurrent = [((0, 0), (1, 0)), ((0, 0), (0, 1)), ((4, 0), (-1, 0))]    # roller reaction pointing through the pin
    print("rank: pin + roller", support_rank(pin_roller), "| three vertical rollers", support_rank(vertical),
          "| reactions through one point", support_rank(concurrent))
    assert support_rank(pin_roller) == 3 and support_rank(vertical) == 2 and support_rank(concurrent) == 2
    # linkages
    cases = {"four-bar": (4, 4), "slider-crank": (4, 4), "five-bar": (5, 5), "triangle (rigid truss)": (3, 3), "Stephenson six-bar": (6, 7)}
    for name, (n, j) in cases.items():
        print(f"{name:22s}: n = {n}, j1 = {j}, M = {grubler(n, j)}")
    assert grubler(4, 4) == 1 and grubler(5, 5) == 2 and grubler(3, 3) == 0 and grubler(6, 7) == 1
```

:::exercise A motorbike rear suspension
The swingarm is pinned to the frame. A rocker is pinned to the frame, a link joins the rocker to the swingarm, and the shock absorber (modelled as a rigid link for now) joins the rocker to the frame. How many links and pins, and what is the mobility? What happens when the shock is allowed to change length?
---solution---
1. Links: frame (ground), swingarm, rocker, link, shock: $n = 5$. With the shock rigid, joints: swingarm–frame, rocker–frame, link–rocker, link–swingarm, shock–rocker, shock–frame: $j_1 = 6$.
2. $M = 3\cdot4 - 2\cdot6 = 0$: a rigid structure.
3. Letting the shock slide (its two halves become two links joined by a slider) adds one link and one joint: $M = 3\cdot5 - 2\cdot7 = 1$. The wheel has exactly one way to move, and the shock controls it.
:::
