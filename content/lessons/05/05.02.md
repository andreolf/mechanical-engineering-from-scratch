Push a door near its hinge and it barely moves; push at the handle and it swings. The turning effect of a force, its **moment**, depends on where it acts as much as on how big it is. Moments decide whether a crane tips, how much torque a bolt gets and how a bridge pier is loaded. This lesson defines the moment as a cross product, which works the same way in 2D and 3D.

## The moment of a force about a point

For a force $\mathbf F$ acting at a point $P$, its moment about a point $O$ is

$$
\mathbf M_O = \mathbf r \times \mathbf F, \qquad \mathbf r = \mathbf r_P - \mathbf r_O .
$$

Its magnitude is $|\mathbf M_O| = F\,d$, where $d = r\sin\varphi$ is the **lever arm**: the perpendicular distance from $O$ to the line of action. That's why sliding a force along its line of action changes nothing, and moving it sideways does. In the plane only the $z$ component survives:

$$
M_O = r_xF_y - r_yF_x \qquad\text{(counter-clockwise positive)}.
$$

**Varignon's theorem**: the moment of a force equals the sum of the moments of its components. Split an awkward force into $x$ and $y$ parts and take each part's moment separately.

## Moment about an axis

In 3D, the turning effect about an axis through $O$ with unit vector $\mathbf e$ is the projection $M_e = \mathbf e\cdot(\mathbf r\times\mathbf F)$. That's the torque that actually turns a valve wheel or a shaft; the other components are carried by its bearings.

## Couples

Two equal, opposite, parallel forces a distance $d$ apart form a **couple**. They have zero resultant but a moment $M = Fd$, and that moment is the **same about every point**:

$$
\mathbf r_A\times\mathbf F + \mathbf r_B\times(-\mathbf F) = (\mathbf r_A - \mathbf r_B)\times\mathbf F,
$$

which doesn't depend on the reference point. A couple is a free vector: a pure twist you can apply anywhere on the body. Your two hands on a steering wheel form one.

:::widget spanner
A spanner on a nut. Change the force, its angle and where you grip. The cyan dashed line is the lever arm. The moment is largest when you pull at 90° at the end of the handle.
:::

:::example Tightening a nut
A 120 N push at 70° to a 250 mm spanner handle.

1. Components at the grip: $F_x = 120\cos70° = 41.04$ N along the handle, $F_y = 120\sin70° = 112.76$ N across it.
2. Varignon: the component along the handle has zero lever arm, so $M = 0.25\cdot112.76 = 28.19$ N m.
3. Lever arm $d = M/F = 234.9$ mm, a little less than the handle length.
4. A couple: two 50 N forces 0.4 m apart give 20 N m about every point. The code checks it about 20 random points.
:::

```python file=moments.py
"""Moments M = r x F about any point, and the couple: same moment about every point."""
import math
import numpy as np


def moment(F, at, about=(0.0, 0.0, 0.0)):
    r = np.array(at, float) - np.array(about, float)
    return np.cross(r, np.array(F, float))


def moment_2d(F, at, about=(0.0, 0.0)):
    """Scalar z-moment, counter-clockwise positive: M = rx Fy - ry Fx."""
    rx, ry = at[0] - about[0], at[1] - about[1]
    return rx * F[1] - ry * F[0]


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # a spanner: 120 N pushed at 70 deg to a 250 mm handle
    L, P, ang = 0.25, 120.0, math.radians(70)
    F = (P * math.cos(ang), P * math.sin(ang))
    M = moment_2d(F, (L, 0.0))
    print(f"spanner moment {M:.3f} N m (= P L sin 70 = {P * L * math.sin(ang):.3f}); lever arm {M / P * 1e3:.1f} mm")
    assert abs(M - P * L * math.sin(ang)) < 1e-12
    # Varignon: moment of the resultant equals the sum of the moments of the components
    assert abs(moment_2d((F[0], 0), (L, 0)) + moment_2d((0, F[1]), (L, 0)) - M) < 1e-12
    # a couple: +F at A and -F at B; its moment is the same about every point
    Fc, A, B = np.array([0.0, 50.0, 0.0]), np.array([0.2, 0.0, 0.0]), np.array([-0.2, 0.0, 0.0])
    rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
    Ms = [moment(Fc, A, p) + moment(-Fc, B, p) for p in rng.uniform(-5, 5, (20, 3))]
    print(f"couple moment {Ms[0]} N m about 20 random points: spread {np.ptp(np.array(Ms), axis=0).max():.1e}")
    assert all(np.allclose(m, Ms[0]) for m in Ms) and abs(Ms[0][2] - 50 * 0.4) < 1e-12
    # 3D: a 200 N pull on a valve wheel rim, moment about the stem axis
    Fv, at = np.array([0.0, 200.0, -50.0]), np.array([0.15, 0.0, 0.3])
    Mo = moment(Fv, at)
    axis = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 1.0])
    print(f"valve: moment about the origin {np.round(Mo, 3)} N m; about the stem (z) axis {Mo @ axis:.3f} N m")
    assert abs(Mo @ axis - 0.15 * 200) < 1e-12
```

:::exercise Pedalling torque
A rider pushes straight down with 600 N on a 170 mm crank. What torque reaches the chainring when the crank is horizontal, and when it is 30° above horizontal?
---solution---
1. Horizontal crank: the force is perpendicular to the crank, $M = 600\cdot0.17 = 102$ N m.
2. At 30° above horizontal, the lever arm is the horizontal distance $0.17\cos30° = 0.1472$ m, so $M = 88.3$ N m.
3. At top dead centre the lever arm is zero: pushing down does nothing, which is why riders "pedal in circles".
:::
