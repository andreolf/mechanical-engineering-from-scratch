Friction is why ladders stand, bolts stay tight and crates stay put on a truck ramp, until they don't. The model engineers use for dry contact is two centuries old and fits on one line. It's an approximation, but used carefully it predicts whether something will slide, tip or hold.

## Coulomb friction

At a dry contact with normal force $N$, the friction force $F$ adjusts itself to whatever equilibrium needs, up to a limit:

$$
|F| \le \mu_s N \quad\text{(sticking)}, \qquad |F| = \mu_k N \quad\text{(sliding)} .
$$

$\mu_s$ (static) is usually a little larger than $\mu_k$ (kinetic). Typical values: rubber on dry asphalt 0.7 to 0.9, steel on steel 0.5 to 0.8 dry and around 0.1 lubricated, wood on wood 0.3 to 0.5. They are measured, not derived, and vary a lot.

Two things to remember:

- Friction is **not** automatically $\mu N$. It is $\mu N$ only at the point of slipping. Otherwise it is whatever the FBD needs.
- The friction force is independent of the contact area (to a first approximation).

## Slide or tip

A crate of width $b$ and height $h$ on a ramp at angle $\theta$:

- **Slides** when the friction needed, $W\sin\theta$, exceeds $\mu W\cos\theta$, i.e. when $\tan\theta > \mu$. The weight doesn't matter.
- **Tips** when the weight's line of action passes outside the lower edge, i.e. when $\tan\theta > b/h$.

Whichever limit is smaller happens first as the ramp tilts. A tall, narrow crate on a grippy ramp tips; a squat one on a slippery ramp slides. The same reasoning applies to pushing a crate: push high and it tips, push low and it slides.

:::example A fridge on a truck ramp
Crate 0.6 m wide, 1.2 m tall: $b/h = 0.5$, so it would tip at $\arctan0.5 = 26.57°$.

1. On a smooth steel ramp, $\mu = 0.3$: $\arctan0.3 = 16.70° < 26.57°$. It slides first, at 16.70°.
2. On a rubber mat, $\mu = 0.6$: $26.57° < \arctan0.6 = 30.96°$. It tips first, at 26.57°. Strap it.
3. Pushing it across the floor (900 N, $\mu = 0.4$): sliding needs 360 N. Tipping about the front edge needs $900\cdot0.3/h_p$, which is less than 360 N once $h_p > 0.75$ m. Push below 0.75 m.
:::

```python file=friction.py
"""Coulomb friction: will a crate on a ramp slide or tip first? And a crate pushed sideways."""
import math


def ramp_event(theta_deg, mu, b, h):
    """Crate of width b and height h (centre of mass at the middle) on a ramp at theta.
    Slides when tan(theta) > mu; tips when the weight line passes the lower edge, tan(theta) > b / h."""
    t = math.tan(math.radians(theta_deg))
    slide, tip = t > mu, t > b / h
    if slide and tip:
        return "slides" if mu < b / h else "tips"          # whichever limit was reached first while tilting
    return "slides" if slide else "tips" if tip else "stays"


def critical_angle(mu, b, h):
    return math.degrees(math.atan(min(mu, b / h))), ("slide" if mu < b / h else "tip")


def push(W, mu, b, h_push):
    """Horizontal push at height h_push on a crate of width b resting on the floor.
    Force to slide: mu W. Force to tip about the front edge: W (b/2) / h_push. The smaller wins."""
    P_slide, P_tip = mu * W, W * b / 2 / h_push
    return (P_slide, "slides") if P_slide < P_tip else (P_tip, "tips")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # a 0.6 m wide, 1.2 m tall fridge crate on a tilting truck ramp
    b, h = 0.6, 1.2
    for mu in (0.3, 0.6):
        ang, mode = critical_angle(mu, b, h)
        print(f"mu = {mu}: first event at {ang:.3f} deg, it will {mode}")
    assert critical_angle(0.3, b, h) == (math.degrees(math.atan(0.3)), "slide")
    assert critical_angle(0.6, b, h)[1] == "tip"
    # slides exactly when tan(theta) > mu (if tipping isn't first)
    mu = 0.3; th = math.degrees(math.atan(mu))
    assert ramp_event(th - 1e-6, mu, b, h) == "stays" and ramp_event(th + 1e-6, mu, b, h) == "slides"
    # pushing the crate: W = 900 N, mu = 0.4; low push slides, high push tips
    for hp in (0.3, 0.9):
        P, what = push(900, 0.4, b, hp)
        print(f"push at {hp} m: {P:.1f} N and it {what}")
    hp_crit = b / 2 / 0.4
    print(f"push higher than {hp_crit:.2f} m and it tips before it slides")
    assert push(900, 0.4, b, hp_crit - 0.01)[1] == "slides" and push(900, 0.4, b, hp_crit + 0.01)[1] == "tips"
```

:::exercise A wedge under a door
A door pushes horizontally on a thin wooden wedge with 40 N. The wedge angle is 8° and $\mu = 0.35$ on both faces. Does the wedge hold? Ignore the wedge's weight.
---solution---
1. Use the friction angle $\varphi = \arctan\mu = \arctan0.35 = 19.29°$. At each face the total contact force can tilt up to $\varphi$ from the normal.
2. A wedge of angle $\alpha$ with friction on both faces stays put (is self-locking) when $\alpha < 2\varphi$: the two contact forces can then line up and cancel. Here $8° < 38.6°$, so it holds with a large margin.
3. The 40 N doesn't matter for whether it holds: both the push-out tendency and the friction scale with it. That's what makes a wedge self-locking.
:::
